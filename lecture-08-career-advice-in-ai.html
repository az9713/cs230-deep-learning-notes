Building a career by showing useful work

A student wants an AI job and needs a project that shows what they can do. A company wants its sales staff to spend less time researching prospective customers. Putting an agent into a demonstration could satisfy neither need: the student might be unable to explain the program, and the company might receive plausible briefs that take longer to check than to write. This chapter follows a more demanding question. What evidence would make the work useful to the company and informative about the student's skills?

The sales-research project comes from Laurence Moroney's consulting account; the student and the proposed evaluation records are original teaching constructions. The project joins the chapter's three quantitative questions. What does a growing task-completion horizon tell us about available capabilities? How much does trying several prototypes improve the chance of finding a useful approach? And what happens to iteration time when building gets faster but feedback does not? Those models help interpret a project decision. They do not estimate the probability of getting hired.

Choose a useful question before choosing a tool 0:04

Provenance. Lecture: Ng's opportunity framing and benchmark-ceiling observation, 4–86 s. Original construction: the student's sales-research project and the distinction between capability, workflow and career evidence. Research extension: none.

A project has at least three different audiences. The user needs an output they can use. The engineer needs evidence about correctness and maintainability. An interviewer needs evidence that the applicant understands the work. A polished screen answers none of these questions by itself. In the running case, a useful output might be a short account of a prospect's business, with source links, dates and a distinction between recorded facts and inferred needs. The same output becomes engineering evidence when its claims can be checked against the sources, and skill evidence when its author can explain why the retrieval and checking steps were chosen.

Consider an original example: a brief says that a prospect opened an office in a new city. A source dated two years earlier reports a plan to open it, but gives no confirmation. The system has retrieved relevant text and produced fluent language, yet changed the status of a plan into a completed event. A demonstration that scores only readability will miss that error. A demonstration that asks whether each factual claim is supported, current enough for its use and correctly qualified will expose it. The desired product has to define these distinctions before the student decides whether to use one model call, retrieval or a loop of tools.

Ng argues that the available building blocks and coding tools make this an unusually good time to build (speaker judgment, 30–339 s). His argument has two parts. Models can supply capabilities that a conventional program would need substantial specialized work to reproduce. Coding assistance can shorten the time between a specification and a prototype. The parts can vary independently: a model may add a useful capability without making the surrounding system easy to maintain, and a coding tool may accelerate an ordinary interface without improving its predictions.

His observation about benchmark ceilings separates a measurement limitation from a capability claim. If a benchmark reports the fraction of a fixed set of questions answered correctly, its score cannot exceed one. Improvement from 90% to 99% leaves only one percentage point available on that test; continued improvement will therefore have little room to appear. That arithmetic does not show that capabilities have stopped growing. It also does not establish that they are growing: the same flat score is consistent with no improvement, an insensitive test or a shift to skills the test does not measure. One needs another measurement to discriminate among those possibilities.

The project has an analogous ceiling. Once the system passes all ten questions in a small demonstration, another iteration cannot increase its score on those ten. It could still improve the handling of stale sources, ambiguous company names or unavailable pages. Adding cases that exercise those distinctions changes what is measured. Keeping a held-out collection of cases also prevents an applicant from presenting success on repeatedly edited examples as evidence of general performance. These are original evaluation choices, rather than observations about the company in Moroney's account.

The consequential question is therefore narrower than whether AI is progressing or whether an applicant has used the latest tool. Does this particular workflow make an identified task better under an explicit test? A horizon measures one aspect of task capability. A prototype tests one proposed way to use it. A feedback cycle tests whether that way meets a user's need. Keeping those three objects separate makes the quantitative argument that follows useful.

Measure a task horizon, then distinguish a fit from a forecast 1:26

Provenance. Lecture: the METR result and Ng's faster coding-horizon remark, 86–167 s; Ng slide 1. Research extension: Kwa et al., March 2025 version, PDF pp. 2, 9–10, 12, 15. Original construction: exponential model, fitted synthetic points and sensitivity diagram.

What the horizon measures

A 50% task-completion time horizon, written \(H_{50}\), is the human-time duration at which an evaluated agent has a fitted 50% chance of completing a task. Its units here are minutes of human work. It is not the agent's runtime, a promise to complete every task of that length or the length of an arbitrary job it can perform reliably.

Kwa et al. use a 170-task suite and human baselines, then fit success against human task time (March 2025 PDF, pp. 2, 9–10). Their release-date trend gives a doubling time of 212 days, with a 171–249-day bootstrap interval (p. 10). The roughly 70-day estimate concerns a separate SWE-bench Verified analysis, for which they identify a timing-estimate bias that can shorten the estimated doubling time (p. 15). These are empirical results for the evaluated tasks and agents. Ng's seven-month headline describes the broader fit; his coding remark needs the narrower benchmark qualification.

The threshold matters. The same paper reports about 59 minutes at 50% success and 15 minutes at 80% for Claude 3.7 Sonnet (p. 12, empirical result). A longer 50% horizon does not remove the gap to a higher reliability requirement. None of these estimates measures the accuracy of the sales-research project; the task distribution and criterion differ.

A constant doubling time gives a straight line on a logarithmic scale

To see what extrapolating a trend assumes, introduce calendar time \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}\), measured in months since a chosen starting date, and a positive doubling time \(t_{\mathrm{dbl}}\), also in months. Assume that the horizon multiplies by the same factor over every interval of the same length. If it doubles in \(t_{\mathrm{dbl}}\) months, it doubles twice in \(2t_{\mathrm{dbl}}\) months and multiplies by \(2^3\) in \(3t_{\mathrm{dbl}}\) months. Extending that constant exponential rate between those dates gives the model

\[ H_{50}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})=H_{50}(0)\,2^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}/t_{\mathrm{dbl}}},\qquad H_{50}(0)>0,\quad t_{\mathrm{dbl}}>0. \]

This is a growth model for the horizon after it has been measured. It is not the model that fits completion probability to individual tasks. The latter estimates each agent's horizon; the former summarizes changes in the estimated horizons across release dates. Confusing the two would hide a source of estimation error.

Take an original synthetic sequence with \(H_{50}(0)=2\) minutes and \(t_{\mathrm{dbl}}=7\) months. At calendar times \(0,3.5,7,10.5,14\), the horizons are \(2,2\sqrt2,4,4\sqrt2,8\) minutes. These numbers were generated from the stated formula, not measured from a model. A two-month future change would multiply the horizon by \(2^{2/7}\), regardless of the starting horizon, under the same constant-rate assumption. This multiplicative property is what the exponential model adds beyond saying that later agents can do longer tasks.

Define the dimensionless log-horizon \(\mathrm{logH}=\log_2(H_{50}/(1\text{ minute}))\). Dividing by one minute before taking the logarithm avoids taking the logarithm of a dimensional quantity. The model becomes a line, \(\mathrm{logH}=b_{\mathrm{hor}}+s_{\mathrm{hor}}t_{\mathrm{cal}}\), where \(b_{\mathrm{hor}}=\log_2(H_{50}(0)/(1\text{ minute}))\) and \(s_{\mathrm{hor}}=1/t_{\mathrm{dbl}}\) has units of inverse months. The synthetic sequence has intercept 1 and slope \(1/7\).

Derive the fitted slope

Suppose there are \(n_{\mathrm{obs}}\ge2\) positive observed horizons at calendar times \(t_{\mathrm{cal},i}\), indexed by \(i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{obs}}\). Write \(\mathrm{logH}_i\) for their base-two log-horizons and use bars for their arithmetic means. Fit a line by minimizing the sum of squared residuals \(\sum_i(\mathrm{logH}_i-b_{\mathrm{hor}}-s_{\mathrm{hor}}t_{\mathrm{cal},i})^2\). This teaching fit gives every point equal weight. It does not reproduce METR's complete measurement and bootstrap procedure.

Differentiating with respect to the intercept sets the sum of residuals to zero, so \(\hat b_{\mathrm{hor}}=\overline{\mathrm{logH}}-\hat s_{\mathrm{hor}}\overline{t_{\mathrm{cal}}}\). Substituting that expression makes every residual a difference of centered values. Differentiating with respect to the slope then gives

\[ \hat s_{\mathrm{hor}}=\frac{\sum_i(t_{\mathrm{cal},i}-\overline{t_{\mathrm{cal}}})(\mathrm{logH}_i-\overline{\mathrm{logH}})}{\sum_i(t_{\mathrm{cal},i}-\overline{t_{\mathrm{cal}}})^2},\qquad \hat t_{\mathrm{dbl}}=\frac1{\hat s_{\mathrm{hor}}}\quad\text{when }\hat s_{\mathrm{hor}}>0. \]

The denominator must be positive: at least two calendar times must differ. The residual sum is a strictly convex quadratic in the slope after eliminating the intercept, because that denominator is its positive curvature coefficient. Its stationary point is therefore the unique minimum. If the fitted slope is zero, the fitted horizon is constant and has no finite doubling time. A negative slope describes decay, not the positive-growth model. The program deliberately rejects either case when asked to return a growth doubling time.

For the five synthetic points, \(\overline{t_{\mathrm{cal}}}=7\) and \(\overline{\mathrm{logH}}=2\). The centered time values are \(-7,-3.5,0,3.5,7\); the centered log values are \(-1,-0.5,0,0.5,1\). The numerator is \(17.5\), the denominator \(122.5\), and their ratio is \(1/7\). The resulting intercept is \(2-7/7=1\), recovering a two-minute anchor and a seven-month doubling time exactly (engineering check). Exact recovery follows because the data were constructed on the line; it is not a realistic error estimate.

An uncertain rate creates a widening extrapolation range

A forecast uses the fitted relationship beyond the release dates used to estimate it. Holding a two-minute anchor fixed, assumed doubling times of 8, 7 and 6 months give 24-month horizons of \(16\), \(21.534\) and \(32\) minutes. The factor-of-two spread between the slow and fast forecasts arises from just one extra doubling over those two years. No uncertainty in the starting horizon was needed to produce it.

More generally, if the assumed doubling time lies in a specified positive interval \([t_{\mathrm{dbl,min}},t_{\mathrm{dbl,max}}]\), then for \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}\ge0\) the slow rate gives the lower curve and the fast rate the upper curve. Their ratio is

\[ \frac{H_{50}(0)\,2^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}/t_{\mathrm{dbl,min}}}}{H_{50}(0)\,2^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}/t_{\mathrm{dbl,max}}}} =2^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}(1/t_{\mathrm{dbl,min}}-1/t_{\mathrm{dbl,max}})}. \]

The anchor cancels. At the starting date the ratio is one; afterward it grows when the endpoints differ. This is a sensitivity calculation for stipulated rates, not a confidence interval. It accounts for neither a future change in rate nor uncertainty in external validity. The sales brief could remain difficult because checking sources and intent is difficult, even while an unrelated task horizon increases.

Using an average 30.44-day month, seven months is about 213 days, roughly three times 70 days. Extending each rate for a year gives factors \(2^{12/7}\approx3.281\) and \(2^{365/70}\approx37.124\) (engineering checks). The second factor is a consequence of sustaining the faster rate for a year, not a measured annual gain that every coding task or career will receive. It illustrates why the domain, period and future-rate assumption must accompany a doubling-time headline.

Twenty attempts help only under a model of their outcomes 2:47

Provenance. Lecture: building blocks, changing coding tools and encouragement to build, 167–339 s; Ng slide 2 supplies twenty prototypes and the speed figures. Original construction: fixed-criterion independent attempts and a shared-outcome counterexample. Research extension: none.

Ng's second slide recommends trying twenty prototypes, calls prototype building ten times faster, and places a question mark beside a 50%-faster production-software figure. Those are his illustrative judgments, not results from a supplied controlled experiment. Twenty is slide-only advice: the spoken passage urges building but does not state that count. The prototype/production distinction is consequential. A prototype can answer whether an interaction is useful before the full maintenance, deployment and failure-handling work is done.

For the student's project, a prototype might let a salesperson inspect a generated brief and correct its inferred priorities. That can test whether the format saves reading effort. It does not show that the system can access every required source, separate customers with similar names or sustain correct behaviour when a website changes. An honest demonstration identifies which of these questions it answers. Otherwise a successful interaction quietly turns into a claim about a finished service.

Let \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\) be a nonnegative integer count of attempted prototypes and \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\in[0,1]\) their common probability of passing a fixed usefulness criterion. An attempt means one complete proposed approach judged against that criterion, not one generated line of code. The probability model samples outcomes over hypothetical repetitions of the whole portfolio. It assumes the criterion and the relevant user population are fixed; it does not derive \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\) from a project title or a model benchmark.

Proposition 1 (independent prototype attempts). If the success events of all \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\) attempts are mutually independent and each has probability \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\), then the chance of at least one success is \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\). For zero attempts it is zero, including when \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=1\).

Proof. At least one success fails to occur exactly when every attempt fails. Each failure has probability \(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}}\); mutual independence makes their intersection's probability the product of these probabilities. Taking the complement gives the expression for a positive attempt count. With no attempts the union of success events is empty, so its probability is zero. The usual empty-product convention gives the same answer without assigning a substantive meaning to \(0^0\) in the edge case. ∎

With a stipulated \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=0.1\), twenty independent attempts give \(1-0.9^{20}\approx0.8784\). One attempt gives 0.1; the next adds 0.09; the twentieth adds only \(0.1\times0.9^{19}\approx0.0135\). The gain decreases because a late success matters to the at-least-one criterion only if every earlier attempt failed. This is a statement about that criterion, not a reason to stop after a prescribed count. A later prototype can teach something even after an earlier one has passed.

Independence can fail in precisely the way that matters to the student. Suppose every proposed design depends on the same inaccessible customer data. In a stipulated shared-outcome construction, draw one event with probability 0.1 and make all twenty designs succeed when it occurs and all fail otherwise. Each design still has marginal success probability 0.1. The portfolio's chance of any success is now 0.1, rather than 0.8784. Making more surface variations did not create twenty independent opportunities.

The example does not establish that real prototypes are perfectly correlated, nor that all positive dependence has the same effect. It establishes that marginal probabilities alone do not justify the independent formula, continuing the distinction introduced in chapter 1. Changing a data source, a task boundary or a verification method can remove a shared failure mode; merely changing the wording of an otherwise identical design may not. Adaptively choosing later prototypes after observing failures also changes their conditional probabilities. In that setting, the equal independent-attempt model is an explanation to test, not a portfolio guarantee.

A successful attempt must also be identifiable. If the student picks the prettiest result from twenty demonstrations without checking factual support, the selected item may simply be the one whose errors were least visible. The probability derived above assumes an actual pass criterion, not an optimistic selection rule. In the running case, an informative record would retain the source evidence, the user's correction and the reason each approach passed or failed. It lets an interviewer inspect learning across attempts even when none yet supports a production claim.

Faster building changes the limit of the feedback loop 5:39

Provenance. Lecture: Ng's build/user-feedback loop, product-management bottleneck and team-ratio observations, 339–554 s; Ng slide 3. Research extension: Amdahl's unaccelerated-work argument, 1967 article in the 2007 reprint, pp. 19–20. Original construction: serial cycle model, bound and numerical example.

Product management here means deciding what to build from an understanding of users and their feedback. Ng describes a loop: implement a version, show it to users, interpret their response and revise the next specification. He observes that coding assistance accelerates the first part more than the second and therefore shifts the bottleneck toward deciding what to build (speaker judgment, 339–469 s). His engineer-to-product-manager ratios, including movement toward one-to-one, are observations from his experience, not a surveyed industry rate.

For the student, feedback might reveal that salespeople need a brief organized by questions they can ask, while the prototype organizes it by retrieved page. The retrieval code can be correct and the product choice wrong. Rewriting the page faster helps only after the student has learned which distinction matters to the user. Asking a model to invent that feedback is a different experiment from observing a person trying to use the brief.

Define \(t_{\mathrm{build}}\ge0\) as the days needed to build one version and \(t_{\mathrm{fb}}>0\) as the days needed to obtain and act on feedback for that version. Assume these stages are sequential, one complete cycle is needed before the next version begins, and coding assistance changes neither the feedback time nor the quality of what reaches the user. Let \(s_{\mathrm{code}}\ge1\) be the factor by which it divides build time. The old cycle takes \(t_{\mathrm{build}}+t_{\mathrm{fb}}\) days; the new one takes \(t_{\mathrm{build}}/s_{\mathrm{code}}+t_{\mathrm{fb}}\).

Proposition 2 (the fixed-feedback limit). Under that serial-cycle model, the speedup is \(\mathrm{Speed}_{\mathrm{cycle}}=(t_{\mathrm{build}}+t_{\mathrm{fb}})/(t_{\mathrm{build}}/s_{\mathrm{code}}+t_{\mathrm{fb}})\). It lies between one and \((t_{\mathrm{build}}+t_{\mathrm{fb}})/t_{\mathrm{fb}}\). If build time is positive, the upper value is approached as the coding factor tends to infinity.

Proof. Speedup is the ratio of the original time to the new time for the same complete cycle. Because \(s_{\mathrm{code}}\ge1\), the new denominator is at most the original total and at least \(t_{\mathrm{fb}}\); both times are positive. Dividing the original total by these two bounds gives the stated interval. As \(s_{\mathrm{code}}\) increases without bound, \(t_{\mathrm{build}}/s_{\mathrm{code}}\) tends to zero and the denominator tends to \(t_{\mathrm{fb}}\). If build time is already zero, both times equal the feedback time and the speedup is one. ∎

Take an original project schedule of eight build days and two feedback days. A tenfold coding factor changes the cycle from ten days to \(0.8+2=2.8\) days: a 3.5714-fold cycle speedup, with a fivefold limiting value. After the change, feedback accounts for \(2/2.8\approx71.4\%\) of the cycle. It has become the larger time component without getting slower. A claim of tenfold faster building is therefore consistent with substantially less than tenfold faster iteration.

Amdahl's original argument concerns computer execution: accelerating a part cannot remove work that remains sequential. The available 2007 reprint identifies the 1967 source and discusses this limit on pp. 19–20. Applying that structure to a build/feedback cycle is this chapter's extension, not a claim that Amdahl studied product managers. The mechanism is the unchanged term in a sum. It transfers to the project only if the two-stage schedule is an adequate description.

The assumptions can fail usefully or harmfully. Feedback can overlap with implementation, producing a pipeline rather than the serial sum. Faster code generation can create more defects for users to report, increasing \(t_{\mathrm{fb}}\). Better instrumentation can make feedback faster, removing the fixed-time premise. Suppose, for example, that an accelerated version takes 0.8 build days but now needs five days to diagnose: its total is 5.8 days, rather than the predicted 2.8. The tenfold build reduction still occurred, while the forecast for the complete cycle failed because feedback time changed.

Ng recommends that engineers learn to talk with users and shape the product, while acknowledging that he previously made engineers feel bad for not being product managers and regretted it (speaker judgment, 469–554 s). The model does not prove that every engineer should combine these roles. It shows where time is going under a specified workflow. For the student, a useful response is to document what user feedback changed in the brief, rather than claim a universal hiring advantage from being both engineer and product manager.

Choose people and projects without turning an anecdote into a law 9:14

Provenance. Lecture: Ng's peer/community advice, company-team anecdote and qualified hard-work recommendation, 554–1122 s. Research extension: Christakis/Fowler, 2008, journal p. 2249; Shalizi/Thomas, local PDF pp. 1–3. Original construction: a project-team comparison and a causal counterexample in words.

Ng places the people one works with above the prestige of a company name (speaker judgment, 554–910 s). His account concerns a student who accepted an offer from a company with a prominent AI brand without knowing the eventual team. The student hoped for AI work but was assigned a backend payments project and left after a frustrated year. Ng recounts a second mismatch later. The stories identify a question to ask about an offer: what work and colleagues are actually being offered? They do not estimate how often such mismatches occur.

The difference is between an organization's reputation and a person's daily learning environment. In the running case, one team might provide access to salespeople who review the student's briefs and an engineer who checks the source trail. Another might have a more famous name but provide neither user access nor someone able to inspect the implementation. Those specified differences affect what the project can teach. The name alone does not tell the student which environment they will receive. This comparison is an original example, not a ranking of employers.

Ng uses smoking patterns to motivate the importance of peers, then says that he knows of no study establishing the analogous claim for hardworking friends (speaker judgment, 554–612 s). Christakis and Fowler report clustered smoking behaviour in a longitudinal social network (2008, journal p. 2249, empirical observation). That observation concerns smoking in the studied population. It is not evidence for a quantified effect of a career peer group.

Even within a social network, similar behaviour can have different causes. Homophily means that people form ties with people who share traits. Social influence means that one person's behaviour changes another's. Shalizi and Thomas explain why these mechanisms can be confounded in observational network data without strong assumptions or adequate knowledge of the relevant traits (local PDF pp. 1–3). Suppose two students independently enjoy building software and meet in a project club. They can later have similar work habits even if neither changed the other's habits. A common inclination both selected the friendship and produced the similarity. That counterexample is enough to show why observing similar friends does not by itself identify influence.

It would also be a mistake to conclude that peers cannot influence learning. An observational ambiguity rules out a particular inference; it does not rule out the mechanism. The student's project can expose concrete exchanges: a teammate finds an unsupported claim, demonstrates a better source check and helps the student explain it. That is evidence of an interaction and a correction in this project. It still does not turn a handful of such exchanges into a population-level estimate of career success.

Ng's account of Stanford's connections to researchers is likewise his description of the community, not a measured comparison with every university (speaker judgment, 612–749 s). Its relevant mechanism is access to specific information and feedback that changes a technical choice. For example, someone who has tested a retriever can warn that it confuses companies with similar names. The warning matters if it causes the student to add a disambiguation step and test it. A contact count without such a link does not show what was learned.

His closing recommendation encourages sustained effort by people who are in a position to give it, and explicitly acknowledges periods and circumstances in which they are not (speaker judgment, 990–1122 s). His examples come from his experience with students, not a controlled estimate of hours and outcomes. Moroney later emphasizes output rather than time spent. Neither speaker's experience establishes a universal relation between hours worked and useful output. For the running project, an inspectable improvement and an account of how it was obtained communicate more than an unsupported claim about how hard the applicant worked.

Interview evidence and a speaker's reading of the job market 19:10

Provenance. Lecture: Moroney's interview-coaching anecdote and hiring narrative, 1150–1752 s; guest slides 1–3. Original construction: what an applicant's project can reveal. Research extension: none; this is not a current labour-market survey.

Moroney begins from the other side of Ng's team-selection advice: an employer is also choosing someone to work with (speaker judgment, 1150–1420 s). His example is a technically strong applicant who had tracked more than 300 prospective jobs and repeatedly failed late interview rounds. In mock interviews, Moroney says that the applicant interpreted advice to defend an answer as a demand to become combative when challenged. After changing how he handled disagreement, the applicant obtained a job and, in Moroney's account, doubled his previous salary. The story has no supplied interview records, comparison group or complete accounting of the other changes between applications.

The mechanism the story proposes is understandable without accepting a causal estimate. A reviewer who gives a counterexample to a program is testing more than whether the applicant can produce an answer. They can observe whether the applicant identifies the violated assumption, corrects the implementation and explains the consequence. In the sales-research project, suppose an interviewer points out that the brief merged two companies sharing a name. Insisting that retrieval returned relevant pages does not answer that objection. Explaining the missing identity check and showing how it will be tested does.

Changing one's response to evidence differs from giving up a correct technical position. A disputed requirement, a flawed test and a real program defect call for different responses. An applicant can ask which company is intended, explain that the existing test permits both and propose an identity field. If the test itself is wrong, the applicant can demonstrate the discrepancy. The useful evidence is the handling of a particular objection, not agreement with every challenge. This is an original interpretation of the story rather than a general interview-success prescription.

Nor does tracking 300 jobs provide a denominator for an interview success rate. The record, as described, combines applications at different stages and possibly jobs never reached. A calculation would need a defined set of applications, a stated outcome for each and treatment of incomplete processes. A late successful application also does not show what would have happened without the coaching. There may have been changes in role, employer, timing or preparation. The anecdote can motivate an applicant to inspect how they discuss work; it cannot supply odds for an applicant with a different background.

Moroney's market narrative describes a transition from rapid hiring toward more selective hiring and production-focused work (speaker judgment, 1420–1752 s). He connects it to pandemic-era changes, subsequent demand for AI talent, overhiring and later adjustments. The guest deck's second and third pages summarize perceived scarcity, competition and a need to understand what employers seek. These are his historical framing at the November 2025 talk. The chapter has no dataset that identifies hiring rates, distinguishes AI from other roles or certifies the proposed causal sequence.

A reader should therefore separate the signal the narrative asks them to investigate from the narrative's unverified generality. A specific role description may ask for reliable deployment and incident diagnosis. Another may ask for model research. The student's source-checked brief is evidence relevant to the first if it demonstrates deployment constraints and evaluation; it may be insufficient for the second without evidence about training or research. Calling the whole market production-focused would erase that difference. The project has to explain its relevance to the role actually being considered.

The timing of the talk also limits its use. Ng mentions a Gemini 3 release that morning. Google's dated announcement places that release on 2025-11-18, corroborating the recording date (product announcement). It does not establish that Ng's tool preferences or Moroney's hiring impressions remain current. The chapter records their arguments as historical advice, while the student's inspectable work provides evidence that can be assessed directly.

Depth, business focus and delivery need different evidence 29:13

Provenance. Lecture: Moroney's three pillars, productivity framing and portfolio/interview story, 1753–2177 s; guest slide 4. Original construction: three kinds of evidence for the sales-research project. Research extension: none.

Moroney names three pillars: understanding in depth, business focus and a bias toward delivery (speaker judgment, 1753–2177 s). Depth includes understanding the academic ideas and understanding what is happening in practice. Business focus connects the output to the work a user needs done. Delivery turns an idea into something one can inspect and use. The terms name different requirements. Knowing how a model works does not establish that anyone needs this application; user demand does not establish that its author understands the implementation.

Depth is an explanation that survives a changed example

In the running case, depth means more than naming retrieval-augmented generation. The applicant can identify what documents enter the system, how a query selects them, what happens when the relevant document is missing and why a generated claim still needs support. Asked why the brief confuses two firms, they can locate the identity assumption instead of changing a prompt until this one example looks better. Their explanation should predict what a corresponding test will reveal.

For example, the applicant might show that the company name is the only retrieval key. Adding a city or domain narrows the intended entity, but only if that additional field is itself correct. A test with two namesakes and an incomplete request then exercises both the retrieval behaviour and the need to ask for clarification. This does not require claiming that the model internally understands corporate identity. It requires a precise account of the information available to the system and the decision made from it.

Academic depth and practical depth can reinforce each other without becoming identical. The probability calculation for repeated prototypes explains why independent attempts have a different portfolio outcome from shared failures. A project log can show whether the student's failed attempts really changed their data dependency. Neither substitutes for the other. The equation cannot certify that designs were independent; the log cannot justify the product formula if no independence argument is given.

Business focus begins with the quantity the user wants changed

Moroney's output framing is explicit: he distinguishes time spent from what that time produced (speaker judgment, 1889–2017 s). He rejects a long-hours schedule as a sufficient measure of hard work and uses his own writing habits as an anecdote. Ng's earlier encouragement emphasizes effort and time when one's circumstances permit it. Their statements need not be forced into a single measure. Both are advice from experience, and neither defines a validated productivity score.

The student's project makes the distinction concrete. Twenty generated briefs are output, but they may leave the sales staff more work if every factual sentence needs repair. The business quantity might instead be time to prepare an accurate, useful meeting brief, including verification. If the manual version takes 30 minutes and the generated version takes five minutes to produce plus 35 minutes to check, the initial generation speed does not make the complete task faster. Those are original illustrative times; the arithmetic exposes why a measurement boundary matters.

The boundary can also change which work should be attempted. If factual verification dominates, the student could improve source attribution rather than make a more attractive interface. If user feedback reveals that the wrong prospects are selected, better brief generation may not address the limiting task. Moroney illustrates debt with borrowing 500,000 dollars over 30 years and paying roughly twice that amount in total (speaker example, 2956–3010 s). None of those assumptions is supplied as a recommendation for an actual loan, and the calculation says nothing about future property value.

Let \(P_{\mathrm{loan}}\) be the principal in dollars, \(r_{\mathrm{month}}\) the monthly interest fraction and \(\mathrm{Pay}\) the fixed monthly payment in dollars. A balance after one month is the previous balance multiplied by \(1+r_{\mathrm{month}}\), minus \(\mathrm{Pay}\). Repeating for \(n_{\mathrm{payment}}\) payments gives the initial principal multiplied by \((1+r_{\mathrm{month}})^{n_{\mathrm{payment}}}\), minus the payment multiplied by the geometric sum \(1+(1+r_{\mathrm{month}})+\cdots+(1+r_{\mathrm{month}})^{n_{\mathrm{payment}}-1}\). Setting the final balance to zero and summing that series yields

\[ \mathrm{Pay}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{loan}}r_{\mathrm{month}}}{1-(1+r_{\mathrm{month}})^{-n_{\mathrm{payment}}}},\qquad r_{\mathrm{month}}>0. \]

At zero interest, the monthly payment is \(P_{\mathrm{loan}}/n_{\mathrm{payment}}\); that is also the limit because the denominator is approximately \(n_{\mathrm{payment}}r_{\mathrm{month}}\) for a small monthly rate. With \(P_{\mathrm{loan}}=500{,}000\), \(r_{\mathrm{month}}=0.053/12\) and \(n_{\mathrm{payment}}=360\), the payment is approximately 2,776.52 dollars and total payments are 1.9991 times the principal. A nominal annual rate near 5.304% gives an exactly doubled total in this stipulated calculation (engineering checks from the laboratory).

The check makes the speaker's approximate total arithmetically possible. It does not show that the purchase is valuable, that a particular loan has these terms or that code carries a corresponding rate. Applying the mortgage formula to technical debt would invent a quantitative law that the metaphor does not supply.

import math


def horizon_fit(points):
    """OLS on log2(horizon_minutes) versus calendar_months."""
    if len(points) < 2:
        raise ValueError("at least two observations are required")
    if any(not math.isfinite(t) or not math.isfinite(h) or h <= 0
           for t, h in points):
        raise ValueError("finite times and positive horizons are required")
    times = [t for t, h in points]
    logs = [math.log2(h) for t, h in points]
    mean_t = sum(times) / len(times)
    mean_log = sum(logs) / len(logs)
    denominator = sum((t - mean_t) ** 2 for t in times)
    if denominator == 0:
        raise ValueError("release times must not all coincide")
    slope = sum((t - mean_t) * (z - mean_log)
                for t, z in zip(times, logs)) / denominator
    if slope <= 0:
        raise ValueError("this growth illustration requires positive slope")
    intercept = mean_log - slope * mean_t
    return 2 ** intercept, 1 / slope

    if any(not math.isfinite(v) for v in (h0, months, doubling_months)):
        raise ValueError("finite values are required")
    if h0 <= 0 or doubling_months <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive anchor and doubling time are required")
    return h0 * 2 ** (months / doubling_months)


def any_success(p_succ, n_try):
    if not math.isfinite(p_succ) or not 0 <= p_succ <= 1:
        raise ValueError("probability must lie in [0, 1]")
    if type(n_try) is not int or n_try < 0:
        raise ValueError("attempt count must be a nonnegative integer")
    if n_try == 0 or p_succ == 0:
        return 0.0
    if p_succ == 1:
        return 1.0
    return -math.expm1(n_try * math.log1p(-p_succ))


def cycle_speedup(t_build, t_fb, coding_factor):
    if any(not math.isfinite(v)
           for v in (t_build, t_fb, coding_factor)):
        raise ValueError("finite times and factor are required")
    if t_build < 0 or t_fb <= 0 or coding_factor < 1:
        raise ValueError("build >= 0, feedback > 0, factor >= 1 required")
    speed = (t_build + t_fb) / (t_build / coding_factor + t_fb)
    bound = (t_build + t_fb) / t_fb
    return speed, bound


def payment(principal, annual_rate, n_payment):
    """Level monthly payment; illustrative nominal rate, no fees."""
    if not math.isfinite(principal) or principal <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive finite principal is required")
    if not math.isfinite(annual_rate) or annual_rate < 0:
        raise ValueError("nonnegative finite rate is required")
    if type(n_payment) is not int or n_payment <= 0:
        raise ValueError("positive integer payment count is required")
    monthly_rate = annual_rate / 12
    if monthly_rate == 0:
        return principal / n_payment
    discount = -math.expm1(-n_payment * math.log1p(monthly_rate))
    return principal * monthly_rate / discount


def sales_time(research_share, saved_research_fraction):
    if any(not math.isfinite(v)
           for v in (research_share, saved_research_fraction)):
        raise ValueError("finite fractions are required")
    if not 0 <= research_share < 1 or not 0 <= saved_research_fraction <= 1:
        raise ValueError("research < 1 and saved fraction in [0, 1] required")
    old_selling = 1 - research_share
    new_selling = old_selling + research_share * saved_research_fraction
    return new_selling, new_selling / old_selling - 1

Keep the inference as narrow as the evidence

The three models answer three specified questions. Exponential growth describes a horizon when a constant multiplicative rate is assumed. The portfolio formula describes mutually independent, fixed-criterion attempts. The cycle bound describes sequential build and feedback stages when only building changes. Each has an explicit failure boundary: future rate changes, shared causes or changed feedback can respectively break its interpretation. None is a quantitative theory of career success.

The loan and sales calculations are arithmetic checks on possible interpretations of a story. They establish that a stipulated payment schedule can approximately double a principal and that a stipulated reallocation can increase selling time. They do not validate the technical-debt metaphor as an interest law or turn time saved into sales caused. Making these distinctions permits the chapter to use numbers without assigning them evidential work they cannot perform.

The lecture's broader advice is to build, seek useful feedback, develop understanding and retain the ability to explain one's work. The original project record can show source checking, correction and a justified design change. It can still fail to improve the chosen workflow. Reporting that result accurately is evidence of reasoning; hiding it behind an impressive demonstration removes the information an interviewer or user needs.

Empirical claims remain attached to their source versions and scope. Market narratives, product demonstrations and employment stories remain historical speaker accounts where independent records are absent. Access anecdotes preserve unknown outcomes. Those boundaries do not answer every career question, but they state exactly what a student can learn from this lecture and what their own project would still need to establish.

Five exercises with worked solutions

This is a low-math lecture. The exercises develop its three justified models and two arithmetic checks; no theorem is invented for career advice, technical debt or a market forecast. All numerical inputs below are original illustrations.

Exercise 1: fit a horizon and identify the extrapolation

At calendar months 0, 7 and 14, synthetic 50% horizons are 2, 4 and 8 minutes. Fit the dimensionless base-two log line, state the doubling time, and compute a 24-month forecast range if the doubling time is instead stipulated to lie between six and eight months. Does the result imply an 80% horizon of the same size?

Solution

The log-horizons are 1, 2 and 3, with means 2 for the log and 7 for time. Centering gives time values −7, 0, 7 and log values −1, 0, 1. The least-squares numerator is 14 and denominator 98, so the slope is \(1/7\) inverse months. The intercept is \(2-(1/7)7=1\), giving a two-minute anchor and seven-month doubling time. At month 24, the slow eight-month rate gives \(2\,2^{24/8}=16\) minutes; the fast six-month rate gives \(2\,2^{24/6}=32\). The interval is stipulated-rate sensitivity, not a confidence interval or observed future outcome.

The horizons use a 50% completion threshold. Raising the threshold changes the fitted horizon and generally shortens it for a success curve decreasing with task time. No 80% horizon follows from these three observations alone. The distinct 50% and 80% horizons in METR's example illustrate that threshold dependence.

Exercise 2: count opportunities without assuming independence

Each prototype has success probability 0.1 against the same criterion. Calculate the chance of at least one success in twenty mutually independent attempts and the minimum independent count giving at least 95%. Then construct a dependent portfolio with the same marginal probabilities but only a 10% chance of any success.

Solution

For twenty independent attempts the probability is \(1-0.9^{20}\approx0.8784\). To reach 95%, require \(0.9^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\le0.05\). Both logarithms are negative, so division reverses the inequality and gives \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\ge\log(0.05)/\log(0.9)\approx28.433\). The smallest integer is 29; 28 remains below 95% and 29 exceeds it.

Draw one Bernoulli event with success probability 0.1 and let all twenty outcomes equal it. Each attempt then has the required marginal probability, but their union is that one event, of probability 0.1. This counterexample proves that knowing every marginal probability is insufficient to identify the portfolio probability. It does not estimate dependence among real designs. A zero-attempt portfolio has probability zero under either model.

Exercise 3: locate the feedback limit

A version takes eight days to build and two to obtain and act on feedback. Coding assistance divides building by ten without changing feedback. Find the cycle speedup and its limiting value. Can increasing only the finite coding factor give a fivefold speedup? What happens if a separate intervention also reduces feedback to one day?

Solution

The original cycle is ten days; the assisted cycle is \(8/10+2=2.8\). Speedup is \(10/2.8=25/7\approx3.5714\). As the coding factor grows without bound, positive build time tends to zero and the unchanged feedback leaves a two-day cycle. The speedup tends to five. Every finite factor leaves a positive build term, so the speedup is strictly less than five in this example.

With the tenfold coding factor and a separately reduced one-day feedback stage, the cycle is \(0.8+1=1.8\) days. Comparing it with the original ten-day baseline gives \(10/1.8\approx5.5556\). This exceeds five because the original bound assumed unchanged feedback; its hypothesis no longer holds. The new intervention also needs evidence that feedback remains adequate, rather than merely being shorter.

Exercise 4: check the debt illustration's arithmetic

A stipulated 500,000-dollar principal is repaid in 360 equal monthly payments at a nominal annual rate of 5.3%, with monthly interest and no fees. Compute the payment and total-to-principal ratio. Explain the zero-interest case and what the result says about software maintenance.

Solution

The monthly fraction is \(r_{\mathrm{month}}=0.053/12\). Setting the final balance of the geometric payment recurrence to zero gives the displayed payment formula. It returns approximately 2,776.52 dollars per month, so \(360\,\mathrm{Pay}/500{,}000\approx1.9991\). The ratio approximately supports the speaker's doubled-total illustration under these particular assumptions. It does not specify anyone's actual borrowing terms.

At zero interest each payment directly reduces principal, so payment is \(500{,}000/360\). This is also the positive-rate formula's limit. Software maintenance has no corresponding stipulated payment recurrence, fixed interest fraction or mandatory amortization schedule. The calculation therefore establishes no maintenance-cost law and no evaluation of whether a shortcut is worth taking.

Exercise 5: preserve the denominator in a sales claim

A 40-hour week contains 32 hours of research and eight of selling. Under complete reallocation, compare saving 10–15% of all research time with saving 10–15 percentage points of the entire week. State what additional evidence would be required to infer an increase in sales.

Solution

The first interpretation frees 3.2–4.8 hours, producing 11.2–12.8 selling hours. These are 28–32% of the week and 40–60% relative increases from eight hours. The second interpretation frees four to six hours, giving 12–14 selling hours: 30–35% of the week and 50–75% relative increases. The two results differ because the saved fractions have different denominators. Neither reconstruction resolves what the speaker's phrase wasted time actually measured.

Both calculations assume every freed hour becomes selling and no new checking work appears. To infer an increase in sales, one must measure an explicitly defined sales outcome and compare relevant cases while addressing factors such as demand, lead quality and pricing. More selling time alone is not such a comparison. The original evaluation record should keep preparation time, support quality, usage and sales outcomes as separate measurements.
