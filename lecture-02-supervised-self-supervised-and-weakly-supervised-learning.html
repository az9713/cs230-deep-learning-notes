Where supervision comes from, and what the loss makes of it

A school wants a camera at the dining-hall door to confirm that the person who swipes a student card is the card's owner. The system must compare two photographs of faces it has never seen, and the school cannot label millions of face pairs for it. This chapter asks where the training signal for such a system can come from: from human labels, from the data itself, or from pairs of data that occur together in the world. It then asks how the choice of loss turns that signal into a vector representation that can classify, verify, identify and retrieve.

The chapter builds its tools in the order of the recording. It first builds the logistic neuron, its loss and the chain rule that trains a network of such neurons, and it uses them on two supervised tasks: classifying photographs as day or night, and detecting a spoken trigger word in an audio clip. It then returns to the dining hall. There a network maps each photograph to a vector, the encoding, and a distance between encodings decides. Three losses train that network: a triplet loss that needs identity labels, a contrastive loss that needs none, and a cross-modal loss that uses pairs such as an image and its caption. One small model of face photographs, for which some error rates have closed forms, tests the first two.

A neuron, a loss, and the signal that trains them 0:05

Provenance. Lecture: the plan of the lecture, the training loop of slide 5, the logistic neuron of slide 6, the change from one output to three on slide 7, one-hot and multi-hot labels (≈5–771 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the likelihood derivation of the binary cross-entropy, the two gradient derivations, the multi-hot softmax computation and the summary table of supervision sources.

A model, in the recording's words, is an architecture plus parameters (≈230–287 s). The architecture is the fixed form of a function \(f_\theta\); the parameters \(\theta\in\mathbb R^{n_\theta}\) are the numbers inside it that training sets. Training needs a set \(\mathcal D=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m\) of \(m\) examples, each an input \(x^{(i)}\) and a target \(y^{(i)}\), and a per-example loss \(\mathcal L(\hat y,y)\ge0\) that is small when the prediction \(\hat y=f_\theta(x)\) is close to the target. The cost is the average loss,

\[J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\mathcal L\bigl(f_\theta(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\bigr),\]

and gradient descent lowers it by repeating the update \(\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta)\) with a step size \(\eta>0\). Chapter 1 develops this set-up, empirical risk minimisation, and the gap between the cost on the training set and the loss expected on new data. Slide 5 circles four parts of this loop that a project can change, the input, the architecture, the output and the loss, and lists three more: the activation functions, the optimiser and the hyperparameters. The recording singles out the loss as the part that demands the most design (≈471–518 s) and gives the object detector YOLO as an example of a loss whose form looks arbitrary until one knows its reasons. Redmon et al. (2015) give those reasons: they optimise a sum of squared errors because it is easy to optimise, weight the box-coordinate errors by 5 and the confidence errors of boxes that contain no object by 0.5 because many grid cells contain no object and would otherwise dominate, and predict the square roots of box widths and heights so that a fixed error counts more in a small box than in a large one (p. 3; the full loss is eq. 3 on p. 4).

The question of this chapter is the second argument of \(\mathcal L\). In the classic set-up the target \(y\) is a human label. Later sections take it from the data itself or from a second modality. The table fixes the vocabulary; each row is developed in the section named in its last column.

The logistic neuron and the binary cross-entropy

Slide 6 turns a photograph into a vector. At the resolution slide 14 chooses for the first case, a colour image of height 64 and width 64 has three channels (red, green, blue) of 8-bit intensities; flattening them gives \(64\cdot64\cdot3=12{,}288\) numbers, and dividing each by 255 puts them in \([0,1]\). The result is the input \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\), here with \(n_x=12{,}288\). A logistic neuron with weight vector \(w\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\) and bias \(b\in\mathbb R\) computes the logit \(u=w^\top x+b\) and the prediction

\[\hat y=\sigma(u)=\frac1{1+e^{-u}}\in(0,1).\]

The sigmoid \(\sigma\) maps any real number into \((0,1)\), so \(\hat y\) can be read as the probability that the label is 1. Slide 6 shows an output of 0.73 and the decision "it's a cat" because \(0.73>0.5\); the value is illustrative, and no input produces it on the slide. The derivative of the sigmoid is \(\sigma'(u)=\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))\), which follows from \(\sigma'(u)=e^{-u}/(1+e^{-u})^2\).

The loss follows from reading \(\hat y\) as a probability. If the model asserts \(\mathbb P(y=1\mid x)=\hat y\), the probability it assigns to the observed label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is \(\hat y^{\,y}(1-\hat y)^{1-y}\). For \(m\) examples drawn independently, the likelihood is the product of these factors, and maximising it is the same as minimising the average negative logarithm of the factors. That average is \(J(\theta)\) with the per-example loss

\[\mathcal L(\hat y,y)=-y\log\hat y-(1-y)\log(1-\hat y),\]

the binary cross-entropy (BCE), measured in nats because the logarithm is natural. Slide 14 writes the same loss for the day-and-night task, and the recording calls it the logistic loss (≈1882–1919 s).

Proposition 1 (gradient at the logit). For the logistic neuron with binary cross-entropy, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u=\hat y-y\), and hence \(\nabla_w\mathcal L=(\hat y-y)\,x\) and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial b=\hat y-y\).

Proof. Write \(\mathcal L=-y\log\sigma(u)-(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(u))\). By \(\sigma'=\sigma(1-\sigma)\), \(\frac{d}{du}\log\sigma(u)=1-\sigma(u)\) and \(\frac{d}{du}\log(1-\sigma(u))=-\sigma(u)\). So \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u=-y(1-\sigma(u))+(1-y)\sigma(u)=\sigma(u)-y\). The chain rule through \(u=w^\top x+b\) gives the other two. \(\blacksquare\)

The gradient is the prediction minus the target, times the input. It is large when the model is confidently wrong and small when it is right. The factor \(\sigma'(u)\), which vanishes for large \(\lvert u\rvert\), has cancelled: the logarithm in the loss undoes the saturation of the sigmoid. This one line, "prediction minus target at the logit", recurs in every cross-entropy of the chapter.

Several classes: one-hot and multi-hot targets

The recording asks how to turn the cat detector into a detector of cats, dogs and giraffes (≈583–677 s). The answer on slide 7 is three logistic neurons that all see the whole input, with outputs 0.12 for dog, 0.73 for cat and 0.04 for giraffe, each compared with 0.5. The recording then warns that a new dataset needs new labels, and names two kinds of label vector for \(K\) classes (≈677–771 s). A one-hot vector \(y\in\{0,1\}^K\) has exactly one entry equal to 1: exactly one class is present. A multi-hot vector may have several: a photograph can contain a cat and a dog, labelled \((1,1,0)\) in the recording's order. Two output layers correspond to these two kinds of label, and the slide title "Multi-class" names the first while the slide draws the second.

The first is the softmax layer. With a logit \(u_k\) for each class \(k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\), it outputs \(\hat y_k=\mathrm{softmax}(u)_k=e^{u_k}/\sum_{j=1}^Ke^{u_j}\), a vector of \(K\) probabilities that sums to 1, and it is trained with the categorical cross-entropy \(\mathcal L=-\sum_{k=1}^Ky_k\log\hat y_k\). The second is \(K\) independent sigmoids, \(\hat y_k=\sigma(u_k)\), trained with the sum of \(K\) binary cross-entropies. Slide 7's outputs sum to 0.89, not to 1, so the slide draws the second design.

Proposition 2 (the two heads at their logits). Let \(\mathbb 1[\cdot]\) be 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. (a) For the softmax, \(\partial\hat y_k/\partial u_j=\hat y_k\bigl(\mathbb 1[k=j]-\hat y_j\bigr)\), and for any target with \(\sum_ky_k=1\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_j=\hat y_j-y_j\). (b) For \(K\) sigmoids with summed BCE, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_j=\hat y_j-y_j\) for every target in \(\{0,1\}^K\).

Proof. (a) \(\log\hat y_k=u_k-\log\sum_je^{u_j}\), so \(\partial\log\hat y_k/\partial u_j=\mathbb 1[k=j]-\hat y_j\), which gives the Jacobian after multiplying by \(\hat y_k\). Then \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_j=-\sum_ky_k(\mathbb 1[k=j]-\hat y_j)=-y_j+\hat y_j\sum_ky_k\), which is \(\hat y_j-y_j\) when the entries of \(y\) sum to 1. (b) The \(j\)-th term of the sum is the only one that depends on \(u_j\), and Proposition 1 applies to it. \(\blacksquare\)

The hypothesis \(\sum_ky_k=1\) is where the two designs part. Feed the softmax the multi-hot label \((1,1,0)\) of a photograph with a cat and a dog: the gradient is \(2\hat y_j-y_j\). For any probability vector \(\hat y\), the loss \(-\log\hat y_1-\log\hat y_2\) is at least \(-2\log\bigl((\hat y_1+\hat y_2)/2\bigr)\), because the logarithm is concave, and at least \(-2\log\tfrac12=2\log2=1.386\) nats, because \(\hat y_1+\hat y_2\le1\); equality needs \(\hat y_1=\hat y_2=\tfrac12\) and \(\hat y_3=0\). A softmax output is never exactly 0, so the loss can only approach this infimum: as \(u_3\to-\infty\) with \(u_1=u_2\), the output tends to \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\) and the loss to \(2\log2\). A softmax whose outputs sum to 1 cannot assert that both animals are present with probability near 1; it can only split its mass. Independent sigmoids can, because nothing ties their outputs together. The softmax is the right head when the classes exclude each other and a single label is the truth; the sigmoids are the right head for multi-hot labels, and they also accept one-hot labels, at the price of not enforcing exclusivity. Exercise 1 extends the computation to any multi-hot target.

Layers, the chain rule, and what the layers learn 12:51

Provenance. Lecture: the layer notation, network depth and capacity, the edge–part–face hierarchy of slide 10, feature engineering versus feature learning, encoding versus embedding (slides 9–11, ≈771–1130 s). Research extension: the convolutional deep belief network of Lee, Grosse, Ranganath and Ng. Original construction: the backpropagation derivation and its check, and the operational definition of an embedding.

A layer applies many neurons to the same input. Layer \(\ell\in\{1,\dots,L\}\) of a network with \(L\) layers has a weight matrix \(W^{[\ell]}\in\mathbb R^{n^{[\ell]}\times n^{[\ell-1]}}\) and a bias \(b^{[\ell]}\in\mathbb R^{n^{[\ell]}}\), where \(n^{[\ell]}\) is its number of units and \(n^{[0]}=n_x\), and computes

\[u^{[\ell]}=W^{[\ell]}h^{[\ell-1]}+b^{[\ell]},\qquad h^{[\ell]}=g\bigl(u^{[\ell]}\bigr),\]

with \(h^{[0]}=x\) and a nonlinearity \(g\) applied to each entry, for example the rectified linear unit \(g(u)=\max(u,0)\). The output layer of a binary classifier uses \(\hat y=\sigma(u^{[L]})\). The square-bracket superscript always indexes the layer, and the parenthesised superscript \((i)\) always indexes the example; the recording introduces the same convention with the letter \(a\) for an activation (≈771–829 s).

Backpropagation is the chain rule, organised by layer

Proposition 1 gave the derivative of the loss at the output logit. The derivative at every earlier layer follows from the chain rule, applied once per layer from the output backward. Write \(\odot\) for the entrywise product of two vectors of equal length.

Proposition 3 (backpropagation). For the layered network above with \(\hat y=\sigma(u^{[L]})\) and binary cross-entropy, \(\nabla_{u^{[L]}}\mathcal L=\hat y-y\); for \(\ell=L,\dots,2\) the first formula below holds, and for \(\ell=L,\dots,1\) the second and third:

\[\nabla_{u^{[\ell-1]}}\mathcal L=g'\bigl(u^{[\ell-1]}\bigr)\odot\Bigl(\bigl(W^{[\ell]}\bigr)^{\!\top}\nabla_{u^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L\Bigr),\qquad\nabla_{W^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L=\nabla_{u^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L\,\bigl(h^{[\ell-1]}\bigr)^{\!\top},\qquad\nabla_{b^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L=\nabla_{u^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L.\]

Proof. The first equation is Proposition 1. The loss depends on \(h^{[\ell-1]}\) only through \(u^{[\ell]}=W^{[\ell]}h^{[\ell-1]}+b^{[\ell]}\), whose Jacobian with respect to \(h^{[\ell-1]}\) is \(W^{[\ell]}\); so \(\nabla_{h^{[\ell-1]}}\mathcal L=(W^{[\ell]})^\top\nabla_{u^{[\ell]}}\mathcal L\). Entry \(j\) of \(h^{[\ell-1]}\) depends only on entry \(j\) of \(u^{[\ell-1]}\), through \(g\), which multiplies that entry of the gradient by \(g'(u^{[\ell-1]}_j)\). Finally \(\partial u^{[\ell]}_j/\partial W^{[\ell]}_{jj'}=h^{[\ell-1]}_{j'}\) and \(\partial u^{[\ell]}_j/\partial b^{[\ell]}_j=1\). \(\blacksquare\)

The recursion costs about as much as the forward pass, one matrix–vector product per layer, which is why a network with millions of parameters can be trained by gradient descent at all. For the ReLU, \(g'(u)=\mathbb 1[u>0]\) away from 0, so a unit that was inactive on an input passes no gradient back for that input. The program of the implementation section computes one entry of \(\nabla_{W^{[1]}}\mathcal L\) for a two-layer ReLU network by this recursion and compares it with a central difference of the loss; the two agree to \(10^{-7}\) (engineering check).

Depth, capacity and memorisation

The recording states that a deeper network has more capacity, meaning the flexibility to fit its data, and that a network of a billion parameters trained on a million cat and non-cat pictures would memorise those pictures instead of learning what a cat is (speaker judgment, ≈801–877 s). No measure of capacity is defined in the recording, and none is used in this chapter; the statement is a rule of thumb about matching model size to the amount and diversity of data, and the book grades it as such.

What the layers learn: encodings and embeddings

Slide 10 shows three grids of small images beside a network: oriented edges at layer 1, parts such as eyes and noses at layer 2, and whole faces at layer 3. The recording explains the progression: first-layer units see pixels and can only combine them into edges, while deeper units see combinations of edges and can respond to circles, eyes and then faces (≈877–1003 s). The slide carries no citation. Its layer-2 and layer-3 grids match the faces column of Figure 3 of Lee, Grosse, Ranganath and Ng (ICML 2009), whose convolutional deep belief network learned "edge detectors, object parts, and objects" in its first, second and third layers (p. 2; Figure 3, p. 7). That network was trained without labels, on unlabelled images of a single Caltech-101 category for the upper layers (p. 6), which is a different setting from the supervised face classifier described in the recording. The figure is an empirical result for that model; the general statement that deeper layers encode more complex features is speaker judgment, and chapter 9 develops the tools that test such statements on a given network.

The recording then fixes two words (≈1103–1130 s; slide 11). An encoding is any vector representation of an input, for example the activation vector \(h^{[\ell]}\) of a hidden layer. An embedding is an encoding in which distance carries meaning: two inputs whose encodings are close are similar in a sense that matters for a task. The book makes "carries meaning" operational. An encoding \(f_\theta\) is an embedding for a relation between inputs, such as "these two photographs show the same person", when a threshold on the distance \(\lVert f_\theta(x_1)-f_\theta(x_2)\rVert_2\) predicts the relation with error rates that the task can accept. The verification section measures those error rates. By this definition the pixel vector of a photograph is an encoding, and for faces it is a poor embedding.

The recording names one more contrast (≈1052–1102 s). In feature engineering a person designs the detectors, for instance an eye detector and a mouth detector, and combines their outputs. In feature learning the detectors are parameters, set by gradient descent on a loss, and no one tells the network that eyes matter. The rest of the chapter is about feature learning, and its question is which loss produces which features.

Specifying a supervised task: day or night 18:50

Provenance. Lecture: the day-and-night case of slides 14–15: task definition and hard cases, data collection, resolution chosen by testing people on printed images, colour, output, last activation, shallow architecture, loss, and the role of iteration speed (≈1130–2055 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the input and weight counts.

The first case is a binary classifier: given a photograph, output 0 if it was taken during the day and 1 if during the night (slide 14). The recording treats it as a warm-up and uses it to show that the decisions before training fix most of what training can achieve (≈1130–1468 s). The task must be defined before data are collected. A classifier for one camera in one park is a smaller problem than a classifier for any camera anywhere; indoor photographs, a visible clock, cloudy skies, and places where the sun does not set in summer are hard cases, and a model that must read a clock to decide is solving a different task. Slide 14 posits 10,000 images for the easy version; the recording gives no derivation of that number.

The resolution of the input is the next decision, and it is a trade-off. Too low a resolution removes information the task needs, such as the clock; too high a resolution costs computation in every training run and slows the cycle of trying an idea and seeing its result (≈1580–1612 s). The recording describes how the resolution was chosen in practice: images were printed at several resolutions and shown to friends, and below about \(64\times64\times3\) they could no longer tell day from night (speaker judgment, ≈1612–1673 s). People serve here as a proxy for the model, on the assumption that information a person cannot extract from an image is hard for a small model to extract too. The recording adds that colour matters for this task because of the blue sky, and that a person given the task in grey-scale finds it harder (speaker judgment, ≈1673–1701 s).

The cost of resolution can be counted. A \(64\times64\times3\) input has \(n_x=12{,}288\) values. The face photographs of the verification section are \(412\times412\times3\) on slide 21, that is \(509{,}232\) values, 41.4 times as many. If the first layer were fully connected with 100 units, it would hold \(100\times12{,}288=1{,}228{,}800\) weights for the day-and-night input and \(100\times509{,}232=50{,}923{,}200\) for the face input (engineering check by shown arithmetic; the width 100 is an assumption of the example). Slide 14 recommends a shallow convolutional network instead. A convolutional layer applies the same small filter at every position of the image, so its number of weights depends on the filter size and not on the image size; chapter 9 writes such a layer as a matrix. The count shows why a fully connected first layer is not used on images.

The remaining choices follow from the task. The output is one probability, so the last activation is a sigmoid and the loss is the binary cross-entropy of the previous section. The recording asks what the hardware allows and answers that the governing quantity is how many experiments per week the team can run, not the final accuracy of one run (speaker judgment, ≈1965–2010 s).

Trigger words: a label for every time step 34:15

Provenance. Lecture: the cascade of models in a voice assistant, the task "detect the word activate in a 10-second clip", data distribution, the labelling experiment with a foreign word, per-step sigmoid outputs and loss, the skew of the labels, the synthesis of labelled clips from three databases, the test set, architecture search and expert advice (slides 16–20, ≈2055–3311 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the per-step loss, Proposition 4, the baseline numbers for slide 17 and the count of synthetic clips.

A voice assistant in a kitchen does not run one large model on all the sound it hears. The recording describes a cascade (≈2084–2157 s): a small model that detects whether anyone is speaking at all, a second model that listens only for the trigger word, and a larger model that interprets the request once the trigger word has been heard. This book uses the standard name voice activity detection (VAD) for the first stage; the recording describes it as a detector of volume and does not use the name. The design rests on energy: the cheap stage runs all the time and the expensive stage rarely (speaker judgment about deployed assistants). This case concerns the second stage. Chapter 6 returns to it with a different trigger phrase and a class-imbalance analysis.

The task on slide 16 is: given a 10-second audio clip \(x\), detect whether the word "activate" occurs in it. The recording spends most of its time on the data (≈2213–2461 s). Recordings of people saying the word are needed, and so are recordings of other words, including words that sound similar such as "deactivate", so that the model learns the difference. The people recorded must resemble the users: the recording's first version of this model failed for the lecturer's German roommates until their speech was added (speaker judgment, ≈2328–2359 s), and age, speaking rate, voice pitch and background noise all change the signal a model receives. A model trained on a quiet campus may fail next to a subway (speaker judgment, ≈2429–2461 s). Chapter 3 states this concern as a difference between the training and deployment distributions of \(x\).

Two labelling schemes, and an experiment with people

The output can be labelled in two ways (slide 18). Scheme 1 gives one bit per clip: \(y=1\) if the word occurs anywhere, else 0. Scheme 2 gives one bit per output time step: the network emits a prediction at each of \(n_{\mathrm{seq}}\) steps, and the label \(y=(y_1,\dots,y_{n_{\mathrm{seq}}})\in\{0,1\}^{n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\) has 1 at a step that marks the word and 0 elsewhere. Where exactly the 1s sit relative to the spoken word, at its start, at its end or after a delay, is a design choice that the recording leaves open (≈2922–2953 s). The recording tests the two on the audience (≈2522–2710 s; slide 17). Three clips in a language the lecturer does not name at first (it is Italian, ≈2585–2676 s) are played; the audience is told only that the first and third contain the target word and must find it. Almost no one does. The clips are played again with scheme-2 labels, which mark where the word is, and the word is found. The recording's conclusion is that scheme 1 would need on the order of a thousand times more data, while scheme 2 is not a thousand times harder to label, so scheme 2 is the better choice (speaker judgment, ≈2710–2741 s; the factor is an estimate, not a measurement).

The mechanism behind the experiment can be stated without the factor. Under scheme 1 a positive label says that the word occurs somewhere among \(n_{\mathrm{seq}}\) positions; the learner must find the acoustic pattern that all positive clips share and all negative clips lack, and each label says nothing about where to look. Under scheme 2 each positive step is paired with the audio around it. The recording adds that a per-step scheme can be used to start training and a cheaper scheme afterwards, to avoid a cold start in which a model given only clip-level labels learns nothing at first (speaker judgment, ≈2780–2830 s).

The per-step loss and the constant predictor

With scheme 2, the network ends in a sigmoid at every step, \(\hat y_t=\sigma(u_t)\) for \(t=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{seq}}\), and the loss of a clip is the binary cross-entropy averaged over steps (slide 18),

\[\mathcal L(\hat y,y)=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\Bigl[-y_t\log\hat y_t-(1-y_t)\log(1-\hat y_t)\Bigr],\]

so that, by Proposition 1 applied at each step, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_t=(\hat y_t-y_t)/n_{\mathrm{seq}}\). Here \(t\) indexes output steps of one clip. The recording warns that with one positive step per clip a model can be very accurate by predicting 0 everywhere, and quotes 99.9 % (≈2804–2859 s). The warning is correct and the number depends on the clip. Let \(p_{+}\) be the fraction of steps labelled 1 over the training set.

Proposition 4 (the constant baseline). Suppose every clip has the same number of steps \(n_{\mathrm{seq}}\), as on slide 17 (51 each). The predictor that outputs 0 at every step has accuracy \(1-p_{+}\). Among predictors that output the same probability \(\hat y\) at every step and on every clip, the average per-step binary cross-entropy is minimised at \(\hat y=p_{+}\), where it equals the binary entropy \(-p_{+}\log p_{+}-(1-p_{+})\log(1-p_{+})\).

Proof. With equal lengths, the average over clips of the per-clip average equals the average over all steps. The all-zero predictor is wrong exactly at the positive steps, a fraction \(p_{+}\). For the constant \(\hat y\), the average loss is \(-p_{+}\log\hat y-(1-p_{+})\log(1-\hat y)\), whose derivative in \(\hat y\) is \(-p_{+}/\hat y+(1-p_{+})/(1-\hat y)\); it is negative for \(\hat y<p_{+}\) and positive for \(\hat y>p_{+}\), so the minimum is at \(\hat y=p_{+}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Each clip on slide 17 is labelled with 51 digits, and the positive clips carry a single 1. For one such clip, \(p_{+}=1/51=0.0196\): the all-zero predictor is 98.0 % accurate, and the best constant probability attains a loss of 0.0965 nats (engineering check; the program checks the digit counts and prints both numbers). A model that ignores the audio therefore reaches 98 % accuracy, and any claim of progress must beat that baseline and that loss, not 50 %. The recording's 99.9 % corresponds to one positive step among 1,000. Accuracy is the wrong summary for this task. What matters are two rates: the fraction of spoken trigger words that are missed, and the rate at which silence or other words activate the assistant. The verification section names these the false-reject and false-accept rates.

The recording's remedy is to make the labels less skewed: instead of a single 1 at the end of the word, mark a run of consecutive steps after it (slide 18, second output; slide 19), which the recording calls an engineering hack with little science behind it (≈2859–2888 s). If a clip carries \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) such steps, then \(p_{+}=n_{\mathrm{on}}/n_{\mathrm{seq}}\); with \(n_{\mathrm{on}}=5\) of 51 steps, \(p_{+}=0.098\), the all-zero accuracy falls to 90.2 % and the best constant loss rises to 0.321 nats (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The change also changes what the label means: a 1 now says "the word ended within the last \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) steps", and the deployed system must fire once per run of positive predictions, not once per step. Chapter 6 treats the other standard remedy, weighting the loss of the rare class.

Manufacturing labelled clips 49:13

Labelling the position of a word by hand in thousands of clips is slow. The recording describes how the team avoided it (≈2968–3151 s; slide 19). Three pools were built: recordings of the positive word, cut to the exact length of the word; recordings of other words; and 10-second background clips, which are plentiful online, subject to licences that permit the use (≈3038–3062 s). A script pasted one positive word and some negative words, without overlap, at random positions into a background clip. Because the script chose the positions, it wrote the scheme-2 labels itself. Frequency changes and changes of speed can be applied as the clips are made. The recording reports that a few people with phones collected the word recordings within three hours and that the script produced millions of labelled clips from them (speaker judgment, ≈3090–3151 s).

The count explains the claim. With \(n_{\mathrm{pos}}\) positive recordings, \(n_{\mathrm{neg}}\) negative recordings and \(n_{\mathrm{bgc}}\) background clips, a clip made of one background, one positive word and an ordered pair of two different negative words can be assembled in \(n_{\mathrm{bgc}}\,n_{\mathrm{pos}}\,n_{\mathrm{neg}}(n_{\mathrm{neg}}-1)\) ways before positions are chosen. With the recording's figures of about 1,000 positive and 10,000 negative recordings (speaker judgment, ≈3119–3151 s) and, as an assumption of this example, 100 background clips, that is \(100\times1{,}000\times10{,}000\times9{,}999\approx1.0\times10^{13}\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The number of combinations is not the amount of information: every positive example is one of the same 1,000 recordings, and a network can learn those recordings instead of the word. For that reason the recording keeps the synthetic data for training and builds a smaller test set of real clips labelled by hand (≈3151–3179 s). The test set then measures whether what was learned from the synthetic clips transfers to real speech.

Face verification: a distance and a threshold 55:11

Provenance. Lecture: the dining-hall task of slide 21, the resolution, pixel comparison and its failures (slide 22), the encoding solution with a distance and a threshold (slide 23), the direction of the threshold trade-off (≈3311–3864 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the running-case model of face photographs, Proposition 5 and its proof, the error-rate table, Figure 1 and the pixel baseline.

The dining hall compares the photograph on the student card with a photograph taken by a camera at the door (slide 21). The input is the pair; the output is \(y=1\) if the two show the same person and 0 otherwise. The recording puts the resolution at \(412\times412\times3\), higher than for day and night because small details such as eye colour matter, and suggests the same human test as before with photographs of twins (≈3413–3530 s). This task is face verification: a one-to-one comparison between a claimed identity and a photograph.

The simplest method compares pixels: accept if the distance between the two pixel vectors is below a threshold (slide 22). The recording lists why it fails (≈3588–3738 s). A pixel in the top-left corner can be bright in one photograph and dark in the other, a difference near 255 that says nothing about the face. A shift of the face by three pixels, a change of distance to the camera or a rotation changes most pixels. Glasses, hats, a beard, make-up and the years since the card photograph was taken change the face itself. Pixel distance measures all of these at once, and most of them are irrelevant to identity.

The solution on slide 23 passes both photographs through the same deep network \(f_\theta\) and compares the outputs. The network maps each photograph to an encoding in \(\mathbb R^{n_e}\), with \(n_e=128\) on the slide, and the system computes the distance

\[d(x_1,x_2)=\bigl\lVert f_\theta(x_1)-f_\theta(x_2)\bigr\rVert_2 .\]

It accepts, \(y=1\), when \(d(x_1,x_2)<c\) for a decision threshold \(c>0\). Slide 23 shows a distance of 0.4 and the decision "0.4 < threshold"; the recording uses a threshold of 0.5 (≈3767–3823 s). The slide's vectors are illustrative: the six printed entries of the first vector already have Euclidean norm 1.44, and those of the second 1.37, so they are not the unit-length encodings of the method in the next section, and the distance 0.4 cannot be recomputed from them.

Two error rates describe the rule. The false-accept rate \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\) is the probability that two photographs of different people are accepted, \(\mathbb P(d<c\mid\text{different people})\). The false-reject rate \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)\) is the probability that two photographs of the same person are rejected, \(\mathbb P(d\ge c\mid\text{same person})\). Raising \(c\) accepts more pairs, so \(\mathrm{FAR}\) rises and \(\mathrm{FRR}\) falls; lowering \(c\) does the opposite. The recording states the first half, that a higher threshold is more relaxed and makes more mistakes of acceptance, and adds that airport security would use a lower threshold than a dining hall (≈3794–3823 s). The second half is the cost of that choice: a stricter door rejects more of its own students. The threshold at which the two rates are equal is the equal-error threshold, and the common value is the equal error rate (EER), a single number that summarises how well \(d\) separates the two kinds of pair.

The running case: photographs as identity plus nuisance

The chapter's running case is a small model of face photographs in which the reason pixel distance fails is explicit. A photograph is a vector \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\) with \(n_x=16\), split into three blocks:

  • a face block \(x_{\mathrm{face}}\in\mathbb R^{4}\), equal to \(\mu_j+x_{\mathrm{w}}\) for a photograph of person \(j\), where \(\mu_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}I)\) is that person's mean face, drawn once per person, and \(x_{\mathrm w}\sim\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}I)\) is drawn anew for each photograph (expression, age, a beard);
  • a lighting block \(x_{\mathrm{lit}}\in\mathbb R^{6}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), drawn anew for each photograph;
  • a scene block \(x_{\mathrm{scene}}\in\mathbb R^{6}\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), the background and framing, also drawn anew for each photograph.

Here \(\mathcal N(0,\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}I)\) is the Gaussian whose coordinates are independent with mean 0 and variance \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}\), and \(I\) is the identity matrix. The between-person variance is \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}=1\) and the within-person variance is \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}=0.09\). All blocks are independent. In this model the relation "same person" is carried by 4 of the 16 coordinates, and the other 12, which vary more, carry nothing about identity: they play the role of the lighting and background differences of slide 22. The block sizes and variances are choices of this book, made so that every effect below is visible in a program that runs in seconds.

Pixel distance in this model sums squared differences over all 16 coordinates. For two photographs of one person the face block contributes \(2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}=0.18\) per coordinate on average and each nuisance coordinate contributes 2; for two people the face block contributes \(2(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}+\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w})=2.18\) per coordinate. The 12 nuisance coordinates dominate both sums, and a threshold on pixel distance has an equal error rate of 0.352 (engineering check, 4,000 pairs of each kind). An encoder that keeps only the face block has an equal error rate of 0.056 (Proposition 5 and the table below), and for it the error rates have a closed form.

Proposition 5 (error rates of the face-block encoder). In the running case let \(f(x)=x_{\mathrm{face}}\) and \(d=\lVert f(x_1)-f(x_2)\rVert_2\). For two photographs of the same person and of two independently drawn people,

\[\mathrm{FRR}(c)=e^{-c^2/(4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w})}\Bigl(1+\frac{c^2}{4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}}\Bigr),\qquad \mathrm{FAR}(c)=1-e^{-c^2/(4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}})}\Bigl(1+\frac{c^2}{4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\Bigr),\]

with \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}}=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}+\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}\).

Proof. For one person, \(f(x_1)-f(x_2)=x_{\mathrm w,1}-x_{\mathrm w,2}\sim\mathcal N(0,2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}I)\) in \(\mathbb R^4\), so \(d=\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}}\,\lVert\chi\rVert_2\) with \(\chi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) in \(\mathbb R^4\), an auxiliary standard Gaussian vector. For two different people the difference also contains the difference of their mean faces, which is \(\mathcal N(0,2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}I)\) and independent of the rest, so \(d=\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\,\lVert\chi\rVert_2\). It remains to show that \(\mathbb P(\lVert\chi\rVert_2>c)=e^{-c^2/2}(1+c^2/2)\) for every \(c\ge0\). The density of \(\chi\), \((2\pi)^{-2}e^{-\lVert\chi\rVert_2^2/2}\), depends only on the length of \(\chi\), and the sphere of radius \(c\) in \(\mathbb R^4\) has three-dimensional area proportional to \(c^3\). So the length \(\lVert\chi\rVert_2\) has a density proportional to \(c^3e^{-c^2/2}\) at \(c\), and total probability 1 fixes the constant. The function \(e^{-c^2/2}(1+c^2/2)\) equals 1 at \(c=0\), tends to 0 as \(c\to\infty\), and has derivative \(e^{-c^2/2}\bigl(-c(1+c^2/2)+c\bigr)=-\tfrac12c^3e^{-c^2/2}\). It is therefore the upper tail of the density \(\tfrac12c^3e^{-c^2/2}\), which has the required form and total probability 1; so it is \(\mathbb P(\lVert\chi\rVert_2>c)\). Replacing \(c\) by \(c/\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}}\) gives \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)=\mathbb P(d\ge c)\); replacing it by \(c/\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}}}\) and taking the complement gives \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Training the encoder: the triplet loss 1:03:43

Provenance. Lecture: why the encoder is a vector and not a single output, public face datasets, triplets of anchor, positive and negative, the vote on slide 25, the loss of slide 26, the remark that the margin prevents a trivial answer, negatives used only in training, the FaceNet reference (slides 24–26, ≈3823–4382 s). Research extension: the triplet loss with hinge and margin, the unit-sphere constraint, semi-hard negatives and measured results of Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin. Original construction: Propositions 6 and 7, the training of the running-case encoder, the unit-sphere oracle check and Figure 2.

The network \(f_\theta\) of the previous section has not been trained. The dining hall's own data are not enough: each student has at least one photograph on file (≈3369–3413 s), and a single photograph cannot form a triplet. Slide 24 proposes training on public face datasets, which contain many photographs of each of many people, and applying the trained encoder to students it has never seen. The recording also answers a student who asks why the encoding is a vector and not the network's final probability: a single number cannot hold the information needed to compare two faces (speaker judgment, ≈4098–4128 s).

What training must achieve can be said in words (slide 24): photographs of the same person should have close encodings, and photographs of different people distant ones. A triplet makes this a loss. It consists of an anchor photograph \(x^{\mathrm a}\), a positive \(x^{\mathrm p}\) of the same person, and a negative \(x^{\mathrm n}\) of a different person. The recording asks the audience which of three expressions to minimise (slide 25); the answer is the difference of squared distances \(\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm p})\rVert_2^2-\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm n})\rVert_2^2\), because minimising it pulls the positive in and pushes the negative out. The two other options reverse the signs or compare the positive with the negative, and they reward the opposite of what is wanted.

Slide 26 adds a constant \(\alpha\), which this book writes \(\Delta\), and minimises the difference plus \(\Delta\). The loss of Schroff, Kalenichenko and Philbin (2015), the FaceNet paper the slide cites, differs in two ways. First, it constrains every encoding to the unit sphere, \(\lVert f_\theta(x)\rVert_2=1\) (p. 3). Second, it applies a hinge \([\cdot]_{+}=\max(\cdot,0)\) to each triplet (p. 3, eq. 3):

\[\mathcal L\bigl(x^{\mathrm a},x^{\mathrm p},x^{\mathrm n}\bigr)=\Bigl[\bigl\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm p})\bigr\rVert_2^2-\bigl\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm n})\bigr\rVert_2^2+\Delta\Bigr]_{+}.\]

The loss is zero when the negative is farther from the anchor than the positive by at least the margin, \(\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm n})\rVert_2^2\ge\lVert f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})-f_\theta(x^{\mathrm p})\rVert_2^2+\Delta\), which is the constraint FaceNet wants for all triplets (p. 3, eq. 1). FaceNet uses \(\Delta=0.2\) (p. 4). The slide's version has neither the hinge nor the sphere. The next two propositions show what each piece does.

Proposition 6 (gradients of the triplet loss). Write \(h^{\mathrm a}=f_\theta(x^{\mathrm a})\), \(h^{\mathrm p}\), \(h^{\mathrm n}\) for the three encodings. If the bracket is positive, then \(\nabla_{h^{\mathrm a}}\mathcal L=2(h^{\mathrm n}-h^{\mathrm p})\), \(\nabla_{h^{\mathrm p}}\mathcal L=2(h^{\mathrm p}-h^{\mathrm a})\) and \(\nabla_{h^{\mathrm n}}\mathcal L=2(h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm n})\); if it is negative, all three gradients are zero.

Proof. Where the bracket is positive the hinge is the identity. \(\nabla_{h^{\mathrm a}}\lVert h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm p}\rVert^2=2(h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm p})\) and \(\nabla_{h^{\mathrm a}}\lVert h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm n}\rVert^2=2(h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm n})\); their difference is \(2(h^{\mathrm n}-h^{\mathrm p})\). The positive appears only in the first term and the negative only in the second, with a minus sign. Where the bracket is negative the loss is 0 in a neighbourhood. \(\blacksquare\)

A gradient step moves each encoding against its gradient: the positive moves toward the anchor, the negative away from it, and the anchor toward the positive and away from the negative. The derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) then follow by backpropagation through the three copies of the same network, which is why the recording describes the three photographs as passing through one network side by side (≈4261–4292 s). On the unit sphere one more factor enters: if an encoding \(h=u/\lVert u\rVert_2\) is the normalised version of the network output \(u\), then \(\nabla_u\mathcal L=(I-hh^\top)\nabla_h\mathcal L/\lVert u\rVert_2\), because \(\partial h_i/\partial u_j=\mathbb 1[i=j]/\lVert u\rVert_2-u_iu_j/\lVert u\rVert_2^3=(\mathbb 1[i=j]-h_ih_j)/\lVert u\rVert_2\), so the Jacobian of the normalisation is \((I-hh^\top)/\lVert u\rVert_2\), and it is symmetric. The factor removes the component of the gradient along \(h\), the only direction in which a unit vector cannot move.

Proposition 7 (what the hinge and the margin buy). Consider a training set whose triplets use at least two people.

  1. A constant encoder, one that returns the same unit vector for every input, gives every triplet the loss \(\Delta\). With \(\Delta=0\) it attains the smallest possible value of the hinged loss, 0.
  2. Without the hinge and without the sphere, if some encoder gives one triplet a negative distance difference, the loss is unbounded below.
  3. Without the hinge but on the sphere, the loss of a triplet is at least \(\Delta-4\), and its gradient is the one of Proposition 6 whether or not the triplet already satisfies the margin.
  4. With the hinge but without the sphere, the margin constrains nothing: any encoder that orders every triplet strictly, \(\lVert h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm n}\rVert^2>\lVert h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm p}\rVert^2\), becomes an encoder with zero loss when multiplied by a large enough constant.

Proof. (1) A constant encoder makes both distances 0, so the bracket equals \(\Delta\); the hinged loss is never negative. (2) Multiplying the encoder by a positive factor multiplies every squared distance by the square of that factor. For a triplet whose distance difference is negative, the unhinged loss becomes \(\Delta\) plus that square times a negative number, which tends to \(-\infty\) as the factor grows. (3) Unit vectors satisfy \(\lVert h^{\mathrm a}-h^{\mathrm n}\rVert^2=2-2(h^{\mathrm a})^\top h^{\mathrm n}\le4\), and likewise for the positive, so the difference of two squared distances is at least \(-4\). Without the hinge the gradient formula of Proposition 6 holds everywhere. (4) Let the smallest strict gap over the triplets be positive. After multiplying the encoder by a factor whose square times that gap exceeds \(\Delta\), every bracket is negative. \(\blacksquare\)

Each item names a way the objective can reward the wrong encoder. Item 1 is the recording's hint to the vote: without the margin, the answer "all encodings equal" is a perfect score (≈4292–4321 s). The margin makes the collapsed encoder cost \(\Delta\) per triplet, while an encoder that separates every triplet by \(\Delta\) costs nothing. Item 2 shows that slide 26's loss, taken literally with unconstrained encodings, is minimised by pushing negatives to infinity. Item 3 shows what the hinge adds even on the sphere: triplets that are already satisfied stop contributing, and the training signal concentrates on the triplets that are still wrong. Item 4 shows that the sphere is what gives \(\Delta\) a meaning: on the unit sphere squared distances lie in \([0,4]\), and \(\Delta=0.2\) is a fixed fraction of that range. The proposition is about the minimisers of the objective. Whether gradient descent from a random start approaches a collapsed encoder is a separate question about the training dynamics; FaceNet reports that choosing the hardest negatives "can in practice lead to bad local minima early on in training", in particular "a collapsed model (i.e. \(f(x)=0\))", and avoids it by choosing semi-hard negatives, those farther from the anchor than the positive but inside the margin (p. 4).

The running case, trained

The program of the implementation section trains a linear encoder on the running case: \(f_\theta(x)=Wx/\lVert Wx\rVert_2\) with \(W\in\mathbb R^{4\times16}\), so \(n_e=4\), the hinged loss with \(\Delta=0.2\), and 1,500 steps of gradient descent with \(\eta=0.5\). Each step draws 64 new people, two photographs of each, and one photograph of another new person as the negative. The trained encoder is then evaluated on people drawn after training, as the dining hall would apply it to students. Its equal error rate is 0.087, against 0.397 for the random starting encoder and 0.352 for pixel distance (engineering check). The trained \(W\) has 99.8 % of its norm on the face block, and Figure 2 shows the consequence: the distances between photographs of one person and of two people, which overlap almost completely in pixel space, separate. The encoder does not reach the oracle's 0.056, and most of the gap is the normalisation, not the training. The oracle encoder projected onto the unit sphere, \(f(x)=x_{\mathrm{face}}/\lVert x_{\mathrm{face}}\rVert_2\), has an equal error rate of 0.081 (engineering check), because the projection discards the length of the face block, which the oracle's distance uses. The normalisation therefore accounts for 0.025 of the gap of 0.031 between 0.087 and 0.056, and the trained encoder's 0.087 is within 0.006 of what a unit-length encoder that keeps exactly the face block achieves.

FaceNet's measured results show the method at scale. On the Labeled Faces in the Wild benchmark it reports 99.63 % accuracy (p. 1; empirical result on that benchmark). It chose \(n_e=128\), and its comparison of encoding dimensions 64, 128, 256 and 512 found validation rates between 85.6 % and 87.9 %, differences the authors call statistically insignificant (p. 7, Table 5; empirical result for their model). The recording's 128 is FaceNet's choice, not a derived optimum. Negatives exist only during training (≈4446–4514 s): at the door there is one card photograph and one camera photograph, and the decision is the threshold rule of the previous section. The recording says the vector is taken from deep inside the network; in FaceNet it is the network's last layer, trained by the triplet loss itself and normalised (p. 3, Figure 2).

Provenance. Verification answers "is this the person on the card?". Identification answers "who, among the enrolled people, is this?" with no card (slide 27). The recording contrasts border control that compares a passport photograph with a camera photograph, which is verification, with systems that recognise travellers from the camera alone, which is identification (≈4409–4446 s; speaker's description of deployed systems, not checked). The trained encoder serves both. Encode every enrolled photograph once and store the encodings instead of the images. For a camera photograph \(x\), compute \(f_\theta(x)\), find the stored encodings nearest to it, and return the identity they belong to (≈4518–4588 s). This is the nearest-neighbour rule. Its version with \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) neighbours, usually called k-nearest neighbours, returns the identity held by most of the \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) nearest encodings; the recording's variant stores three photographs per student and accepts only if all three nearest encodings belong to the same person, which trades coverage for confidence (≈4588–4665 s). A person who is not enrolled should be rejected: if the nearest encoding is farther than the threshold \(c\), the system answers "unknown" (≈4689–4724 s).

Identification is harder than verification, and the difference can be computed. A verification query compares a photograph with one stored identity; an identification query compares it with all \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) of them, and every comparison is a chance of a false accept.

Proposition 8 (false matches grow with the database). Suppose a person who is not enrolled is compared with \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) enrolled identities, each comparison falsely accepts with probability \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\), and the comparisons are independent. Then the probability of at least one false match is \(1-(1-\mathrm{FAR}(c))^{N_{\mathrm{id}}}\). The expected number of false matches is \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\), with or without the independence assumption.

Proof. By independence, no comparison accepts with probability \((1-\mathrm{FAR}(c))^{N_{\mathrm{id}}}\); take the complement. The number of false matches is a sum of \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) indicators, each with mean \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\), and the mean of a sum is the sum of the means whether or not the terms are independent. \(\blacksquare\)

A threshold with \(\mathrm{FAR}=0.001\), acceptable for a card check, gives a stranger a probability \(1-0.999^{10{,}000}=0.99995\) of matching someone in a school of 10,000 enrolled students, and 10 false matches on average (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Independence is an assumption: photographs of look-alikes are correlated, which changes the number but not the growth. The running case shows the same effect without the assumption. With one enrolled photograph per person and the triplet-trained encoder, the nearest-neighbour rule identifies the right person in 61.6 % of queries among 10 enrolled people, 40.3 % among 100 and 9.1 % among 1,000 (engineering check, 1,000 queries each). The encoder whose equal error rate for one-to-one verification is 0.087 identifies the right person in fewer than one query in ten among 1,000 enrolled people; identification needs a far lower false-accept rate than verification, and so a better encoder.

Self-supervision: contrastive learning without labels 1:20:26

Provenance. Lecture: the question of training an encoder from unlabelled faces, the audience's proposals (clustering, reconstruction), rotations and patches as views of the same image, the SimCLR reference and the move from labelled triplets to unlabelled pairs (slide 30, ≈4826–5217 s). Research extension: the NT-Xent loss, projection head, batch negatives, augmentation findings and measured results of Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton. Original construction: the collapse reading of the spoken version, the derivation of the gradient, and the running-case experiment with full and partial augmentation.

Everything so far used labels: day or night, the position of a word, the name behind a face. Labels are expensive, and the recording asks how an encoder for faces could be trained from photographs alone, with no names (≈4855–4926 s). Proposals to cluster first fail for the reason the recording gives: clustering needs a good encoder, and the encoder is what is missing (≈4926–4964 s). A proposal to train the network to reconstruct its input is deferred to the generative models of chapter 4.

The recording's answer is to let the data supply the pairs. Rotate a photograph of a dog by 90 degrees: a person still sees the same dog. Cover half of a face in one copy and the other half in another: it is still one face. Present two such altered views of one image to the network and require their encodings to agree (≈5047–5131 s). The labels are generated by the procedure itself, which is why the method is called self-supervised (≈5131–5158 s). The alterations are data augmentations: random crops, flips, rotations, colour changes, blur and noise.

As spoken, the procedure contains only positive pairs: the two views of one image should receive the same vector. Taken literally, that objective is minimised by the constant encoder, for the reason of Proposition 7(1): if every image has the same encoding, every pair agrees perfectly. The loss on slide 30 and in the paper it cites also pushes apart the encodings of different images, the negative pairs, and that term is what excludes the collapse. Chen, Kornblith, Norouzi and Hinton (2020) take a minibatch of \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) images, make two augmented views of each, and treat, for each view, the other view of the same image as its positive and the other \(2(n_{\mathrm{batch}}-1)\) views as its negatives, without sampling negatives separately (p. 2). Number the \(2n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) views by \(i\), let \(\mathrm{pos}(i)\) be the index of the other view of the same image, and let \(\cos(f_\theta(x_i),f_\theta(x_j))=f_\theta(x_i)^\top f_\theta(x_j)/(\lVert f_\theta(x_i)\rVert_2\lVert f_\theta(x_j)\rVert_2)\) be the cosine similarity of two encodings. The loss of view \(i\) is (p. 2, eq. 1)

\[\mathcal L_i=-\log\frac{\exp\bigl(\cos(f_\theta(x_i),f_\theta(x_{\mathrm{pos}(i)}))/\tau\bigr)}{\sum_{j\ne i}\exp\bigl(\cos(f_\theta(x_i),f_\theta(x_j))/\tau\bigr)}.\]

The display is written on the encodings \(f_\theta(x_i)\); the paper computes it on the output of a small projection network placed after the encoder, and a later paragraph gives its measurement of that choice. The loss is averaged over all \(2n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) views. The temperature \(\tau>0\) scales the similarities. The authors name this loss NT-Xent, "the normalized temperature-scaled cross entropy loss", and note that it had been used before (p. 2). The subscript \(i\) here numbers views in a batch, not training examples.

The loss is a categorical cross-entropy in disguise. For view \(i\), treat the \(2n_{\mathrm{batch}}-1\) other views as classes, the scaled similarities \(\cos(\cdot,\cdot)/\tau\) as logits, and the index \(\mathrm{pos}(i)\) as the one-hot target. Then \(\mathcal L_i\) is exactly the loss of Proposition 2(a), and its gradient with respect to the similarity with view \(j\) is

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal L_i}{\partial\cos(f_\theta(x_i),f_\theta(x_j))}=\frac{\hat y_{ij}-\mathbb 1[j=\mathrm{pos}(i)]}{\tau},\]

where \(\hat y_{ij}\) is the softmax weight, the predicted probability, that view \(i\) puts on view \(j\). The positive's similarity is pushed up with weight \(1-\hat y_{i,\mathrm{pos}(i)}\), and each negative's similarity is pushed down in proportion to \(\hat y_{ij}\), that is, in proportion to how similar it already is. A small temperature sharpens the softmax and concentrates the push on the most similar negatives; exercise 5 computes the weights for \(\tau=1\) and \(\tau=0.1\). SimCLR's measurements are consistent with this reading: with normalised encodings, linear-evaluation top-1 accuracy was 64.4 % at \(\tau=0.1\) and 58.0 % at \(\tau=1\), and without normalisation it was 57.2 % and 57.0 % at the two temperatures tried (p. 7, Table 5; empirical result). The authors read their batch-size results as larger batches supplying more negatives and so reaching a given accuracy in fewer epochs (p. 7, Figure 9; empirical result for their runs). With a batch of 8,192 there are 16,382 negatives per positive pair (p. 3). As noted at the display, SimCLR computes the loss on the output of the projection network and uses the representation before that projection for downstream tasks (p. 2; the representation before the projection was better by more than 10 %, p. 6). A linear classifier trained on the representations of a ResNet-50 four times wider than the standard one reached 76.5 % top-1 accuracy on ImageNet, which the authors describe as matching a supervised ResNet-50 (p. 1; p. 7, Table 6; empirical result).

What the augmentations decide

Contrastive learning keeps whatever features let a view find its partner among the other views. Features the augmentations change cannot help a view find its partner, and features they leave unchanged can; the objective therefore rewards the second kind and ignores the first, whether or not a rewarded feature is the one the downstream task needs. The running case makes this exact. A photograph's face block, lighting and scene are drawn independently. Suppose the augmentation redraws the lighting and the scene blocks and keeps the face block. The two views of a photograph then share only the face block, and it is the only information that can identify the partner. If the augmentation redraws only the lighting, the two views share the scene block too, and the scene identifies a photograph among others as well as the face does.

The program trains the same linear encoder as in the triplet section, with NT-Xent at \(\tau=0.1\), batches of 64 photographs, no identity labels, and the same number of steps. With augmentations that redraw lighting and scene, the equal error rate for verification on new people is 0.088, against 0.087 for the triplet-trained encoder, although no name was ever used. With augmentations that redraw only the lighting, it is 0.394, no better than the random encoder, and 84 % of the norm of the trained \(W\) sits on the scene block (engineering check). The encoder learned to recognise backgrounds, because the training task rewarded exactly that. SimCLR reports the same failure in real images: with cropping alone, patches of one image share a colour distribution, colour histograms alone suffice to tell images apart, and "neural nets may exploit this shortcut" (p. 5). Its remedy is to compose cropping with colour distortion, and its broader finding is that no single augmentation suffices, although the model then identifies positive pairs almost perfectly (p. 5).

SimCLR's own experiments use the ImageNet ILSVRC-2012 images without their labels (p. 2). The recording adds that face verification can emerge from contrastive training on photographs scraped online without anyone defining that task (speaker judgment, ≈5329–5352 s). The running case shows the condition under which this holds: the augmentations must change everything that is not identity.

Self-supervision in text: predicting the next token 1:26:57

Provenance. Text supplies its own targets. Split a text into tokens \(v_1,v_2,\dots\), elements of a finite vocabulary \(\mathcal V\) of size \(n_{\mathrm{voc}}\); the recording asks the reader to think of tokens as words for now (≈5217–5238 s). A language model reads the preceding tokens \(v_{1:t-1}\) and outputs a probability vector over \(\mathcal V\) through a softmax, and it is trained to give high probability to the token that actually comes next. The loss at position \(t\) is the categorical cross-entropy with the one-hot target \(v_t\), \(-\log\hat y(v_t\mid v_{1:t-1})\), so Proposition 2(a) gives its gradient at the logits. No person labels anything: every position of every text on the web is a training item, which is why the recording calls the method self-supervised (≈5238–5268 s). Radford, Narasimhan, Salimans and Sutskever state the objective as maximising \(\sum_t\log p(v_t\mid v_{t-n_{\mathrm{ctx}}},\dots,v_{t-1};\theta)\) over a corpus of tokens, with a context window of \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}\) tokens (p. 3, eq. 1; the paper writes the tokens \(u_i\), the window \(k\) and the parameters \(\Theta\)), pretrained on BooksCorpus, "over 7,000 unique unpublished books" (p. 4), and then fine-tuned on labelled tasks (p. 1). Slide 31 dates this report to 2017; the PDF's creation date is 8 June 2018, and it cites papers published in 2018 (pp. 9–10).

What does a model trained this way learn to output? The answer does not depend on the network.

Proposition 9 (what next-token cross-entropy rewards). Fix a context, and let \(p(v)\) be the probability that token \(v\) follows it in the training distribution. Among all probability vectors \(\hat y\) over \(\mathcal V\), the expected loss \(\sum_vp(v)\bigl(-\log\hat y(v)\bigr)\) is minimised exactly by \(\hat y=p\).

Proof. The expected loss equals \(-\sum_vp(v)\log p(v)+\sum_vp(v)\log\bigl(p(v)/\hat y(v)\bigr)\). The first term does not depend on \(\hat y\). The second, the Kullback–Leibler divergence of \(\hat y\) from \(p\), is nonnegative: for every token with \(p(v)>0\), the logarithm of the ratio \(\hat y(v)/p(v)\) is at most the ratio minus 1, with equality only when the ratio is 1, because the ratio minus 1 minus its logarithm vanishes at 1 and its derivative with respect to the ratio, 1 minus the reciprocal of the ratio, changes sign there. Hence \(\sum_vp(v)\log(\hat y(v)/p(v))\le\sum_vp(v)\bigl(\hat y(v)/p(v)-1\bigr)=\sum_v\hat y(v)-\sum_vp(v)\le0\), where the sums run over tokens with \(p(v)>0\). Equality forces \(\hat y(v)=p(v)\) wherever \(p(v)>0\), and then \(\hat y\) has no mass left for other tokens. \(\blacksquare\)

Emergent behaviour: two definitions

The recording defines emergent behaviours as capabilities that nobody taught or labelled and that arise, unexpectedly, from a simple objective trained at scale (slide 31; ≈5499–5522 s). The research literature uses the word for a narrower and disputed phenomenon. Wei et al. (2022) "consider an ability to be emergent if it is not present in smaller models but is present in larger models", so that it "cannot be predicted simply by extrapolating the performance of smaller models" (p. 1; definition on p. 2). Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) argue that such abilities can "appear due the researcher's choice of metric rather than due to fundamental changes in model behavior with scale" (p. 1). Their illustration is short enough to check: if each token of an answer is correct with probability \(p_{\mathrm{tok}}\), independently, then an exact-match score on an answer of \(n_{\mathrm{seq}}\) tokens is about \(p_{\mathrm{tok}}^{\,n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\) (p. 4, where they note that the independence assumption is not true). For \(n_{\mathrm{seq}}=10\), a per-token probability that rises smoothly through 0.5, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95 and 0.99 gives exact-match scores of 0.001, 0.107, 0.349, 0.599 and 0.904 (engineering check by shown arithmetic): flat, then steep, although nothing about the per-token behaviour changed abruptly. Weak supervision: pairs that occur in the world 1:34:14

Provenance. Lecture: connecting modalities, captioned images as naturally occurring pairs, the term weakly supervised (slide 33), the audience's examples of natural pairs, ImageBind and its demonstration, shared embedding spaces (slides 34–35, ≈5654–5981 s). Research extension: the contrastive objective and data of Radford et al. (CLIP); the image-binding method and results of Girdhar et al. (ImageBind). Original construction: the triangle-inequality bound on emergent alignment.

A photograph posted with a caption is a pair that nobody labelled for machine learning. The recording calls training on such pairs weakly supervised: the pairing acts as supervision, but it was not produced by annotators and it is loose, since a caption describes only part of an image (≈5654–5711 s; slide 33). Its purpose is to give different modalities one embedding space, so that an image and a sentence about it have nearby encodings (≈5711–5740 s). The audience's examples of natural pairs include a video and its soundtrack, films and their subtitles, a song and its title, a genotype and a phenotype, ultrasound images taken together, and game footage with the keystrokes that produced it (≈5750–5859 s).

Radford et al. (2021) trained the model CLIP on 400 million (image, text) pairs collected from the internet (p. 1). For a batch of \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) pairs, an image encoder and a text encoder produce normalised encodings, and the model is trained to predict which of the \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\times n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) possible (image, text) pairings across a batch actually occurred, by maximising the cosine similarity of the \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) true pairs and minimising it for the others with "a symmetric cross entropy loss" over the similarity matrix (p. 4; the paper writes the batch size \(N\)). Each row of the matrix is a softmax problem in which an image must pick its caption, and each column one in which a caption must pick its image: two losses of the same form as NT-Xent, with the positive coming from the other modality instead of from an augmentation and the candidates restricted to the \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) items of that modality. The temperature is learned, initialised at 0.07, and the batch has 32,768 pairs (p. 5).

Aligning every pair of modalities directly would need data in which each pair occurs together. Girdhar et al. (2023) show that "only image-paired data is sufficient to bind the modalities together" (p. 1). ImageBind aligns each of audio, depth, thermal and inertial-sensor data with images, using naturally paired data such as videos with their audio, and takes its image and text encoders from a pretrained CLIP-style model, kept frozen (p. 2; p. 4). Because images and text were already aligned and every other modality is aligned to images, pairs such as audio and text that were never trained together end up close in the shared space, an "emergent alignment" (p. 2). Slide 34 reproduces the paper's Figure 2, which draws images at the hub with the other modalities attached to it. The recording describes the method the same way at ≈5883–5890 s: thermal data connect to images, images to text. Slide 35's summary instead names text as the central pivot, and the recording says that most modalities connect to text, which is therefore the usual shared space (≈5859–5883 s). As a statement about ImageBind this is wrong: its binding modality is the image. As a statement about CLIP-style systems in which text is one side of every pair, it is speaker judgment.

Why should an alignment that was never trained hold? The triangle inequality gives a bound, and its hypothesis shows what the bound needs. Let an audio clip \(x_{\mathrm{au}}\) and a sentence \(x_{\mathrm{tx}}\) both be paired with the same image \(x_{\mathrm{im}}\), and let the three encoders map them into one space. Then

\[\bigl\lVert f_{\mathrm{au}}(x_{\mathrm{au}})-f_{\mathrm{tx}}(x_{\mathrm{tx}})\bigr\rVert_2\le\bigl\lVert f_{\mathrm{au}}(x_{\mathrm{au}})-f_{\mathrm{im}}(x_{\mathrm{im}})\bigr\rVert_2+\bigl\lVert f_{\mathrm{im}}(x_{\mathrm{im}})-f_{\mathrm{tx}}(x_{\mathrm{tx}})\bigr\rVert_2 .\]

If both trained alignments hold to within a small distance, the untrained one holds to within their sum. The bound needs an image that plausibly goes with both the sound and the sentence; it bounds a distance, not the ranking that a retrieval query returns, and it says nothing when no such image exists. It matches the paper's observation that the emergent capabilities "improve with the strength of the image encoder" (p. 1; empirical result).

A running-case laboratory in NumPy

Provenance. Original construction throughout. It implements Propositions 1–5, the triplet gradient of Proposition 6, the NT-Xent gradient of the contrastive section and the nearest-neighbour rule.

The program below checks the chapter's central numbers in one run. Its inputs are fixed constants: a logit of 0.7, a three-class logit vector, a two-layer network with seeded random weights, one label string of slide 17, and the running case with \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}=1\), \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}=0.09\), a face block of 4 coordinates and 12 nuisance coordinates of unit variance. All random draws come from one generator with seed 0. The program has no side effects beyond printing. Each check is an assert, so a wrong formula or a changed constant stops the program with an AssertionError. It needs Python 3 and NumPy (tested with Python 3.13.5 and NumPy 2.2.6). Fresh runs took 18.3 s of wall time or less, depending on the machine and its load; the fastest measured run took 1.4 s.

The program has four numbered parts. Part 1 checks Proposition 1 at the logit 0.7 and Proposition 2(a) at the logits \((2,1,-1)\) by central differences, and checks one entry of the backpropagated gradient of Proposition 3. Part 2 computes the baseline of Proposition 4 for the slide 17 label string. Part 3 builds the running case: it compares the closed form of Proposition 5 at \(c=1\) with simulated pairs, computes the equal error rates of pixel distance and of the face block, and then trains three copies of one random linear encoder for 1,500 steps, one with the triplet loss and two with NT-Xent under the two augmentations of the contrastive section. The functions trip_grad and nt_grad return a loss and its gradient with respect to \(W\), and both pass the gradient through the normalisation with the factor \((I-hh^\top)/\lVert u\rVert_2\) of the triplet section. Part 4 measures nearest-neighbour identification with the triplet-trained encoder; the last computation is the equal error rate of the oracle encoder projected onto the unit sphere.

The helper eer finds the equal-error threshold by bisection on \(c\). Before training, the program compares one entry of the triplet and NT-Xent gradients with a central difference of the returned loss, on inputs from a separate generator with seed 1 so that the training draws are unchanged; both agree to \(10^{-6}\).

import numpy as np

H = 1e-6                                          # finite-difference step


def sigmoid(u):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-u))


def softmax(u):
    e = np.exp(u - u.max())
    return e / e.sum()


def bce(u, y):
    return -y * math.log(sigmoid(u)) - (1 - y) * math.log(1 - sigmoid(u))


def cce(u, y):
    return float(-np.log(softmax(u)) @ y)

def eer(genuine, impostor):
    """Equal error rate and its threshold c, by bisection on c."""
    lo, hi = 0.0, float(max(genuine.max(), impostor.max()))
    for _ in range(60):
        c = (lo + hi) / 2
        if np.mean(genuine > c) > np.mean(impostor <= c):
            lo = c
        else:
            hi = c
    return round(float(np.mean(genuine > c)), 3), round(c, 3)

def trip_grad(W, xa, xp, xn, margin):
    zs = [x @ W.T for x in (xa, xp, xn)]
    ns = [np.linalg.norm(z, axis=1, keepdims=True) for z in zs]
    ea, ep, en = [z / n for z, n in zip(zs, ns)]
    slack = np.sum((ea - ep) ** 2, 1) - np.sum((ea - en) ** 2, 1) + margin
    act = (slack > 0)[:, None]
    grads = [act * 2 * (en - ep), act * 2 * (ep - ea), act * 2 * (ea - en)]
    G = np.zeros_like(W)
    for e, n, g, x in zip((ea, ep, en), ns, grads, (xa, xp, xn)):
        G += ((g - np.sum(g * e, 1, keepdims=True) * e) / n).T @ x
    return np.mean(np.maximum(slack, 0)), G / len(xa)


def nt_grad(W, v1, v2, tau):
    x = np.vstack([v1, v2])
    z = x @ W.T
    nz = np.linalg.norm(z, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    e = z / nz
    S = e @ e.T / tau
    np.fill_diagonal(S, -np.inf)                  # an item is not its pair
    P = np.exp(S - S.max(1, keepdims=True))
    P /= P.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    n2 = len(x)
    pos = np.roll(np.arange(n2), n2 // 2)         # index of the other view
    R = P.copy()
    R[np.arange(n2), pos] -= 1.0                  # dLoss/dS times n2
    dE = (R + R.T) @ e / (tau * n2)
    dz = (dE - np.sum(dE * e, 1, keepdims=True) * e) / nz
    return -np.mean(np.log(P[np.arange(n2), pos])), dz.T @ x

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction, with one statement from FaceNet and one from SimCLR.

Each result of this chapter holds under an idealisation, and each idealisation fails in practice in a specific way.

The heads and the chain rule. Propositions 1–3 are exact for the stated networks. They say what gradient descent is given, not where it ends: the cost of a multilayer network is not convex, and nothing in the chapter guarantees that training finds a good minimum.

Label skew. Proposition 4 gives the baseline that any per-step detector must beat. Changing the labels, as the run of ones does, changes the task being learned; the deployed system must be evaluated on what it must do, detect each spoken trigger word once, and not on per-step accuracy.

The running case. Identity in the model sits in 4 known coordinates, the nuisance is independent Gaussian noise, and the encoder is linear. In photographs identity is spread over all pixels and entangled with pose and light, and no linear map separates it. Proposition 5 is exact for the oracle encoder of the model and for nothing else; the other rates in the chapter are measured on the model only.

Objectives versus training. Proposition 7 describes the minimisers of the triplet objective. Whether training reaches a collapsed or a useful encoder depends on the dynamics and on which triplets are chosen; FaceNet's semi-hard negatives (p. 4) are an engineering answer, not a theorem. The same holds for NT-Xent, whose negatives exclude the collapsed encoder as a minimiser but do not guarantee what training finds.

Independence. Proposition 8 and the exact-match computation assume independent events. Look-alike faces and dependent tokens violate the assumption; the direction of the effect, more comparisons giving more false matches, survives, but the numbers do not.

Augmentations. The running case shows that contrastive learning keeps what the augmentations do not change. In real data nobody knows the list of nuisance factors, so the choice of augmentations is a judgment about the downstream task, and SimCLR's finding that composition matters (p. 5) is an empirical result for ImageNet, not a rule for other data.

Claims not checked. Statements about deployed voice assistants, border-control systems, phone photo software and the data-collection anecdotes of the recording are speaker judgments or product claims recorded with their times.

What you can now do. You can derive the binary and categorical cross-entropies from likelihoods, compute their gradients at the logits, choose between a softmax and independent sigmoids from the form of the labels, and derive backpropagation from the chain rule. You can specify a supervised task, count what its resolution costs, compute the baseline of a skewed per-step label and count the clips a synthesis script can make. You can state a verification rule as a distance and a threshold, compute its false-accept and false-reject rates in a model where they have closed forms, and explain why identification needs a stricter false-accept rate than verification. You can write the triplet loss, its gradients, and the roles of its hinge, margin and normalisation; write NT-Xent as a cross-entropy over similarities and explain what the temperature and the augmentations decide; state what next-token cross-entropy rewards; and explain how pairs of modalities, and pairs through images, align encoders.

1. A photograph with two animals

A softmax head over \(K\) classes is trained with the categorical cross-entropy on a multi-hot target \(y\in\{0,1\}^K\) with \(\lVert y\rVert_1=\sum_ky_k\ge1\) ones. (a) Show that \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_j=\lVert y\rVert_1\hat y_j-y_j\) and that the loss is minimised over probability vectors at \(\hat y=y/\lVert y\rVert_1\). (b) For \(y=(1,1,0)\), give the minimising output and the minimum loss, and compare with three independent sigmoids trained with summed binary cross-entropy. (c) Show that adding the same constant to every logit leaves a softmax unchanged, and say what the same change does to independent sigmoids.

Solution

(a) The proof of Proposition 2(a) gives \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_j=-y_j+\hat y_j\sum_ky_k=\lVert y\rVert_1\hat y_j-y_j\), which vanishes at \(\hat y=y/\lVert y\rVert_1\). To see that this is the minimum over probability vectors, write \(\mathcal L=-\sum_ky_k\log\hat y_k=\lVert y\rVert_1\bigl(-\sum_k(y_k/\lVert y\rVert_1)\log\hat y_k\bigr)\). The vector \(y/\lVert y\rVert_1\) is a probability vector, and by Proposition 9 the bracket is minimised exactly at \(\hat y=y/\lVert y\rVert_1\). (b) The output is \((\tfrac12,\tfrac12,0)\) in the limit \(u_3\to-\infty\), and the minimum loss is \(-2\log\tfrac12=2\log2=1.386\) nats. The softmax must split its mass between the two animals and can never assert both. Three sigmoids have loss \(-\log\sigma(u_1)-\log\sigma(u_2)-\log(1-\sigma(u_3))\), which tends to 0 as \(u_1,u_2\to+\infty\) and \(u_3\to-\infty\): each head says "present" or "absent" on its own. (c) Adding a constant to every logit multiplies the numerator \(e^{u_k}\) and every term of the denominator \(\sum_je^{u_j}\) by the exponential of that constant, which cancels. A softmax sees only differences of logits. Adding a positive constant to every logit of independent sigmoids raises every output probability, because each output depends on its own logit alone; the sigmoid head has no such redundancy, and its logits have an absolute meaning.

2. Baselines for the three clips of slide 17

Slide 17 labels three clips with 51 steps each; the first and third carry one 1, the second none. (a) Over the three clips, compute \(p_{+}\), the accuracy of the all-zero predictor, and the best constant per-step binary cross-entropy. (b) What fraction of positive steps would make the all-zero accuracy 99.9 %, and how many steps per positive clip does that imply with one positive step each? (c) Relabel the two positive clips with runs of \(n_{\mathrm{on}}=5\) ones and recompute (a). (d) Which assumption makes "accuracy of the all-zero predictor" a meaningful baseline, and which two numbers should a trigger-word detector report instead?

Solution

(a) Two of 153 steps are positive: \(p_{+}=2/153=0.0131\). By Proposition 4 the all-zero accuracy is \(1-p_{+}=0.987\), and the best constant loss is the binary entropy of 0.0131, 0.0697 nats. (b) The all-zero accuracy is \(1-p_{+}\), so 99.9 % needs \(p_{+}=0.001\). With one positive step per positive clip, and every clip positive, that is 1,000 steps per clip; with negative clips mixed in, fewer. (c) Ten of 153 steps are positive: \(p_{+}=0.0654\), all-zero accuracy 0.935, best constant loss 0.241 nats, the binary entropy of 0.0654. (d) The baseline assumes the per-step labels are the quantity of interest and that errors at all steps cost the same. Neither holds for a trigger word: a missed word and a false wake-up have different costs, and the number of steps per word is a labelling choice. A detector should report, on real held-out clips, the fraction of spoken trigger words it detects (one minus a false-reject rate) and the number of false activations per hour of audio without the word (a false-activation rate per unit time, the deployment counterpart of the false-accept rate).

3. What the margin needs

(a) One-dimensional unnormalised encodings put an anchor at 0, a positive at 0.3 and a negative at 0.5. Compute the hinged triplet loss with \(\Delta=0.2\), then multiply the encoder by 2 and recompute. (b) Put the three encodings on the unit circle in \(\mathbb R^2\) instead, at angles 0, 0.3 and 0.5 radians. Compute the loss, and find how far the negative must be moved in angle, with the positive fixed, for the loss to vanish. (c) Construct an encoder with zero hinged loss for \(\Delta=0\) that is useless for verification.

Solution

(a) The squared distances are 0.09 and 0.25, so the bracket is \(0.09-0.25+0.2=0.04\) and the loss is 0.04. After multiplying by 2 they are 0.36 and 1.00, the bracket is \(0.36-1.00+0.2=-0.44\), and the loss is 0. The geometry is the same up to scale and nothing about the ordering improved; the loss vanished because squared distances grew by 4 while \(\Delta\) stayed fixed. This is Proposition 7(4). (b) On the unit circle, two points at angle \(\varphi\) apart have squared distance \(2-2\cos\varphi\). The squared distances are \(2-2\cos0.3=0.0893\) and \(2-2\cos0.5=0.2448\), the bracket is \(0.0893-0.2448+0.2=0.0445\), and the loss is 0.0445. No rescaling exists on the circle. The loss vanishes when \(2\cos0.3-2\cos\varphi_{\mathrm n}\ge0.2\), that is \(\cos\varphi_{\mathrm n}\le\cos0.3-0.1=0.8553\), so the negative must sit at an angle of at least 0.545 radians from the anchor. (c) An encoder that returns the same unit vector for every input gives every bracket the value \(\Delta=0\) and loss 0, and it gives every pair of photographs distance 0, so every threshold accepts everyone or no one: \(\mathrm{FAR}=1\) or \(\mathrm{FRR}=1\). This is Proposition 7(1).

4. Operating points of the oracle encoder

(a) Repeat Proposition 5 for a face block of 2 coordinates instead of 4, and show that \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)=e^{-c^2/(4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w})}\) and \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)=1-e^{-c^2/(4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}})}\). (b) With \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm b}=1\) and \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}=0.09\), find the equal error rate for 2 coordinates and compare with 4. (c) With 4 coordinates, what false-reject rate does a door pay for a false-accept rate of 1 %, and an airport for 0.1 %?

Solution

(a) With 2 coordinates, \(d^2/(2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w})\) is the sum of two squared standard normals for one person, and the argument of the proof of Proposition 5 applies with the circle of radius \(c\), whose length is proportional to \(c\): the length of a standard Gaussian vector \(\chi\) in \(\mathbb R^2\) has density \(ce^{-c^2/2}\), because \(e^{-c^2/2}\) equals 1 at 0, tends to 0 and has derivative \(-ce^{-c^2/2}\); so \(\mathbb P(\lVert\chi\rVert_2>c)=e^{-c^2/2}\). Replacing \(c\) by \(c/\sqrt{2\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w}}\) gives \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)=e^{-c^2/(4\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm w})}\); the same step with \(\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{tot}}=1.09\) and the complement gives \(\mathrm{FAR}\). (b) The equal-error threshold solves \(e^{-c^2/0.36}=1-e^{-c^2/4.36}\); bisection gives \(c=0.831\) and an equal error rate of 0.147, against 0.056 at \(c=1.287\) with 4 coordinates. More coordinates that carry identity make the genuine and impostor distances concentrate around their different means, so the distributions overlap less. (c) Solving \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)=0.01\) with the formula of Proposition 5 gives \(c=0.805\) and \(\mathrm{FRR}=0.463\); \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)=0.001\) gives \(c=0.445\) and \(\mathrm{FRR}=0.894\). The airport's threshold rejects 89 % of honest travellers with this encoder. The numbers show why the recording's remark that an airport uses a stricter threshold (≈3794–3823 s) presupposes a much better encoder, and higher resolution, than a dining hall needs.

5. Temperature, and a model that is too good at its own task

(a) A view has cosine similarity 0.8 with its partner and 0.7, 0.2 and −0.3 with three negatives. Compute the softmax weights and the gradients of \(\mathcal L_i\) with respect to the four similarities for \(\tau=1\) and \(\tau=0.1\). (b) A team's contrastive model picks the partner view correctly in 99 % of its training batches, yet its encodings verify faces barely better than chance. Using the running case, name the likely cause, a test for it, and a remedy.

Solution

(a) The weights are the softmax of the similarities divided by \(\tau\). For \(\tau=1\): \(\hat y=(0.359,0.325,0.197,0.119)\) for the partner and the three negatives; the gradients \((\hat y_{ij}-\mathbb 1[j=\mathrm{pos}(i)])/\tau\) are \(-0.641\), \(0.325\), \(0.197\), \(0.119\). For \(\tau=0.1\): \(\hat y=(0.730,0.268,0.0018,0.00001)\), and the gradients are \(-2.70\), \(2.68\), \(0.018\), \(0.0001\). At the low temperature almost all the push falls on the negative with similarity 0.7, the one most easily confused with the partner, and the other two negatives are ignored. (b) The partner task can be solved by any feature that the two views share and that differs between photographs, whether or not it has to do with identity. In the running case, when the augmentation redraws only the lighting, the scene block is such a feature: the trained encoder puts 84 % of its norm there and its equal error rate is 0.394. A test: measure verification on pairs of photographs of one person taken against different backgrounds, and compare with pairs sharing a background; a large gap indicates the shortcut. Where the encoder is inspectable, as here, measure which input coordinates carry its weight. The remedy is an augmentation that changes the shortcut feature between the two views, as SimCLR composes cropping with colour distortion because colour histograms alone identify an image (p. 5).

6. A dining-hall system with one photograph per student

A school of 5,000 students has one card photograph per student, a camera at the door, and months of unlabelled camera footage. Design the training and deployment of the verification system. State what each step can and cannot establish, and decide whether the same encoder could be used to identify students without their cards if its false-accept rate at the chosen threshold is \(10^{-4}\).

Solution

Training. The card photographs cannot train an encoder, since a triplet needs two photographs of one person. Two sources can. Public face datasets with many photographs per person support the triplet loss (slide 24); they establish good separation for the people and conditions they contain, not for this camera. The unlabelled footage supports contrastive pretraining, provided the augmentations change what varies at the door: lighting, background, framing, scale. The running case shows the risk: an augmentation that leaves the background unchanged teaches the encoder the background. Fine-tuning with triplets from the public data can follow. Threshold. Collect a modest set of labelled pairs from the door camera and card photographs, with the students' consent, and choose \(c\) from the false-accept rate the school accepts; the false-reject rate at that \(c\) is then measured, not chosen. This establishes behaviour on that sample only; chapter 3 discusses what changes after deployment, such as beards in winter. Identification. Without cards, an unenrolled visitor is compared with all 5,000 students. By Proposition 8 the probability of at least one false match is \(1-(1-10^{-4})^{5000}=0.393\), and the expected number of false matches is 0.5, under independence. A door that admits 39 % of strangers is not acceptable, so the encoder is usable with cards and not without them; identification would need a false-accept rate near \(10^{-6}\) or a second factor. Security. A backdoor in public pretrained weights or in contributed training pairs would pass all of these tests; chapter 4 treats that threat for this system.
