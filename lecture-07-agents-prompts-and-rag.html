Building a reliable application around a frozen language model

A company has access to a pretrained language model whose weights it cannot or will not change. It wants a customer-support system that reads a message such as "I need to change my shipping address for order A127. I moved to 325 Mission Street.", updates the right record, and answers the customer politely. This chapter asks two questions about such a system. What can be changed around the fixed model — the instructions, the examples in its input, the documents it reads, the tools it calls, the number of calls, the number of cooperating programs, and, if the fixed-weight constraint is relaxed, the weights — and what does each change assume? And how does one measure whether the resulting system works, when parts of its output can only be judged by a person or by another model?

The answers in this chapter are mostly engineering practice, and the chapter keeps them graded as practice. Four pieces admit mathematics, and each is derived here: retrieval by embedding similarity, and the recall with which it finds a relevant document; the probability that a chain of model and tool calls succeeds end to end, with and without an independence assumption; the latency of a chain; and the error of a language model used as a judge, measured against human labels by chance-corrected agreement and propagated into a comparison of two prompts.

A frozen model, and what can change around it 0:05

Provenance. Lecture: the plan of the lecture, the audience's list of limitations of a base model, the claim that language models are difficult to control, the knowledge, style, task and context gaps of slide 4 (≈5–693 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the running case as a formal chain, the formal model and its notation, Figure 1.

A language model in this chapter is the next-token model of chapter 2 at a much larger scale. Its input \(x\) is a sequence of tokens from a vocabulary \(\mathcal V\), and it defines a conditional distribution \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\) over output sequences \(y\) by predicting one token at a time. The parameters \(\theta\) were set during pretraining and later tuning by someone else. A base model, or foundation model, is such a model used as delivered. The model reads at most \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}\) tokens at once; \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}\) is its context window, and every token of instructions, examples, documents, conversation history and user text must fit inside it.

The recording opens by asking what goes wrong when a base model is used directly (≈219–471 s). The audience's answers, with the lecturer's additions, form a list of gaps. The model may lack the knowledge of a narrow domain, such as images of diseased crops that no public dataset contains. Deployment data may differ from training data. Its training data cannot include later events, so answering about them may require information supplied in the input or by a tool; a fixed model can still use that new information without changing its parameters. A general model may be less precise than a narrow application requires. And the model may be far larger than the task needs. Slide 4 groups the gaps into four kinds with one example each: missing domain knowledge (medical guidelines), inconsistent style or format (a legal clause), task-specific understanding (review categories that differ by industry), and limited context (a document of 100,000 words).

The lecturer adds that language models are difficult to control and gives two public examples (≈471–577 s). Microsoft's Twitter bot of March 2016 learned from users, produced racist posts, and was removed, in the recording's account, 16 hours after launch; slide 3 cites a New York Times report for the episode (product claim: press report, not checked here). A 2024 exchange between two company heads about political bias in each other's assistants is offered as evidence that well-funded teams also fail to control their models (speaker judgment, ≈526–577 s). Neither example measures controllability. They motivate the question the chapter answers: when the weights are fixed, which inputs and which surrounding program make the model's behaviour acceptable, and how is "acceptable" measured?

The running case: a customer-support chain

The recording reaches its main case study near the end (slides 32–35, ≈5132–5660 s). This chapter introduces it first, because every mechanism below changes one part of it. A customer writes: "I need to change my shipping address for order A127. I moved to 325 Mission Street." Slide 33 designs the system as four steps. A language-model call extracts the intent, the order identifier and the new address. A tool queries the company's customer-relationship database (CRM), finds the record and updates the address. A second model call drafts a confirmation email. A second tool sends it. Slide 35 gives five illustrative failures of such a system; the first is an agent that replied "We've updated your address for order #A172" to a request about order #A127.

Figure 1 fixes the objects that the chapter reuses. The system is a chain of \(n_{\mathrm{stage}}\) stages. Each stage is either a call to the fixed model \(\pi_\theta\) with an assembled input, or a call to a deterministic program, a tool, such as a database query. The final output \(y\) is the email that reaches the customer. Two numbers describe each stage: \(p_j\), the probability that stage \(j\) produces a correct output when its inputs are correct, and \(t_{\mathrm{lat},j}\), its latency in seconds. The values in the figure are illustrative choices of this book, used to make the arithmetic concrete; the email step's 5 seconds is the recording's example of a slow component.

The formal model

For one model call, the input is a concatenation of parts, written with \(\oplus\):

\[x=x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{shot}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{doc}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{hist}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{user}},\qquad \lvert x\rvert\le n_{\mathrm{ctx}},\]

where \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\) is the builder's fixed instructions, \(x_{\mathrm{shot}}\) worked examples, \(x_{\mathrm{doc}}\) retrieved documents or tool results, \(x_{\mathrm{hist}}\) earlier turns of the conversation, \(x_{\mathrm{user}}\) the customer's text, and \(\lvert x\rvert\) the number of tokens. Any part may be empty. The chapter's mechanisms act on distinct parts of this model:

A context budget

Whatever \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}\) is, the parts of \(x\) compete for it. Suppose the template and the user text take \(n_{\mathrm{fix}}\) tokens, the answer needs \(n_{\mathrm{out}}\) tokens (for models that count the output inside the window), and each few-shot example or retrieved chunk costs \(n_{\mathrm{per}}\) tokens. Then the number of examples or chunks that fit is

\[n_{\mathrm{shot}}\le\Bigl\lfloor\frac{n_{\mathrm{ctx}}-n_{\mathrm{fix}}-n_{\mathrm{out}}}{n_{\mathrm{per}}}\Bigr\rfloor .\]

Brown et al. (2020) state the same constraint for GPT-3: they set the number of examples "in the range of 10 to 100 as this is how many examples can fit in the model's context window (nctx = 2048)" (p. 6). With \(n_{\mathrm{ctx}}=2048\), and taking as an assumption of this example \(n_{\mathrm{fix}}=200\), \(n_{\mathrm{out}}=100\) and \(n_{\mathrm{per}}=40\), the bound is \(\lfloor1748/40\rfloor=43\) examples (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The inequality is the reason every mechanism of this chapter that adds text to \(x\) must also decide what to leave out.

Fitting is not using

A larger window does not guarantee that the model uses what it reads. The recording describes the needle-in-a-haystack test (≈806–863 s): insert one sentence stating a fact, the needle, at some depth of a long text, then ask about the fact. The test is a practice maintained as an open-source repository by Gregory Kamradt, which sweeps "context length × needle depth" (product claim: repository description, README read 2026-09-28). A controlled study of the same question is Liu et al. (2023). They placed the one document that answers a question among 10, 20 or 30 retrieved documents and moved it through the input. Accuracy was "highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context" and degraded "when models must access relevant information in the middle of long contexts, even for explicitly long-context models" (p. 1). For GPT-3.5-Turbo in the 20- and 30-document settings, the worst position gave lower accuracy than answering with no documents at all, whose accuracy was 56.1% (p. 5; empirical result for that model and task). The consequence for the formal model is that the order of the parts of \(x\) is itself a design variable.

Why prompting skill is argued to matter

The recording motivates prompt engineering with a field experiment on consultants (≈1085–1289 s). Dell'Acqua et al.'s working paper, dated September 22, 2023, assigns participants to no AI, GPT-4 alone, or GPT-4 with a prompt-engineering overview (PDF pp. 11–12). On the selected inside-frontier tasks, both AI arms improve quality and completion relative to control. The overview arm has higher quality point estimates, but the comparison with GPT-4 alone depends on the outcome and specification: Table 1's human-rated-quality comparisons have p-values 0.029, 0.053 and 0.047, whereas Table 2 finds no statistically significant completion difference between the AI arms (PDF pp. 38–39). These empirical results do not establish that training makes a group universally best, as the recording's summary might suggest.

For the selected outside-frontier task, the approximately 19-percentage-point correctness decrease pools the two AI arms relative to control (PDF p. 18). Table 4 reports a control mean of 0.844 and, in its first specification, coefficients of −0.245 for GPT + Overview and −0.139 for GPT Only; the between-arm p-values are 0.082, 0.095 and 0.088 (PDF p. 41). Thus the overview arm's correctness point estimate is lower here, and these comparisons do not establish a difference at the conventional 5% threshold. The result concerns this deliberately selected task and GPT-4 version, not every use of AI. The recording's account of over-reliance, which the lecturer calls falling asleep at the wheel, remains his interpretation (≈1171–1229 s). Slide 8 defines centaurs as users who divide work between themselves and the model, and cyborgs as users who interact with the model throughout. The paper discusses these qualitative collaboration styles (PDF p. 20 and Appendix E, pp. 55–56); they are not a universal ranking of users. The lecturer's view that prompting is a skill everyone should have rather than a career is speaker judgment (≈1289–1307 s).

Instructions: changing \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\)

Slide 9 starts from the instruction "Summarize this document" and lists what it leaves out: the type of document, the length and form of the summary, and the audience. The improved instruction names all three: a 10-page scientific paper on renewable energy, five bullet points, key findings and implications for policymakers (≈1309–1359 s). In the formal model this is a change to \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\) only. It adds constraints that a correct output must satisfy, so that more of the output is determined by the instruction and less by the model's guess about the user's intent.

The audience adds two variants (≈1359–1450 s). A role prompt begins "act like" an expert; the lecturer reports that such prompts work (speaker judgment, ≈1392–1425 s; no measurement is given). Self-critique, or reflection, asks the model for an output, then asks it to criticise that output, then asks for a revision; the lecturer calls it probably the most effective of the techniques named so far (speaker judgment, ≈1425–1450 s). Reflection uses more than one model call, so it is a chain in the sense of the next section, not a change to a single \(x\).

Step-by-step instructions and chain-of-thought prompting

Slide 9's final prompt adds "Approach this task step by step, and do not skip any step" and three numbered steps: identify the three most important findings, explain their policy impact, write the five bullets. The slide and the recording attribute the technique to Wei et al. and date it 2023 (≈1450–1495 s). The paper is Wei et al., "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models"; its arXiv identifier dates the first version to January 2022, it appeared at NeurIPS 2022, and the version read for this chapter is the sixth, dated 10 January 2023 (p. 1), which may explain the slide's year.

The paper's method differs from the slide's prompt. A chain of thought is "a series of intermediate reasoning steps", and chain-of-thought prompting provides "a few chain of thought demonstrations ... as exemplars" (p. 1): worked examples of question, reasoning and answer placed in \(x_{\mathrm{shot}}\). For grade-school arithmetic the authors used one set of eight such exemplars (p. 4). The slide's prompt instead places an instruction and an outline of steps in \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\), with no worked example. The two share the idea that the model should write its intermediate steps, but Wei et al. measured the first, and their results say nothing directly about the second. What they did measure bounds the claim. Chain-of-thought prompting "does not positively impact performance for small models, and only yields performance gains when used with models of ∼100B parameters" (p. 4), and with eight exemplars a 540-billion-parameter model reached "state-of-the-art accuracy" on the GSM8K benchmark of arithmetic word problems (p. 1) (empirical results for those models and benchmarks). The gain is a property of a model scale and a task family, not of the prompt alone.

Templates: \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\) as a function

Slide 10 defines a prompt template as "a pre-defined structure with placeholders for dynamic inputs", for example "Summarize the following text for [audience] in [format]: [text]". Formally, a template is a function from a record of fields to a string, and the application calls it once per request. The recording's example comes from the lecturer's company, Workera: fields from a human-resources record (name, role, level, country, preferred language) fill one template for every user, so a person whose preferred language is Spanish is addressed in Spanish without a separately written prompt (≈1525–1600 s). Asked whether hosted assistants use such templates, the lecturer answers that they probably place a hidden system prompt and stored user memories before the user's text, and says this is not public (speaker judgment, ≈1600–1668 s). A template is also the first place where untrusted text enters the model's input: \(x_{\mathrm{user}}\) is inserted verbatim beside the builder's instructions, which is the channel that chapter 4's prompt-injection section analyses.

Zero-shot and few-shot prompts: changing \(x_{\mathrm{shot}}\)

A zero-shot prompt states the task with no worked example; a few-shot prompt adds \(n_{\mathrm{shot}}\ge1\) examples of inputs with their desired outputs. In the formal model the two predictions are

\[\pi_\theta\bigl(y\mid x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{user}}\bigr)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\pi_\theta\bigl(y\mid x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{shot}}\oplus x_{\mathrm{user}}\bigr),\]

with the same \(\theta\). Brown et al. (2020) define the few-shot setting in exactly this way: the model "is given a few demonstrations of the task at inference time as conditioning ..., but no weight updates are allowed" (p. 6), and they applied GPT-3 to all tasks "without any gradient updates or fine-tuning" (p. 1). Slide 11 shows the effect on one sentence. Asked to classify "The product is fine, but I was expecting more." as positive, negative or neutral, the zero-shot prompt returns "Neutral". With three labelled examples added, one of which labels "It's okay, but I wish it had more features." as negative, the output becomes "Negative" (≈1668–1790 s). The slide shows one output of one unnamed model on one sentence. It illustrates that examples can move a borderline case toward the labeller's convention; it is not a measurement of how often they do, and the lecturer's attribution of the flip to the second example is an interpretation of that one output.

The recording draws a practical consequence (≈1790–1837 s). A team can collect user inputs that the model handled badly, have a person label them, and add them to \(x_{\mathrm{shot}}\). This is a dataset, but one that lives in the input instead of in the weights. Changing it takes effect on the next call, and replacing the model with a newer one keeps it. Its limit is the context budget of the previous section: every example costs \(n_{\mathrm{per}}\) tokens, and the number that fits is bounded.

Long conversations

Asked how long a prompt can grow before the model stops following instructions, the lecturer answers that published measurements age quickly and gives an observation from Workera's voice product: after eight turns of conversation, each turn pasted into the next prompt, the model "loses itself" (speaker judgment, ≈1837–1927 s; no measurement or model is named). The remedy described is to split a conversation into chapters, summarise the earlier chapter, and start a new prompt with the summary in \(x_{\mathrm{hist}}\). The same mechanism appears in Packer et al.'s MemGPT, where the first entry of the message queue holds "a recursive summary of messages that have been evicted from the queue" (p. 2). Both replace a growing \(x_{\mathrm{hist}}\) by a bounded one; both lose whatever the summary omits.

Chains: several calls instead of one, and how to test them 32:13

Provenance. Lecture: prompt chaining as distinct from chain of thought, the customer-review example of slides 12–13, intermediate outputs as the reason to chain, latency as its cost, testing prompts with human ratings and with a testing tool (slide 14), and four kinds of LLM judge (slide 15) (≈1933–2475 s). Research extension: the judge variants and their definitions in Zheng et al. Original construction: Proposition 1 and its bounds, the correlated-failure computation, the latency sum, the sampling-error remark on slide 14's pass counts.

The recording separates two ideas with similar names (≈1933–1961 s). Chain of thought is one prompt that asks the model to write out intermediate reasoning. Prompt chaining is several model calls, where the output of one call is pasted into the input of the next. Slide 12's example answers a customer who received a laptop three days late in damaged packaging. The single-call version asks for a professional response that acknowledges the concern, explains the issue and offers a resolution. The chained version uses three calls: extract the key issues from the review; turn the issues into an outline of a response; write the response from the outline. Slide 13 draws the general pattern: prompt \(j\) contains task \(j\) and the previous output.

The lecturer claims performance gains, without reporting a measurement, and stresses that the intermediate outputs exist and can be inspected (≈1990–2231 s). With one call, a thumbs-down from a user says that the final email was bad, not why. With three calls, a reviewer can inspect the extracted issues, the outline and the final writing to locate errors and choose a prompt to investigate. These observations describe what each stage did on the input it actually received. After an earlier error, they need not reveal how a later stage would have behaved on a correct input. Estimating component reliability on correct reference inputs therefore requires separate tests, including requests whose live prefixes failed. The model below keeps those test outcomes distinct from live-call observations. The stated cost is latency: each additional call adds its own response time, which matters for interactive applications (≈2233–2261 s).

When does a chain succeed?

Chaining makes the question "how reliable is the whole system?" a question about its parts. Draw a request from a specified population and fix its correct reference output at each stage. Include any randomness used by the stages in the experiment. For stage \(j\), let \(E_j\) be the event that it returns its correct reference output when tested on the correct reference input. Two assumptions connect these component-test events to the chain. (A1) No recovery: any incorrect intermediate output makes the final result incorrect, and a chain of correct outputs succeeds. (A2) Isolation: on the same request and with the same randomness, a stage reached through a correct prefix returns the same output as its test on the reference input. Under these assumptions the chain succeeds exactly on \(E_1\cap\dots\cap E_{n_{\mathrm{stage}}}\). Write \(p_j=\mathbb P(E_j)\), the marginal success rate estimated by component tests over that same request population. Figure 1's "correct inputs" means reference inputs, not selection of only those requests where earlier stages happened to succeed.

Proposition 1 (success of a chain). Under (A1) and (A2), with \(\mathbb P(\text{success})=\mathbb P(E_1\cap\dots\cap E_{n_{\mathrm{stage}}})\):

(a) \(\mathbb P(\text{success})=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{stage}}}\mathbb P(E_j\mid E_1\cap\dots\cap E_{j-1})\) when each conditioning prefix has positive probability; if a prefix has probability zero, the whole intersection has probability zero;

(b) if the events \(E_1,\dots,E_{n_{\mathrm{stage}}}\) are independent, \(\mathbb P(\text{success})=\prod_{j}p_j\);

(c) whatever the dependence, \(\max\bigl(0,\,1-\sum_j(1-p_j)\bigr)\le\mathbb P(\text{success})\le\min_j p_j\).

Proof. (a) is the chain rule of conditional probability applied to the intersection, one event at a time. (b) Under independence each conditional probability in (a) equals the unconditional \(p_j\). (c) The intersection is contained in each \(E_j\), so its probability is at most every \(p_j\). Its complement is the union of the complements \(E_j^{\mathrm c}\), whose probability is at most \(\sum_j\mathbb P(E_j^{\mathrm c})=\sum_j(1-p_j)\) by the union bound; subtracting from 1 gives the lower bound, and a probability is never negative. \(\blacksquare\)

Part (a) needs no independence, but its factors are not what an unselected component test measures. Testing the drafting stage on correct reference inputs for all sampled requests estimates \(p_3=\mathbb P(E_3)\). Part (a) instead needs \(\mathbb P(E_3\mid E_1\cap E_2)\), the drafting success among requests on which extraction and the CRM stage succeeded. Component tests and live traces can therefore describe different request subsets. Under mutual independence every factor equals its marginal, which gives part (b); part (c) requires only the marginal rates and (A1)–(A2).

The running case gives numbers. With the illustrative rates of Figure 1, \((p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)=(0.95,0.99,0.93,0.995)\), the independent product is \(0.95\times0.99\times0.93\times0.995=0.8703\); the bounds of (c) are \(1-(0.05+0.01+0.07+0.005)=0.865\) and \(\min_jp_j=0.93\) (engineering check; the code below asserts both). Now let the failures share a cause. Suppose 7% of messages are hard, for example because they mention two order numbers, and on those messages drafting always fails and extraction fails with probability \(5/7\), while every other message is handled correctly by both. The marginal rates are unchanged: drafting succeeds on \(0.93\) of messages, and extraction on \(0.93+0.07\times2/7=0.95\). Keep the CRM and email success events independent of each other and of the hard-message mechanism. The joint event that extraction and drafting succeed consists exactly of the 93% of messages that are not hard, so the chain succeeds with probability \(0.93\times0.99\times0.995=0.9161\), higher than the independent product by \(0.046\) (engineering check; a simulation of 200,000 messages in the code below gives 0.9163). In this example the failures fall on the same messages, and the true rate lies above \(\prod_jp_j\); exercise 3 constructs a dependence that lowers it to the union bound, so the product is not a bound in either direction.

An extraction error propagated through the database update into the email, which is (A1). Proposition 1 describes a chain without such checks. Latency adds along a chain

If the stages run one after another, the chain's latency is the sum of the stage latencies,

\[t_{\mathrm{lat}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{stage}}}t_{\mathrm{lat},j},\]

which is \(1.2+0.3+2.5+5.0=9.0\) s for Figure 1 (engineering check). Replacing one call by three sequential calls multiplies the fixed overhead of a call by three, whatever the length of each output. The sum also shows where to optimise: the recording's example of a slow component is a 5-second email step (≈5573–5597 s), which here is 56% of the total. Parallel branches, discussed in the multi-agent section, replace a sum by a maximum.

Fine-tuning: changing the weights 41:15

Provenance. Lecture: the limitations and uses of fine-tuning on slide 17, the lecturer's preference against it, the Slack fine-tuning story of slide 18 (≈2475–2691 s). Research extension: the blog post behind slide 18. Original construction: the fine-tuning objective in the chapter's notation and the reading of the Slack story as distribution matching.

Fine-tuning changes the one object the other mechanisms leave fixed. Starting from \(\theta\), it minimises the next-token cross-entropy of chapter 2 on a new dataset \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ft}}\) of input–output pairs, and yields new parameters \(\theta_{\mathrm{ft}}\):

\[\theta_{\mathrm{ft}}\approx\arg\min_{\theta}\ -\frac1{\lvert\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ft}}\rvert}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ft}}}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x),\quad\text{found by gradient descent started at the pretrained }\theta .\]

The approximation sign marks that training stops after a finite number of steps. A few-shot prompt conditions the old model on examples at every call; fine-tuning stores the examples' effect in the weights, so the examples no longer occupy the context window.

Slide 17 lists three limitations: fine-tuning needs substantial labelled data; the tuned model may overfit to its data and lose general-purpose ability; and it costs time and money, especially when the base model is updated often. It lists two uses: tasks that require repeated high-precision outputs such as legal writing or scientific explanation, and domains whose language the general model handles poorly. The lecturer states his own preference against fine-tuning: by the time a team has fine-tuned a model, a newer base model is available and outperforms the tuned older one, while a prompt-based system can switch to the newer model by changing one name in the code (speaker judgment, ≈2475–2584 s). The same passage says that newer methods make fine-tuning look more like few-shot prompting (speaker judgment, ≈2504–2532 s; no method is named). The preference is an economic argument about the rate of model releases. It does not say that fine-tuning fails.

Slide 18 shows the example the lecturer uses (≈2584–2690 s). In a blog post of 4 September 2023, Ross Lazerowitz describes fine-tuning GPT-3.5-turbo on a dump of 140,000 Slack messages accumulated over four years at his company, after prompts had failed to reproduce his writing style (product claim: blog post, read 2026-09-28). The slide's screenshot shows the result. Asked to "write a 500 word blog post on prompt engineering", the tuned model answered that it would work on it in the morning, and when pressed it said it did not know what to write and could only describe the process. This is one anecdote about one tuned model. Read through the objective above, it shows what fine-tuning optimises: the likelihood of the data. The data were workplace chat, where answering a request with a promise to do it later is an ordinary move, and the tuned model answered the request in that way (this book's reading of one anecdote; the data were not examined). The builder wanted the style of the messages and received their behaviour as well. Choosing \(\mathcal D_{\mathrm{ft}}\) is choosing the task.

The pipeline of slide 21

Slide 21 draws the pipeline for the question "What are the side effects of drug X?" (≈2846–3017 s). Before any question arrives, every document in a knowledge base of medical documents is passed through an embedding model, and the resulting vectors are stored in a vector database, a store built for fast nearest-neighbour search; this is indexing. When a question arrives, it is embedded with the same model, the stored vectors nearest to it are found, and the corresponding documents are retrieved. The documents are pasted into a template, "Answer <user query>, based on the <doc>. If answer not in the document, say 'I don't know.'", and the fixed model generates the answer. The recording adds that the template can also ask for the page, chapter and line of the supporting passage (≈2982–3017 s).

In the chapter's notation, the corpus \(\mathcal C\) holds \(N_{\mathrm{doc}}\) documents \(\mathrm{doc}_1,\dots,\mathrm{doc}_{N_{\mathrm{doc}}}\). An embedding model \(f_{\mathrm{emb}}\) maps any text to a vector in \(\mathbb R^{n_e}\); it is a separate network from \(\pi_\theta\), and in the lecture's system it is also fixed. The score of document \(j\) for the user text \(x_{\mathrm{user}}\) is the cosine similarity of chapter 2, defined only when both encodings are nonzero,

\[\cos\bigl(f_{\mathrm{emb}}(x_{\mathrm{user}}),f_{\mathrm{emb}}(\mathrm{doc}_j)\bigr)=\frac{f_{\mathrm{emb}}(x_{\mathrm{user}})^\top f_{\mathrm{emb}}(\mathrm{doc}_j)}{\lVert f_{\mathrm{emb}}(x_{\mathrm{user}})\rVert_2\,\lVert f_{\mathrm{emb}}(\mathrm{doc}_j)\rVert_2},\]

and retrieval returns the \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) documents with the highest scores; \(x_{\mathrm{doc}}\) is their concatenation. The recording speaks of "the distance between those embeddings" (≈2924–2982 s) and names no formula; the cosine, and the proposition below that connects it to distance, are this book's additions. The recording also connects the document embeddings to the triplet-loss embeddings of chapter 2 (≈2874–2904 s). The geometry is the same: an embedding is an encoding in which distance carries meaning. The training signal differs, and so does the meaning of "close": same person there, relevant document here. The lecturer's remark that too small a dimension \(n_e\) loses information and too large a dimension adds latency states a trade-off without numbers (speaker judgment, ≈2874–2904 s).

Write \(\phi_{\mathrm q}=f_{\mathrm{emb}}(x_{\mathrm{user}})\) for the query's encoding and \(\phi_j=f_{\mathrm{emb}}(\mathrm{doc}_j)\) for the encoding of document \(j\).

Proposition 2 (cosine and Euclidean rankings agree on unit vectors). If \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}\rVert_2=\lVert\phi_j\rVert_2=1\), then \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}-\phi_j\rVert_2^2=2-2\cos(\phi_{\mathrm q},\phi_j)\). Consequently, for a unit-norm query vector and unit-norm document vectors, sorting documents by decreasing cosine similarity and sorting them by increasing Euclidean distance give the same order.

Proof. \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}-\phi_j\rVert_2^2=\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}\rVert_2^2+\lVert\phi_j\rVert_2^2-2\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_j=2-2\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_j\), and \(\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_j=\cos(\phi_{\mathrm q},\phi_j)\) because both norms are 1. Squared distance is therefore a strictly decreasing function of the cosine, so it reverses the order of scores, and the square root that turns squared distance into distance is increasing. \(\blacksquare\)

The proposition is why "distance" and "similarity" can be used interchangeably for normalised embeddings, and why a system that normalises its vectors can hand the search to any Euclidean nearest-neighbour index. Without normalisation the three common scores differ. Karpukhin et al. (2020), whose dense passage retriever (DPR) is the retriever of the original RAG model, score a passage by the plain inner product of the two encodings (p. 3, eq. 1) and note that "cosine is equivalent to inner product for unit vectors" (p. 3). In their ablation, Euclidean distance performed comparably to the inner product and both were better than cosine for their trained encoders (p. 6; empirical result for their training setup). Exercise 1 constructs vectors for which inner-product and distance rankings disagree.

Cost of exact search

Exact retrieval compares the query vector with every stored vector. Each cosine of unit vectors is one inner product of length \(n_e\), so one query costs \(N_{\mathrm{doc}}\,n_e\) multiply-adds, plus the selection of the \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) largest scores. DPR's Wikipedia index held about 21 million passages (p. 7) encoded in \(n_e=768\) dimensions (p. 3, where the paper writes the dimension as \(d\)), so an exact search costs about \(21\times10^6\times768=1.6\times10^{10}\) multiply-adds per question (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The authors instead indexed the vectors with the FAISS similarity-search library and report 995 questions per second, each returning the top 100 passages, on one CPU server (p. 7; empirical result for their hardware). Lewis et al. (2020) describe top-k retrieval (their name for returning the \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) highest scores) as a maximum-inner-product search problem "which can be approximately solved in sub-linear time" (p. 3). An approximate index can return a set that differs from the exact top \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\), which is one more reason to measure retrieval quality directly.

Measuring retrieval: Recall@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\)

Retrieval can be measured only against labels. Take a set of \(n_{\mathrm{qry}}\) test queries and, for each query \(x\), a person's label \(j_{\mathrm{rel}}(x)\), the index of the document that answers it. Then

\[\mathrm{Recall@}n_{\mathrm{nb}}=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{qry}}}\sum_{x}\mathbb 1\bigl[\,j_{\mathrm{rel}}(x)\in\text{top-}n_{\mathrm{nb}}(x)\,\bigr],\]

the fraction of queries whose relevant document is among the \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) retrieved. Because the top-\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) set only grows with \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\), Recall@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) is non-decreasing in \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\), and it equals 1 at \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}=N_{\mathrm{doc}}\). Larger \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) raises recall and spends context budget: each retrieved document costs its tokens in \(x\), and Liu et al.'s position effect makes a long \(x_{\mathrm{doc}}\) harder to use. Karpukhin et al. define the same quantity, "top-k retrieval accuracy", as "the fraction of questions for which" the retrieved set "contains a span that answers the question" (p. 2); on the Natural Questions test set their retriever reached 78.4% at \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}=20\) against 59.1% for the keyword method BM25 (p. 5, Table 2; empirical result).

A small corpus makes every number checkable. The code below builds an original eight-document corpus: three documents about drug X (side effects, dosage, interactions), one about drug X storage, one about a drug Y, and three customer-support policies (refunds, address changes, subscriptions). Six test queries each carry one relevance label. The embedding is a stand-in for a learned model: a unit-normalised count of the corpus words each text contains, after removing common function words. Score, relevance, correctness and faithfulness

The drug-Y case separates four quantities that the phrase "grounded answer" runs together:

QuantityDefined byHow it is measured
scorethe cosine of the query and document encodingscomputed exactly by the retriever
relevancewhether the document answers the querya person's label; Recall@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) summarises it
correctnesswhether the final answer \(y\) is truea reference answer, a person, or a judge
faithfulnesswhether every claim in \(y\) is supported by the retrieved documentsa person or a judge reading \(y\) beside \(x_{\mathrm{doc}}\)

Each can hold while the next fails. A high score can attach to an irrelevant document, as the drug-Y document shows. A relevant document can be present and the answer still be wrong: in Liu et al.'s experiment the answering document was always among the 20 or 30 in the input, yet GPT-3.5-Turbo's accuracy at the worst position fell below its accuracy with no documents (p. 5). A correct answer can be unfaithful, if the model answers from its own training rather than from the retrieved text. A faithful answer can be incorrect, if the document is outdated. The template's instruction "If answer not in the document, say 'I don't know'" asks the model for faithfulness; it does not guarantee it, because the fixed model decides whether to follow it. Slide 20's "ensures answers are more accurate" is therefore a claim about correctness that no quantity computed by the retriever can establish.

Memory as a latency and budget decision

The recording's distinction between core (working) and archival memory is about access cost (≈4042–4146 s). A user's name is needed in every turn, so it belongs in fast working memory; a birthday is rarely needed and can sit in slower archival memory. Because the working memory is read on every turn, its cost recurs: if a memory lookup took 3 seconds, every turn would take 3 seconds longer (the recording's illustrative figure). In the formal model, working memory is part of \(x_{\mathrm{hist}}\) or \(x_{\mathrm{tmpl}}\) on every call, costing tokens from the budget and any lookup latency on every turn; an archival fact costs a lookup of latency \(t_{\mathrm{lat,arch}}\) only on the turns that need it. If a fact is needed on a fraction \(p_{\mathrm{need}}\) of turns, keeping it archival adds \(p_{\mathrm{need}}\,t_{\mathrm{lat,arch}}\) seconds per turn on average, and keeping it in working memory adds its tokens to every call. For a fact needed on 2% of turns with a 1-second archival lookup, the average cost is 0.02 s per turn (engineering check by shown arithmetic; both numbers are assumptions of the example).

The tier names come from research on the same problem. Packer et al. (MemGPT) divide memory into main context, "the LLM prompt tokens", and external context held outside the window (p. 2). Main context contains the system instructions, a writable "working context" and a queue of recent messages; external context contains "recall storage" of past messages and "archival storage", "a read/write database storing arbitrary length text objects", and the model moves data between the tiers by function calls (p. 3). The slide's archival memory matches MemGPT's archival storage; its core memory corresponds to MemGPT's working context inside the prompt.

Degrees of autonomy

Slide 28 names three degrees of autonomy (≈4217–4335 s). The least autonomous agent has hard-coded steps and hard-coded tools: identify the intent, look up the customer's history, call the flight API, and so on, in a fixed order. A semi-autonomous agent has hard-coded tools but chooses which to use and when. A more autonomous agent chooses its steps and can create tools, for example by writing and running code to compute distances between airports and hotels. These are design categories. They are not measured capability levels, and the slide assigns no reliability to any of them.

In the formal model the degree of autonomy decides who fixes the chain. The running case of slide 33 is at the least autonomous level: four stages in a fixed order, so Proposition 1 applies with \(n_{\mathrm{stage}}=4\). When the model chooses its next step, the number and kind of stages vary from input to input, and any application of Proposition 1 must condition on a path and verify (A1)–(A2) for it. Selecting a path can change its stage-success rates, so unconditioned component rates cannot simply be reused. An evaluation must sample complete requests and their paths. The research form of a model that chooses its steps is ReAct (Yao et al., 2022), which prompts a model "to generate both reasoning traces and task-specific actions in an interleaved manner", so that the reasoning updates the plan and the actions "gather additional information from external sources" (p. 1). Measuring a judge against people

Let a person and a judge each label the same \(n_{\mathrm{item}}\) traces as pass or fail. The observed agreement \(p_o\) is the fraction of traces on which the two labels match. Agreement can be high by chance: if most traces pass, a judge that always says "pass" agrees with the person on most traces without reading any. Let \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\) be the fraction the person labels "pass" and \(p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}\) the fraction the judge labels "pass". The chance agreement is \(p_e=p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\,p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}})(1-p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}})\), and Cohen's kappa is

\[\kappa=\frac{p_o-p_e}{1-p_e},\qquad p_e\lt 1 .\]

The statistic is named after Cohen (1960). The chapter uses only the definition above and proves the properties it needs.

For Proposition 3, read \(p_o\), \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}\) as probabilities over one trace drawn at random from the population of traces; on a finite set the fractions estimate them.

Proposition 3 (what \(\kappa\) measures). (a) If the judge's label is independent of the person's label, then \(p_o=p_e\) and \(\kappa=0\). (b) \(\kappa=1\) exactly when \(p_o=1\). (c) A judge that labels every trace "pass" has \(p_o=p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\) and \(\kappa=0\), whatever \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\) is, provided \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\lt 1\).

Proof. (a) Under independence, the probability that both say "pass" is the product \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\,p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}\) of the two pass probabilities, and likewise for "fail"; the probability of agreement is the sum of the two products, which is \(p_e\). So \(p_o=p_e\), and the numerator of \(\kappa\) is 0. (b) The numerator equals the denominator exactly when \(p_o=1\). (c) With \(p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}=1\), \(p_e=p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\cdot1+(1-p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}})\cdot0=p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}\), and the judge agrees exactly on the traces the person passes, so \(p_o=p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}=p_e\). \(\blacksquare\)

On a finite sample, independence of the two labellers in the population does not force the observed \(\kappa\) to be near zero. For example, two independent fair-coin labellers can both produce the labels pass, fail on two traces; then \(p_o=1\), \(p_e=1/2\), and the sample \(\kappa=1\). The sample statistic can also equal zero exactly, or be undefined when the sample gives \(p_e=1\). Under independent, identically distributed sampling from a fixed population, the observed agreement and pass fractions converge to their population probabilities. If the population \(p_e<1\), substituting these limits in the ratio shows that sample \(\kappa\) converges to zero when the population labels are independent. A finite sample still needs an uncertainty assessment, especially when chance agreement is close to one.

A worked table shows why the correction matters. On 100 traces a person marks 90 polite and 10 impolite. The judge marks 84 of the 90 polite traces "pass" and 6 "fail", and 3 of the 10 impolite traces "pass" and 7 "fail". Then \(p_o=(84+7)/100=0.91\), \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}=0.90\), \(p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}=(84+3)/100=0.87\), \(p_e=0.90\times0.87+0.10\times0.13=0.796\), and \(\kappa=(0.91-0.796)/(1-0.796)=0.559\) (engineering check; the code below recomputes it). By Proposition 3(c), a judge that never reads the traces would score \(p_o=0.90\): the real judge's 91% agreement is one point above that. Seen from the failures, the judge catches 7 of the 10 impolite traces and wrongly flags 6 of the 90 polite ones. For an eval whose purpose is to find impolite replies, those two numbers, and \(\kappa\), describe the instrument; 91% does not.

Zheng et al. (2023) measured judges this way on pairwise comparisons of chatbot answers. They define agreement as "the probability of randomly selected individuals ... of each type agreeing on a randomly selected question" (p. 7), an observed-agreement statistic. Excluding ties, GPT-4's agreement with human experts reached 85%, above the 81% agreement among the experts themselves (p. 7), against a stated random baseline of 50% (p. 8; empirical result on their MT-bench data). The 50% baseline is the agreement of a judge that picks uniformly at random; the chance agreement \(p_e\) of Proposition 3 uses the labellers' actual rates instead, and the two coincide only when one of the rates is one half.

A noisy judge in a comparison of two prompts

The recording's use of judges is comparative: which model, or which prompt wording, is better. Let prompts A and B be run on the same inputs. On each input a person would prefer A with probability \(p_{\mathrm A}\), with no ties. The judge picks the output the person would prefer with probability \(p_{\mathrm{judge}}\), independently of the input and of which prompt produced which output.

Proposition 4 (judge error shrinks a comparison). Under these assumptions the probability that the judge prefers A is

\[p_{\mathrm A}^{\mathrm{judge}}=p_{\mathrm A}\,p_{\mathrm{judge}}+(1-p_{\mathrm A})(1-p_{\mathrm{judge}})=\tfrac12+(2p_{\mathrm{judge}}-1)\bigl(p_{\mathrm A}-\tfrac12\bigr).\]

Proof. The judge prefers A either when the person prefers A and the judge is right, probability \(p_{\mathrm A}p_{\mathrm{judge}}\), or when the person prefers B and the judge is wrong, probability \((1-p_{\mathrm A})(1-p_{\mathrm{judge}})\); the two cases are disjoint. Expanding, \(p_{\mathrm A}p_{\mathrm{judge}}+1-p_{\mathrm A}-p_{\mathrm{judge}}+p_{\mathrm A}p_{\mathrm{judge}}=\tfrac12+(2p_{\mathrm{judge}}-1)(p_{\mathrm A}-\tfrac12)\), which is checked by expanding the right-hand side. \(\blacksquare\)

Figure 2 shows the three consequences. For \(1/2\lt p_{\mathrm{judge}}\lt1\), the judge keeps the direction of the comparison and shrinks its margin \(p_{\mathrm A}-1/2\) by \(2p_{\mathrm{judge}}-1\). A perfect judge preserves the margin, a judge at \(p_{\mathrm{judge}}=1/2\) carries no information, and one below \(1/2\) reverses the comparison. With \(n_{\mathrm{item}}\) independent pairs, the observed win rate has standard deviation at most \(1/(2\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{item}}})\). A conservative design criterion is to require the population margin to be at least twice this worst-case standard deviation; for a nonzero margin it is satisfied by \(n_{\mathrm{item}}\ge1/\text{margin}^2\). This is a sufficient arithmetic criterion, not a power calculation or a confidence guarantee for an observed comparison. If people prefer A on 60% of inputs, it gives 100 human-labelled pairs; a judge right 80% of the time sees a margin of \(0.6\times0.1=0.06\) and the criterion gives 278 pairs, approximately 2.78 times as many (engineering check by shown arithmetic; the code's simulation of 200,000 pairs gives 0.5607 against the formula's 0.56).

The proposition assumes that the judge's errors do not depend on which output it is looking at. Zheng et al. measured three ways in which they do. Position bias: when the order of two answers was swapped, GPT-4 with the default prompt gave a consistent verdict in 65.0% of cases and favoured the first position in 30.0% (p. 5, Table 2). Verbosity bias: when an answer was padded with a repeated list, Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 preferred the padded answer in 91.3% of 23 cases and GPT-4 in 8.7% (p. 5, Table 3). Self-enhancement: compared with human judgments, GPT-4 as judge gave itself a 10% higher win rate and Claude-v1 gave itself a 25% higher one, although the authors state that with limited data and small differences their study cannot determine whether the models exhibit a self-enhancement bias (p. 5) (all empirical results for those judges and data). Each bias breaks the independence assumption of Proposition 4 in a specific direction. A self-enhancement bias, where present, bears directly on the recording's plan to compare GPT-4, Grok and Llama with an LLM judge: if the judge belongs to one of the compared families, its errors are not independent of which output it reads. Zheng et al.'s remedy for position bias is to call the judge twice with the order swapped and to count a win only when both calls agree (p. 6); exercise 5 shows what this does to a biased judge.

What parallelism buys

Let an orchestrator taking \(t_{\mathrm{lat},0}\) seconds dispatch \(n_{\mathrm{stage}}\) independent subtasks with latencies \(t_{\mathrm{lat},1},\dots,t_{\mathrm{lat},n_{\mathrm{stage}}}\). Run one after another, they take \(t_{\mathrm{lat},0}+\sum_jt_{\mathrm{lat},j}\); run in parallel, \(t_{\mathrm{lat},0}+\max_jt_{\mathrm{lat},j}\). With an orchestrator of 1.0 s and subtasks of 2.0, 3.0 and 5.0 s, the times are 11.0 s and 6.0 s (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Assume parallel execution preserves the same joint distribution of subtask outcomes, with no resource contention or shared-state interference. Then the success probability does not change: if the result requires every subtask to succeed, Proposition 1 applies to the set of subtasks whatever their order in time, because it depends only on the events \(E_j\), not on when they occur. Parallelism buys time, not reliability.

Slide 38 presents a comparison table attributed to McKinsey, favouring multi-agent workflows on modularity, fault isolation, optimisation, debugging, parallel processing and flexibility (product claim; the original article is unverified here). The recording repeats two rows, easier debugging of a specialised agent and time saved by parallel execution (≈6158–6184 s). The preceding latency formula supports the time comparison under its scheduling assumptions. Fault isolation is a different property: a fault in one agent does not corrupt or disable other agents. It depends on the boundaries between components, including their use of shared state and resources; having several agents does not ensure it. An entertainment-agent failure may be isolated while the lighting agent continues correctly. If the user's request requires both results, the complete request still fails, even though the fault was contained. Proposition 1 describes the probability of obtaining all required results under its hypotheses; it does not establish whether a fault propagates between agents.

def tokens(text):
    return [w for w in re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())
            if w not in STOP]


VOCAB = sorted({w for doc in CORPUS for w in tokens(doc)})


def f_emb(text):
    """Unit-norm bag-of-words encoding over VOCAB (a stand-in model)."""
    v = [float(tokens(text).count(w)) for w in VOCAB]
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(c * c for c in v))
    if norm == 0.0:
        raise ValueError("text shares no word with the corpus vocabulary")
    return [c / norm for c in v]


def cos(a, b):
    return sum(p * q for p, q in zip(a, b))  # a, b already unit norm

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction.

Each result in this chapter holds under a stated idealisation.

The chain model. Proposition 1 treats each stage as correct or incorrect and assumes that errors are never recovered (A1) and that stages behave as they do in isolation (A2). Real stages can be partly right, later stages can repair or hide earlier errors, and an agent that chooses its own steps has no fixed chain. The product formula additionally assumes independent failures. In the chapter's correlated example the product understates the rate by 0.046; in general the true rate can lie anywhere between the bounds of part (c), above or below the product.

Retrieval. The laboratory's embedding is a word count, not a trained encoder, and its corpus has eight documents with one relevance label per query. Recall@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) measures only whether a labelled document is retrieved; it says nothing about irrelevant documents retrieved beside it, about the answer's correctness, or about its faithfulness to the documents.

Judges. Proposition 3 compares a judge with one person's labels, which have their own errors; human–human agreement supplies a reference for interpreting judge agreement, not an upper bound. Zheng et al. report 85% GPT-4–human agreement alongside 81% human–human agreement. Proposition 4 assumes errors independent of the outputs judged, which the measured position and verbosity biases contradict, and which the self-favouring win rates reported by Zheng et al. put in doubt.

Evidence limits. McKinsey's article, Victor's New York Times report and Cohen's original paper lack retained local originals. The credit-memo exhibit is corroborated by the official McKinsey PDF text, but the six-row comparison remains slide attribution. Victor's reported bot episode retains recording/slide attribution. Cohen's bibliographic identity is verified; the chapter supplies its own definition and derivation rather than claiming to have checked his formula page. Context windows, the MCP specification and vendor descriptions are dated product claims and will change. The lecturer's statements about Workera's product, fine-tuning economics, prompting techniques, enterprise change and the future of the field are speaker judgments with their recording times.

What you can now do. You can describe a language-model application as a fixed model inside a program, and say which part of the input or the program a technique changes: instructions, examples, retrieved documents, conversation history, tools, the number and order of calls, the number of agents, or the weights. You can budget a context window, state what chain-of-thought and few-shot results were measured and for which models, and write the fine-tuning objective. You can build a retrieval step, show that cosine and Euclidean rankings agree on unit vectors, compute its cost and its Recall@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\), and keep score, relevance, correctness and faithfulness apart. You can compute the success probability of a chain under independence, bound it without independence, and add up its latency, and you can state when parallel agents shorten time while preserving the outcome distribution. You can design objective, subjective, component and end-to-end evals, measure a judge's agreement with people using \(\kappa\), and compute how judge error changes the sample count under the stated margin-to-standard-deviation criterion.

1. When do similarity scores disagree?

Let the query vector be \(\phi_{\mathrm q}=(1,0)\) and two document vectors be \(\phi_1=(1,0)\) and \(\phi_2=(3,3)\). (a) Rank the two documents by inner product, by cosine similarity and by Euclidean distance to \(\phi_{\mathrm q}\). (b) Normalise all three vectors to unit length and rank again. (c) A retriever trained with inner-product scores, like DPR, is deployed with a Euclidean index on unnormalised vectors. What can go wrong, and what fixes it?

Solution

(a) Inner products: \(\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_1=1\), \(\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_2=3\), so document 2 ranks first. Cosines: \(\cos(\phi_{\mathrm q},\phi_1)=1\), \(\cos(\phi_{\mathrm q},\phi_2)=3/(1\cdot3\sqrt2)=0.707\), so document 1 ranks first. Distances: \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}-\phi_1\rVert_2=0\), \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}-\phi_2\rVert_2=\sqrt{4+9}=3.606\), so document 1 ranks first. The inner product disagrees with the other two because it rewards the length of \(\phi_2\), not only its direction. (b) After normalisation \(\phi_1=(1,0)\) and \(\phi_2=(0.707,0.707)\). Inner product and cosine now coincide, 1 against 0.707, and the distances are 0 and \(\sqrt{2-2\times0.707}=0.765\); all three rank document 1 first, as Proposition 2 guarantees for unit vectors. (c) A model trained to score by inner product may encode relevance partly in vector length. A Euclidean index on the raw vectors ranks by a different quantity, \(\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}-\phi_j\rVert_2^2=\lVert\phi_{\mathrm q}\rVert_2^2+\lVert\phi_j\rVert_2^2-2\phi_{\mathrm q}^\top\phi_j\), which penalises long document vectors that the training rewarded. Either index with the score the model was trained for (maximum inner product search), or normalise at training and deployment alike. Karpukhin et al. found Euclidean distance and inner product comparable for their trained model and both better than cosine (p. 6), which shows that the choice is an empirical property of a trained encoder, not a matter of geometry alone.

2. Recall and what it cannot see

In the laboratory's run, the query "Does drug X make you feel sick?" has scores 0.354, 0.447, 0.500, 0.392, 0, 0, 0, 0.447 for documents 0 to 7, and its relevant document is 0. The query "My order arrived broken, can I get my money back?" ranks documents 5, 4, 0 first, with the relevant document 4 second; every other query ranks its relevant document first. (a) Compute Recall@4 and Recall@5 over the six queries. (b) For "What are the side effects of drug X?" the drug-Y document ranks second with score 0.555. How does Recall@2 register this document? Define and compute a measure that does register it, over all six queries at \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}=2\). (c) Name two changes to the retrieval step that address the "sick" query, and the cost of each.

Solution

(a) For the "sick" query the scores in decreasing order are 0.500 (doc 2), 0.447 (docs 1 and 7), 0.392 (doc 3), 0.354 (doc 0), so the relevant document is fifth. The "broken" query already succeeds at rank 2 and the other four at rank 1. Recall@4 = 5/6 = 0.833 and Recall@5 = 6/6 = 1. (b) Recall@2 counts only whether the labelled document is retrieved; the side-effects query succeeds at rank 1, and the drug-Y document beside it changes nothing. Precision@\(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\), the fraction of retrieved documents that are relevant, does register it. At \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}=2\) the six queries retrieve 12 documents, of which 5 are relevant (every query except "sick" retrieves its relevant document within two), so precision@2 = 5/12 = 0.417; with one relevant document per query it cannot exceed 0.5. The irrelevant drug-Y text would enter \(x_{\mathrm{doc}}\) and could be quoted by the model, a correctness risk that recall does not measure. (c) A learned embedding trained on suitable question–answer pairs may rank the nausea document higher for the "sick" query; evaluating that change costs an embedding model, a held-out retrieval check and re-indexing the corpus. A HyDE step may generate a hypothetical answer mentioning nausea before retrieval; it costs one model call per query and its latency, and the generated text can also direct retrieval toward the wrong neighbourhood. Raising \(n_{\mathrm{nb}}\) to 5 also retrieves the document here, at the cost of five documents' tokens in every prompt and more irrelevant text.

3. The extremes of a chain

Use the running case's rates \((p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4)=(0.95,0.99,0.93,0.995)\). (a) Construct a joint distribution of the four success events with these marginals for which the chain succeeds with probability exactly 0.865. (b) Construct one with success probability exactly 0.93. (c) A team measures each stage on correct inputs and reports \(\prod_jp_j=0.870\) as the system's reliability. What measurement would test their assumption, and what would a result of 0.91 tell them?

Solution

Think of the inputs as the interval \([0,1]\) with length as probability. (a) Make the failure sets disjoint: stage 1 fails on \([0,0.05)\), stage 2 on \([0.05,0.06)\), stage 3 on \([0.06,0.13)\), stage 4 on \([0.13,0.135)\). Every marginal is right, and the chain fails on the union, of length 0.135, so it succeeds with probability 0.865, the lower bound of Proposition 1(c). This is possible because the failure probabilities sum to less than 1. (b) Nest the failure sets inside the largest one: stage 3 fails on \([0,0.07)\), stage 1 on \([0,0.05)\), stage 2 on \([0,0.01)\), stage 4 on \([0,0.005)\). The union is \([0,0.07)\), so success is 0.93, the upper bound. (c) Run the whole chain on a labelled sample of real requests and measure the end-to-end success rate directly; compare it with 0.870 using its sampling error. A rate of 0.91 whose uncertainty excludes 0.870 rejects the joint set of assumptions behind the product prediction. If (A1)–(A2), the tested marginals and the request population still hold, mutual independence fails and the product understated reliability. Overlapping failure sets, as in the "hard input" example, are one explanation; the single rate does not identify all pairwise correlations. Recovery or changed component rates are alternative explanations when those other assumptions fail. Examine paired component outcomes and recovery traces before attributing the discrepancy.

4. Two judges with the same agreement

(a) On the chapter's 100 traces, of which people mark 90 polite, a judge labels every trace "pass". Compute \(p_o\), \(p_e\) and \(\kappa\). (b) On a second set of 100 traces people mark 50 polite. A judge agrees with them on 45 polite and 45 impolite traces and disagrees on 5 of each. Compute \(p_o\), \(p_e\) and \(\kappa\). (c) Both judges have \(p_o\ge0.9\). Which would you use to find impolite replies, and why does the chance-corrected statistic differ so much?

Solution

(a) The judge agrees on the 90 polite traces and disagrees on the 10 impolite ones, so \(p_o=0.90\). With \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}=0.90\) and \(p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}=1\), \(p_e=0.90\times1+0.10\times0=0.90\), and \(\kappa=(0.90-0.90)/(1-0.90)=0\), as Proposition 3(c) predicts. (b) \(p_o=(45+45)/100=0.90\). Both labellers pass 50 traces, so \(p_{\mathrm{pass,hum}}=p_{\mathrm{pass,jdg}}=0.5\) and \(p_e=0.25+0.25=0.5\); \(\kappa=(0.90-0.5)/0.5=0.80\). (c) The second judge. The first detects no impolite reply; its 90% agreement is the prevalence of politeness. The second catches 45 of 50 impolite replies. Observed agreement mixes the judge's skill with the base rate of the label; \(\kappa\) subtracts the agreement that the two labellers' pass rates alone would produce, so it is 0 for the first judge and 0.80 for the second. When the failure of interest is rare, report also the fraction of failures caught, since that is the quantity the eval exists to measure.

5. Swapping the order of a biased judge

A pairwise judge ignores the content with probability \(p_{\mathrm{first}}\) and picks whichever answer is shown first; otherwise it picks the answer people prefer. People prefer A on a fraction \(p_{\mathrm A}\) of inputs. (a) If A is always shown first, find the probability that the judge prefers A, and evaluate it for \(p_{\mathrm{first}}=0.3\), \(p_{\mathrm A}=0.4\). (b) If the order is chosen by a fair coin for each input, show that the probability becomes \(\tfrac12+(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})(p_{\mathrm A}-\tfrac12)\). (c) Zheng et al.'s conservative rule calls the judge twice with the order swapped and declares a win only when both calls agree, otherwise a tie. With calls independent conditional on the fixed human preference, find the fractions of A wins, B wins and ties, and A's share of the non-tied inputs.

Solution

(a) With probability \(p_{\mathrm{first}}\) the judge picks the first answer, which is A; otherwise it picks A exactly when people prefer A. Thus the A preference probability is \(p_{\mathrm{first}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}=0.3+0.7\times0.4=0.58\). The judge favours A although people prefer B on 60% of inputs. (b) When A is first the probability is \(p_{\mathrm{first}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}\); when B is first it is \((1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}\). Averaging gives \(p_{\mathrm{first}}/2+(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}=\tfrac12+(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})(p_{\mathrm A}-\tfrac12)\). For \(p_{\mathrm{first}}<1\), the positive multiplier preserves the sign of a nonzero human preference margin; at \(p_{\mathrm{first}}=1\), the judged rate is exactly one half and conveys no direction. For part (a)'s numbers it is 0.43. (c) If people prefer A, the A-first call always picks A, while the B-first call picks A with probability \(1-p_{\mathrm{first}}\). A therefore wins with probability \(1-p_{\mathrm{first}}\), and otherwise the two calls disagree and the rule records a tie; B never wins on those inputs. The symmetric argument applies when people prefer B. Overall, A wins on a fraction \((1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}\), B on \((1-p_{\mathrm{first}})(1-p_{\mathrm A})\), and ties occupy a fraction \(p_{\mathrm{first}}\). If \(p_{\mathrm{first}}<1\), the non-tie probability is positive, and A's population share conditional on a non-tie is \((1-p_{\mathrm{first}})p_{\mathrm A}/(1-p_{\mathrm{first}})=p_{\mathrm A}\). At \(p_{\mathrm{first}}=1\), every input ties and that conditional share is undefined. Even in the interior, a finite run may contain no non-ties, so its empirical share need not be available. Under this model the rule removes the population bias among non-ties by discarding a fraction \(p_{\mathrm{first}}\) of inputs.

6. An evaluation plan for the support agent

Design the evals for the customer-support agent of the case study before launch. Cover each failure of slide 35, say which evals are component-based or end-to-end, objective or subjective, and quantitative or qualitative, state how you would validate any LLM judge, and set a latency budget for the chain of Figure 1. Say what the plan cannot establish.

Solution

Prerequisites: traces of actual calls, labelled end-to-end outcomes, and component tests on correct reference inputs drawn from the same request population. The component tests must include requests whose live prefixes failed; otherwise their rates describe a selected subset. Objective, component, quantitative. (i) Order-ID check in code on every trace, as in the laboratory; report the fraction failed and review flagged traces where the customer's text holds zero or several IDs. (ii) A policy stage, which slide 33 lacks, implemented as a rule table (address changes allowed until shipping; refunds for damaged orders within 30 days in the corpus's policy) and tested with one case per rule and its boundary; this catches the refund row. (iii) An effective-date check comparing the extracted date with phrases such as "next month"; this catches the cancellation row. (iv) Per-stage latency from traces; with Figure 1's numbers the chain takes 9.0 s and the email step takes 5.0 s, so a 6-second user-response budget can be met by acknowledging the queued email asynchronously while retaining a separate budget for completed delivery; this does not reduce the time to actually send the email. Subjective, component (drafting) and end-to-end. (v) A rubric judge for tone and acknowledgement, catching the porch and birthday rows. Validate it on at least a few hundred traces labelled by two people: report the people's agreement with each other, the judge's \(\kappa\) against them and the fraction of impolite replies it catches; use a judge from a different model family than the drafting model, given the self-favouring win rates that Zheng et al. report for GPT-4 and Claude-v1. End-to-end, quantitative. (vi) Estimate whole-request success on a labelled sample of real requests per request type. Compare it, allowing for sampling uncertainty in both estimates, with the product of the corresponding reference-input component rates. A discrepancy rejects the joint assumptions behind that prediction. It implicates failure dependence only if (A1)–(A2), the component marginals and the request population remain valid; recovery or changed rates are other explanations. Examine paired reference-input component outcomes and recovery traces before attributing the discrepancy, as in exercise 3. Qualitative. (vii) Weekly reading of about 20 random traces to find new failure types; under the independent-sampling model this finds a failure affecting 10% of traces with probability 0.878 and one affecting 1% with probability only 0.182. Limits. The plan estimates rates on sampled requests of known types; it cannot establish behaviour on request types absent from the samples, on adversarial inputs such as prompt injection through the customer's text, or after the model provider changes the model behind the same name.
