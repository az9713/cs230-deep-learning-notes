Learning curves, capacity, and the decision to collect more data

A team has a few hundred labelled examples and a model whose test error is too high for its product. It can spend months collecting ten times more data, train a larger model, buy more computing hardware, or change what the model is given to read. The opening lecture of CS230 makes two claims that bear on this choice: that the progress of deep learning over the last ten to fifteen years came from models that keep improving as data and computation grow while older learning algorithms stop improving, and that teams lose months pursuing the wrong option. This chapter asks what a measured learning curve can tell such a team, and what it cannot.

The answer needs a precise model of learning from examples, which this chapter builds and every later chapter uses: a dataset, a family of predictors indexed by parameters, a loss, the average loss on the training set, and the expected loss on the population the data came from. With that model, the lecture's whiteboard sketch becomes a statement about learning curves that can be compared with the measurements of Hestness et al. (2017) and Kaplan et al. (2020), and the lecture's advice about how much data to collect becomes a forecast whose error can be measured. The rest of the lecture, on the structure of the course, prototyping and careers, is recorded with the evidence behind each statement, which for most of it is the lecturer's experience.

Learning from examples

A supervised learning problem has inputs \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\), vectors of \(n_x\) numbers such as measurements or pixel intensities, and targets \(y\); in this chapter \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is a class label. The pairs \((x,y)\) come from a fixed joint distribution \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\) that the learner does not know. A training set \(\mathcal D=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m\) consists of \(m\) pairs drawn independently from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\); the superscript \((i)\) indexes examples. Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville call this the i.i.d. assumption: the examples are independent of each other, and training and test examples are drawn from the same distribution (Deep Learning, 2016, p. 109). A model is a family of functions \(f_\theta:\mathbb R^{n_x}\to\mathbb R^{n_y}\), with \(n_y=1\) throughout this chapter, indexed by a parameter vector \(\theta\in\mathbb R^{n_\theta}\), and the prediction for an input \(x\) is \(\hat y=f_\theta(x)\). A neural network is one such family; chapter 2 builds it. The running case below uses a simpler family.

A per-example loss \(\mathcal L(\hat y,y)\ge0\) scores one prediction. The cost, also called the empirical risk, is its average over the training set, and the population risk is its expectation over new pairs:

\[J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\mathcal L\bigl(f_\theta(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)}\bigr),\qquad \mathcal R(\theta)=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\,\mathcal L\bigl(f_\theta(x),y\bigr).\]

Empirical risk minimisation (ERM) chooses parameters \(\hat\theta\) that minimise \(J\), because \(\mathcal R\) cannot be computed without knowing \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\). Goodfellow et al. define the risk as the expected loss under the data-generating distribution (p. 272, eq. 8.2, where it is written \(J^*\)) and the empirical risk as its average over the training set, naming the training process "empirical risk minimization" (p. 273, eq. 8.3). What matters after training is \(\mathcal R(\hat\theta)\). The generalisation gap is \(\mathcal R(\hat\theta)-J(\hat\theta)\).

For a classifier two losses appear. Training minimises a smooth loss, in this chapter the squared loss \(\mathcal L(\hat y,y)=(\hat y-y)^2\). The number reported is the classification error. The rule "predict class 1 when \(f_\theta(x)>\tfrac12\)" has the 0-1 risk

\[\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)=\mathbb P_{(x,y)\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\Bigl(\mathbb 1\bigl[f_\theta(x)>\tfrac12\bigr]\ne y\Bigr),\]

the probability that the predicted label is wrong, where \(\mathbb 1[\cdot]\) equals 1 when its condition holds and 0 otherwise. Goodfellow et al. explain why training does not minimise \(\mathcal R_{01}\) directly: the 0-1 loss has a derivative that is zero or undefined everywhere, so training optimises a surrogate loss instead (p. 273). A guarantee about \(\mathcal R\) is therefore not a guarantee about \(\mathcal R_{01}\), and the two are kept apart below.

Proposition 1 (the training cost is optimistic). Assume the \(m\) training pairs are drawn independently from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\), that \(\hat\theta\) minimises \(J\) exactly over \(\mathbb R^{n_\theta}\) for every training set, and that some \(\theta^*\) minimises \(\mathcal R\) over \(\mathbb R^{n_\theta}\) with \(\mathcal R(\theta^*)\) finite. Then, with expectations taken over the training set,

\[\mathbb E\bigl[J(\hat\theta)\bigr]\ \le\ \mathcal R(\theta^*)\ \le\ \mathbb E\bigl[\mathcal R(\hat\theta)\bigr].\]

Proof. For every training set, \(J(\hat\theta)\le J(\theta^*)\), because \(\hat\theta\) minimises \(J\). The vector \(\theta^*\) does not depend on the training set, so each term of \(J(\theta^*)\) is the loss of a fixed function on a fresh draw from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and has expectation \(\mathcal R(\theta^*)\); hence \(\mathbb E[J(\theta^*)]=\mathcal R(\theta^*)\), and taking expectations in the first inequality gives the left half. The right half holds because \(\mathcal R(\theta^*)\le\mathcal R(\theta)\) for every \(\theta\), in particular for \(\theta=\hat\theta\). \(\blacksquare\)

The expected generalisation gap is therefore never negative. The training cost of the chosen parameters underestimates their population risk on average, because the same data both chose \(\theta\) and scored it. Goodfellow et al. state the inequality between expected test error and expected training error without a proof (p. 109). The argument also shows what does give an unbiased estimate: the average loss of \(\hat\theta\) on a separate test set, drawn from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and not used to choose \(\hat\theta\), has expectation \(\mathcal R(\hat\theta)\) given \(\hat\theta\), for the same reason that \(J(\theta^*)\) has expectation \(\mathcal R(\theta^*)\).

Two limits belong beside Proposition 1. It concerns the loss that was minimised, here the squared loss, and says nothing directly about \(\mathcal R_{01}\). And it assumes an exact minimiser; a network trained by an iterative optimiser stopped after a finite number of steps is not one, and a choice among several trained models made on the test set turns that set into training data for the choice (chapter 6).

The running case: sixteen weak measurements and three models

Every mechanism in this chapter is tested on one distribution for which the best achievable error of every model has a closed form. A label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is drawn with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. The input has \(n_x=16\) coordinates,

\[x=(2y-1)\,\mu+\chi,\qquad \mu=(\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac12)\in\mathbb R^{16},\qquad \chi\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\]

where \(\mathcal N(0,I)\) is the standard Gaussian distribution on \(\mathbb R^{16}\), with mean 0 and identity covariance \(I\). Class 1 is a cloud of points around \(\mu\), class 0 a cloud around \(-\mu\), and \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2=2\). Each coordinate is a weak measurement: alone, it separates the two class means by 1 against noise of standard deviation 1. The classes become distinguishable only when many coordinates are combined.

Three models differ in capacity. The model with \(n_{\mathrm{feat}}\in\{1,4,16\}\) reads only the first \(n_{\mathrm{feat}}\) coordinates, written \(x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\), and computes

\[f_\theta(x)=b+w^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}},\qquad \theta=(b,w)\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{feat}}+1},\]

with a bias \(b\in\mathbb R\) and a weight vector \(w\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\). It is trained by ERM with the squared loss and classifies by the rule \(f_\theta(x)>\tfrac12\). The three models have 2, 5 and 17 parameters. Their capacity is restricted by the inputs they may use, which is the simplest restriction whose best error has a closed form. In a neural network the restriction comes from width and depth instead, and the best error of a given architecture cannot be computed; the running case exhibits the mechanism, not the numbers of any network. ERM has an exact solution here: \(J\) is a quadratic in \((b,w)\), and setting its gradient to zero gives a linear system, the normal equations, which the process section writes out after the Hessian; so no optimisation error enters the results.

Proposition 2 (the floor of each model). Write \(\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) for the first \(n_{\mathrm{feat}}\) coordinates of \(\mu\). Over all \((b,w)\), the population risk under the squared loss is minimised by

\[b^*=\tfrac12,\qquad w^*=\frac{\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}}{2\bigl(1+\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2\bigr)},\qquad \mathcal R(\theta^*)=\frac1{4\bigl(1+\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2\bigr)}.\]

The rule \(f_{\theta^*}(x)>\tfrac12\) has 0-1 risk \(\Phi\bigl(-\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2\bigr)\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function, and no classifier that sees only \(x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) has a lower 0-1 risk.

Proof. For \(y\in\{0,1\}\), \(y(2y-1)=y\) and \((2y-1)^2=1\). Hence \(\mathbb E[x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}]=0\), \(\mathbb E[x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\,y]=\tfrac12\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) and \(\mathbb E[x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top]=I+\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top\). The risk \(\mathcal R(b,w)=\mathbb E\bigl[(b+w^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-y)^2\bigr]\) is a quadratic whose Hessian, twice the second-moment matrix of \((1,x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})\), is positive definite, so its unique minimiser is the point where its gradient vanishes. Setting the derivative in \(b\) to zero gives \(b=\mathbb E[y]-w^\top\mathbb E[x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}]=\tfrac12\). Setting the gradient in \(w\) to zero gives \((I+\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top)\,w=\tfrac12\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\), and since \((I+\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top)\,\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}=(1+\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2)\,\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\), the solution is \(w^*\). At the minimiser the residual \(y-b^*-w^{*\top}x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) has mean 0 and is uncorrelated with \(x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) (the two gradient conditions), so multiplying it by itself equals, in expectation, multiplying it by \(y-\tfrac12\):

\[\mathcal R(\theta^*)=\mathbb E\bigl[(y-b^*-w^{*\top}x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})(y-\tfrac12)\bigr]=\mathbb E\bigl[(y-\tfrac12)^2\bigr]-\tfrac12\,w^{*\top}\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}=\frac14-\frac{\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2}{4\bigl(1+\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2\bigr)},\]

which equals the stated value. Because \(w^*\) is a positive multiple of \(\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\), the rule \(f_{\theta^*}(x)>\tfrac12\) is the rule \(\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}>0\). Given \(y=1\), the score \(\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}=\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2+\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top\chi_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) is Gaussian with mean \(\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2\) and standard deviation \(\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2\), so the rule errs with probability \(\Phi(-\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2)\); the case \(y=0\) is symmetric. For optimality, \(x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) given the class is \(\mathcal N(\pm\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}},I)\), so by Bayes' rule with equal priors the log-odds of class 1 are \(-\tfrac12\lVert x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2+\tfrac12\lVert x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}+\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2^2=2\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\). Any rule that sees \(x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) errs there with probability at least the smaller of the two class posteriors, and the rule that picks the more probable class attains that bound; that rule is \(\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}>0\). \(\blacksquare\)

As \(m\) grows, the least-squares solution converges to \((b^*,w^*)\), because it is a continuous function of the sample moments, which converge to the population moments above; the 0-1 risk of the trained rule then approaches \(\Phi(-\lVert\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\rVert_2)\). This limit is the floor of the model family. Only the 16-input model's floor, 0.0228, is the lowest error any classifier can reach on this problem, its Bayes error. The floors 0.3085 and 0.1587 of the smaller models are limits of their capacity: the information they need is in coordinates they do not read.

The curves below need the risks of an arbitrary trained rule, not only of \((b^*,w^*)\). For any \((b,w)\) with \(w\ne0\), the score \(b+w^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) given \(y=1\) equals \(b+w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}+w^\top\chi_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\), which is Gaussian with mean \(b+w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) and standard deviation \(\lVert w\rVert_2\); given \(y=0\) its mean is \(b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) with the same standard deviation. The rule \(b+w^\top x_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}>\tfrac12\) therefore misclassifies a class-1 point with probability \(\Phi\bigl((\tfrac12-b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})/\lVert w\rVert_2\bigr)\) and a class-0 point with probability \(\Phi\bigl((b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-\tfrac12)/\lVert w\rVert_2\bigr)\), and with equal class probabilities

\[\mathcal R_{01}(b,w)=\tfrac12\,\Phi\Bigl(\frac{\tfrac12-b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}}{\lVert w\rVert_2}\Bigr)+\tfrac12\,\Phi\Bigl(\frac{b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-\tfrac12}{\lVert w\rVert_2}\Bigr).\]

The squared-loss risk of the same rule is the average over the two classes of the expected squared distance between score and label. In class 1 the expectation is \(\mathbb E\bigl[(b+w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-1+w^\top\chi_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})^2\bigr]=(b+w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-1)^2+\lVert w\rVert_2^2\), because \(w^\top\chi_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\) has mean 0 and variance \(\lVert w\rVert_2^2\); in class 0 it is \((b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})^2+\lVert w\rVert_2^2\). Hence

\[\mathcal R(b,w)=\lVert w\rVert_2^2+\tfrac12\bigl(b+w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}-1\bigr)^2+\tfrac12\bigl(b-w^\top\mu_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}}\bigr)^2.\]

At \((b^*,w^*)\) these give the values in Proposition 2. For \(n_{\mathrm{feat}}=16\), \(b^*=\tfrac12\), \(w^*=\mu/10\), so \(w^{*\top}\mu=0.4\) and \(\lVert w^*\rVert_2=0.2\); both arguments of \(\Phi\) equal \((\tfrac12-\tfrac12-0.4)/0.2=-2\), giving 0.0228, and the squared-loss risk is \(0.04+\tfrac12(0.01)+\tfrac12(0.01)=0.050\). The program in the laboratory section evaluates these two formulas at every trained \((b,w)\); no test set is sampled, so the curves carry no test-set noise.

Proposition 1 can be seen in the same fixture. For the 16-input model trained on \(m=40\) examples, the average over 200 training sets of \(J(\hat\theta)\) is 0.0284 and that of \(\mathcal R(\hat\theta)\) is 0.088, on either side of \(\mathcal R(\theta^*)=0.050\) (engineering check). The gap is wide because 17 parameters are fitted to 40 examples.

Lecture: the whiteboard figure of performance against amount of data for older algorithms and for small, medium and large neural networks; speech recognition as the example; the Stanford work on graphics processors and the story of the first machine; the Baidu and OpenAI scaling papers (≈172–425 s). Original construction: the learning-curve definition as used here, Proposition 3, Figure 1, and the floors computed from Kaplan et al.'s constants.

The lecturer draws a figure on the whiteboard (video frames at ≈290 and ≈330 s). The vertical axis is labelled "performance" and the horizontal axis "Data", and several curves rise from a common origin. The lowest, for older algorithms such as logistic regression and decision trees, flattens early; curves labelled "Small NN" and, higher, "Large NN", with a medium network between them, keep rising longer (≈204–295 s). The drawing has no scale and no numbers. With speech recognition as the example, the lecturer says that older algorithms stopped improving even as tens of thousands of hours of audio were added, while larger networks kept improving (speaker judgment, ≈233–295 s). The drawing is a qualitative sketch of a claim, not a measurement. Measured versions exist, and comparing them with the sketch needs one definition.

A learning curve is the expected test error as a function of the number of training examples,

\[\mathrm{Err}(m)=\mathbb E_{\mathcal D}\bigl[\mathcal R_{01}(\hat\theta_{\mathcal D})\bigr],\]

where \(\hat\theta_{\mathcal D}\) is the ERM solution for a training set \(\mathcal D\) of size \(m\) and the expectation is over training sets. It differs from a training curve, which plots the cost against optimisation steps on one fixed dataset; Hestness et al. draw the same distinction (p. 2, footnote 1). The sketch's "performance" axis is \(\mathrm{Err}\) turned upside down.

What was measured

Hestness et al. (2017), of Baidu Research, trained state-of-the-art models on subsets ("shards") of large training sets, with sizes spanning two to three orders of magnitude in steps of about 2 (p. 4), in machine translation, language modelling, image classification and speech recognition (p. 2). They write a learning curve as \(\varepsilon(m)=\alpha m^{\beta_g}\) and, for a model of fixed size, as \(\varepsilon(m)=\alpha m^{\beta_g}+\gamma\), where \(\gamma\) is "the error when the model family has exhausted its capacity" (p. 5). This book writes the second form as

\[\mathrm{Err}(m)\approx\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}+\Lambda\,m^{-\xi},\qquad \Lambda>0,\ \ \xi>0,\]

with scale \(\Lambda=\alpha\), exponent \(\xi=-\beta_g\), and floor \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}=\gamma\). Their findings, each an empirical result for the models and data they tested, are these:

  • Power-law learning curves appeared in all four domains, with exponents \(\xi\) that usually settled between 0.07 and 0.35 (their \(\beta_g\) between −0.07 and −0.35), against 0.5 or 1 predicted by earlier theory (p. 2).
  • In translation, two fixed-size models with 208 and 512 hidden units per layer followed \(\mathrm{Err}_{208}(N_{\mathrm{tok}})=0.39+41.2\,N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{-0.36}\) and \(\mathrm{Err}_{512}(N_{\mathrm{tok}})=0.32+21.5\,N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{-0.30}\) (the paper writes \(\varepsilon_{208}\), \(\varepsilon_{512}\)), with the training-set size counted in tokens, this book's \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\), between \(2^{20}\) and \(2^{27}\) (p. 6, Figure 1); the power-law reading below (Proposition 3) applies with \(m\) read as \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\). The larger model has the lower floor.
  • In speech recognition, a model with 1.7 million parameters stopped improving from around 170 hours of audio and one with 6 million from around 860 hours, while one with 87 million stayed close to the power-law trend up to larger datasets (p. 10, Figure 5).
  • Better architectures shifted learning curves down but did not change the exponent (p. 2, p. 11), and the exponents are "yet to be explained by theoretical work" (p. 1).

The speech result is the measured counterpart of the whiteboard sketch in one respect: networks of fixed size flatten, and larger ones flatten later and lower. It is not evidence for the lecture's statement about older speech-recognition algorithms, which Hestness et al. did not test; that statement remains speaker judgment. Their Figure 6 (p. 11), which divides a learning curve into a small-data region, a power-law region and an irreducible-error region, is itself a sketch, and the authors state that they had not yet reached the irreducible-error region for any real application in their study (p. 11).

Kaplan et al. (2020), of OpenAI, measured the test cross-entropy loss, in nats per token (p. 6), of Transformer language models as a function of the number of parameters \(N_{\mathrm{param}}\), excluding embeddings, and the number of training tokens \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\). They write these as \(N\) and \(D\) and the loss as \(L\); this book reserves \(D\) for a discriminator (chapter 4) and \(L\) for a number of layers (chapter 2). Their joint fit, eq. 1.5 (p. 5), is in this book's letters

\[\mathcal R_{\mathrm{fit}}(N_{\mathrm{param}},N_{\mathrm{tok}})=\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}}{N_{\mathrm{param}}}\Bigr)^{\xi_{\mathrm{param}}/\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}}+\frac{N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{\mathrm c}}{N_{\mathrm{tok}}}\Bigr]^{\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}},\]

with the constants of their Table 2 (p. 11): exponents \(\xi_{\mathrm{param}}=0.076\) and \(\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}=0.103\) and scale constants \(N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}=6.4\times10^{13}\) parameters and \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{\mathrm c}=1.8\times10^{13}\) tokens (their \(\alpha_N,\alpha_D,N_c,D_c\)). The two one-variable laws that eq. 1.5 combines were fitted separately (p. 4): for models trained to convergence on enough data, \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{fit}}=(N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}/N_{\mathrm{param}})^{\xi_{\mathrm{param}}}\) with \(\xi_{\mathrm{param}}\approx0.076\) and \(N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}\approx8.8\times10^{13}\) (eq. 1.1), and for large models trained on a limited dataset with early stopping, \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{fit}}=(N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{\mathrm c}/N_{\mathrm{tok}})^{\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}}\) with \(\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}\approx0.095\) and \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{\mathrm c}\approx5.4\times10^{13}\) (eq. 1.2). The authors note that the joint fit's parameters "differ very slightly" from the one-variable fits because the joint fit is made to the whole surface (p. 11). The fits hold across six orders of magnitude in model size but over only two orders of magnitude in dataset size (p. 5), and the constants "do not have a fundamental meaning" because they depend on the vocabulary and tokenisation (p. 5).

The fixed-size plateau follows from the formula. For fixed \(N_{\mathrm{param}}\), the bracket decreases as \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\) grows and tends to \((N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}/N_{\mathrm{param}})^{\xi_{\mathrm{param}}/\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}}\); since the outer exponent is positive, \(\mathcal R_{\mathrm{fit}}\) decreases toward

\[\lim_{N_{\mathrm{tok}}\to\infty}\mathcal R_{\mathrm{fit}}(N_{\mathrm{param}},N_{\mathrm{tok}})=\Bigl(\frac{N_{\mathrm{param}}^{\mathrm c}}{N_{\mathrm{param}}}\Bigr)^{\xi_{\mathrm{param}}},\]

which has the form of their eq. 1.1 and falls as \(N_{\mathrm{param}}\) grows. Kaplan et al. chose the form of eq. 1.5 so that it has this limit (p. 10). For the smallest model in their Figure 4, with 393.2 thousand parameters, the limit with the Table 2 constants is \((6.4\times10^{13}/3.932\times10^5)^{0.076}=4.21\) nats per token, and eq. 1.1 with its own constants gives \((8.8\times10^{13}/3.932\times10^5)^{0.076}=4.31\); for the largest, with 708 million, the two values are \((6.4\times10^{13}/7.08\times10^{8})^{0.076}=2.38\) and \((8.8\times10^{13}/7.08\times10^{8})^{0.076}=2.44\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic from the published constants). The 0.06 to 0.10 nats between the two fits is the size of the disagreement the authors call slight. Figure 4 (left panel, p. 5) shows the smallest model's loss levelling near 4.3 and the largest reaching about 2.4, below the lowest tick of 2.5, at the largest dataset shown (empirical result). Doubling the parameters lowers the floor by the factor \(2^{-0.076}=0.95\), the factor the authors quote (p. 5). The sketch's picture, in which each network flattens and a larger network flattens lower, is what this fit states over the measured ranges.

Two statements in the same paper limit how far the fits reach. The data law, eq. 1.2, has no floor: it predicts a loss that falls toward zero. The authors write that performance "must flatten out eventually before reaching zero loss" (p. 3) and that the trends "must eventually level off" because natural language has non-zero entropy (p. 17). What a power law states, and what it does not

Proposition 3 (reading a power-law learning curve). Suppose \(\mathrm{Err}(m)=\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}+\Lambda m^{-\xi}\) with \(\Lambda>0\) and \(\xi>0\) for all \(m\) in a range. Then, within that range:

(a) \(\log\bigl(\mathrm{Err}(m)-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\bigr)=\log\Lambda-\xi\log m\), a straight line of slope \(-\xi\) on log-log axes;
(b) doubling the data multiplies the reducible error by a constant: \(\mathrm{Err}(2m)-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}=2^{-\xi}\bigl(\mathrm{Err}(m)-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\bigr)\);
(c) a target error \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tgt}}>\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\) is reached at \(m_{\mathrm{tgt}}=\bigl(\Lambda/(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tgt}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}})\bigr)^{1/\xi}\), and a target \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tgt}}\le\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\) is reached by no \(m\);
(d) the slope of the curve itself on log-log axes is \(\dfrac{d\log\mathrm{Err}}{d\log m}=-\xi\Bigl(1-\dfrac{\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}}{\mathrm{Err}(m)}\Bigr)\).

Proof. (a) Take logarithms of \(\mathrm{Err}(m)-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}=\Lambda m^{-\xi}\). (b) \(\Lambda(2m)^{-\xi}=2^{-\xi}\Lambda m^{-\xi}\). (c) Solve \(\Lambda m^{-\xi}=\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tgt}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\) for \(m\); if the right side is not positive there is no solution, because \(\Lambda m^{-\xi}>0\). (d) By the chain rule, \(d\log\mathrm{Err}/d\log m=(m/\mathrm{Err})\,d\mathrm{Err}/dm=(m/\mathrm{Err})(-\xi\Lambda m^{-\xi-1})=-\xi\Lambda m^{-\xi}/\mathrm{Err}=-\xi\,(\mathrm{Err}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}})/\mathrm{Err}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Part (d) is the plateau of the sketch in a formula. The slope of the curve on log-log axes is the exponent scaled by the fraction of the error that is still reducible, so the curve flattens as \(\mathrm{Err}(m)\) approaches the floor, even though the reducible part keeps shrinking by the factor \(2^{-\xi}\) at every doubling. A model family looks saturated once its reducible error is small compared with its floor. Parts (a)–(c) hold only inside the range where the formula was fitted; the proposition says nothing about sizes outside it.

Part (b) turns an exponent into a data budget. Halving the reducible error takes \(2^{1/\xi}\) times the data: twice as much at \(\xi=1\), 10.1 times at the speech exponent \(\xi=0.30\) of Hestness et al. (p. 10), and 225 times at \(\xi=0.128\), the exponent they project for their composite translation curve, which uses the best model at each size (p. 6). Kaplan et al.'s data law, eq. 1.2, has \(\xi_{\mathrm{tok}}=0.095\) and no floor, so doubling the tokens multiplies the loss by \(2^{-0.095}=0.936\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic for each number in this paragraph).

For each model and each \(m\in\{40,80,160,\dots,5120\}\), doubling at each step, the code below draws 200 training sets, solves ERM exactly, and computes the exact 0-1 risk of each solution; \(\mathrm{Err}(m)\) is the average (engineering check). The measurements answer the chapter's first question only in part. They do not explain why the exponents take their values, and they do not show that the curves continue beyond the measured ranges.

Parameters, hyperparameters and gradient descent

The lecturer describes hyperparameters as parameters that control other parameters: the weights of a network are parameters, while the learning rate and the size of the network are hyperparameters (≈1453–1482 s). Goodfellow et al. define hyperparameters as settings that control a learning algorithm's behaviour and whose values are "not adapted by the learning algorithm itself" (p. 118). In the running case, \(\theta=(b,w)\) are parameters and \(n_{\mathrm{feat}}\) is a hyperparameter. For a neural network, ERM has no closed-form solution, and \(\theta\) is found by gradient descent: from a starting point \(\theta_0\), repeat

\[\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla J(\theta_t),\qquad t=0,1,2,\dots,\]

where \(t\) counts the steps of the iteration and the learning rate \(\eta>0\) is a hyperparameter. The lecturer recalls nights in the office until 2 or 3 a.m. tuning hyperparameters, and says that skill at it decided whether he went home at 3 a.m. or at 7 a.m. (speaker judgment, ≈1482–1536 s). One reason the learning rate matters can be proved for a quadratic cost such as the squared-loss cost of the running case. Write \(\tilde x^{(i)}=(1,x^{(i)}_{1:n_{\mathrm{feat}}})\) for the \(i\)-th input with a constant 1 prepended, so that \(f_\theta(x^{(i)})=\theta^\top\tilde x^{(i)}\). Then

\[J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\bigl(\theta^\top\tilde x^{(i)}-y^{(i)}\bigr)^2,\qquad \nabla^2J=\frac2m\sum_{i=1}^m\tilde x^{(i)}\tilde x^{(i)\top}.\]

Setting \(\nabla J=\frac2m\sum_{i=1}^m\tilde x^{(i)}\bigl(\tilde x^{(i)\top}\theta-y^{(i)}\bigr)\) to zero gives the normal equations \(\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^m\tilde x^{(i)}\tilde x^{(i)\top}\bigr)\theta=\sum_{i=1}^m\tilde x^{(i)}y^{(i)}\), the linear system that the running case's exact ERM solution solves; and the Hessian \(\nabla^2J\) does not depend on \(\theta\). It is positive definite when the vectors \(\tilde x^{(i)}\) span \(\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{feat}}+1}\), which in the running case happens with probability 1 once \(m\ge n_{\mathrm{feat}}+1\). Write \(\mathrm{eig}_{\max}(\nabla^2J)\) for its largest eigenvalue.

Proposition 4 (the step-size threshold of a quadratic cost). Let \(J\) be a quadratic cost with constant, positive-definite Hessian \(\nabla^2J\) and minimiser \(\hat\theta\). Gradient descent with a fixed learning rate \(\eta>0\) converges to \(\hat\theta\) from every starting point if and only if \(\eta<2/\mathrm{eig}_{\max}(\nabla^2J)\).

Proof. Because \(J\) is quadratic with minimiser \(\hat\theta\), its gradient is exactly \(\nabla J(\theta)=\nabla^2J\,(\theta-\hat\theta)\). Subtracting \(\hat\theta\) from both sides of the update gives \(\theta_{t+1}-\hat\theta=(I-\eta\nabla^2J)(\theta_t-\hat\theta)\). Expand \(\theta_0-\hat\theta\) in an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of \(\nabla^2J\). The component along an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\mathrm{eig}_j\) is multiplied by \(1-\eta\,\mathrm{eig}_j\) at every step, so every component of every start shrinks to zero exactly when \(\lvert1-\eta\,\mathrm{eig}_j\rvert<1\), that is \(0<\eta\,\mathrm{eig}_j<2\), for every \(j\). For positive eigenvalues this is \(\eta<2/\mathrm{eig}_{\max}(\nabla^2J)\). If \(\eta\ge2/\mathrm{eig}_{\max}(\nabla^2J)\), a start with a non-zero component along the top eigenvector keeps that component's size or increases it at every step. \(\blacksquare\)

For the 16-input model the population version of the Hessian is \(2\,\mathbb E[\tilde x\tilde x^\top]\), whose eigenvalues, by the moments in the proof of Proposition 2, are 2, with multiplicity 16, and \(2(1+\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2)=10\); gradient descent is safe only for \(\eta<0.2\). On one training set of 5,120 examples the largest eigenvalue is 9.955 and the threshold 0.2009. From \(\theta_0=0\), 300 steps at 0.9 times the threshold end within \(9.0\times10^{-16}\) of the least-squares solution, and 300 steps at 1.1 times the threshold end \(1.1\times10^{23}\) away from it (engineering check). A learning rate 10% above the threshold does not give a slightly worse answer; the iterates diverge. The threshold depends on the data through \(\nabla^2J\): multiplying every input by 10 multiplies the input block of the Hessian by 100. Exercise 5 shows that the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue also sets how fast a safe learning rate converges.

The proposition needs a quadratic cost. The cost of a neural network is not quadratic, and its Hessian changes with \(\theta\). Near a minimum the second-order Taylor expansion makes the cost approximately quadratic, and the proposition then describes the behaviour of gradient descent there, with the Hessian at that minimum, not along the whole path from the starting point.

Proposition 5 (a portfolio of attempts). Consider \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\) attempts and a probability \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\in(0,1)\).
(a) If the attempts succeed independently, each with probability \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\), then at least one succeeds with probability \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\); and in an unlimited sequence of such attempts, the expected number up to and including the first success is \(1/p_{\mathrm{succ}}\).
(b) If instead a premise shared by all attempts fails with probability \(p_{\mathrm{com}}\), in which case every attempt fails, and given that the premise holds the attempts succeed independently with probability \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\) each, then at least one succeeds with probability \((1-p_{\mathrm{com}})\bigl(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\bigr)\), which is less than \(1-p_{\mathrm{com}}\) for every \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\).

Proof. (a) By independence, all \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\) attempts fail with probability \((1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\); the complement is the first claim. The number of attempts up to the first success exceeds \(j\) exactly when the first \(j\) attempts fail, which has probability \((1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^j\); the expectation of a count with values \(1,2,\dots\) is the sum of these tail probabilities over \(j\ge0\), \(\sum_{j\ge0}(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^j=1/p_{\mathrm{succ}}\). (b) Condition on the premise. If it fails, the probability of a success is 0; if it holds, it is \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\) by (a). The law of total probability gives the product, and the second factor is less than 1. \(\blacksquare\)

The lecture gives no success probability, so take \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=0.1\) as an assumption of the example. Twenty independent attempts then succeed at least once with probability \(1-0.9^{20}=0.878\); reaching 0.9 takes 22 attempts, since \(\ln0.1/\ln0.9=21.9\); and a success takes 10 attempts on average. If a shared premise fails with probability \(p_{\mathrm{com}}=0.3\), twenty attempts succeed with probability \(0.7\times0.878=0.615\), and no number of attempts gives more than 0.7 (engineering check).

The lecture's own example is closer to case (b) than to case (a): twenty prototypes from one team usually share the data, the users and the judgment of what the users need, and more attempts cannot rescue a wrong shared premise. Attempts that vary the premise, such as different users or different data, lower \(p_{\mathrm{com}}\), which more attempts of the same kind cannot do. Under (a), the expected cost of one success is the cost of one attempt divided by \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\), so if the slide's tenfold speed-up of prototyping holds, the cost of each success falls tenfold at the same \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\).

A running-case laboratory in NumPy

Provenance. Original construction throughout. It implements Propositions 1–5 on the running case.

The program below checks the chapter's numbers in one run. Its inputs are fixed constants: the running case with \(n_x=16\) and \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2=2\), the training sizes 40 to 5,120, 200 training sets per size, and a seeded random generator. It has no side effects beyond printing. The function fit solves the normal equations of the squared-loss cost exactly and raises ValueError when \(m<n_{\mathrm{feat}}+1\), where the minimiser is not unique. The function err01 evaluates the exact 0-1 risk of a linear rule by the displayed formula for \(\mathcal R_{01}(b,w)\) in the running-case section, and risk_sq the exact squared-loss risk by the displayed formula for \(\mathcal R(b,w)\) that follows it, so no test-set sampling error enters the curves. Each check is an assert, so a wrong formula or a changed constant stops the program with an AssertionError. It needs Python 3 and NumPy (tested with Python 3.13.5 and NumPy 2.2.6) and ran in a fresh process in a few seconds.

The program does four things, in the order of the chapter. It measures the three learning curves and checks the inequalities of Proposition 1 on the averages and the floors of Proposition 2. It fits the power law of Proposition 3 to the four smallest sizes in the three ways of the forecasting section, forecasts the sizes for the targets 0.025 and 0.022, and measures the error reached at each forecast size. It runs gradient descent on one training set of 5,120 examples at 0.9 and 1.1 times the step-size threshold of Proposition 4. And it evaluates the two cases of Proposition 5.

import math
import numpy as np


def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))


N_X = 16
MU = np.full(N_X, 0.5)  # every coordinate 1/2, so ||mu||_2 = 2
SIZES = [40, 80, 160, 320, 640, 1280, 2560, 5120]
N_REP = 200
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)


def sample(m, n_feat, n_rep):
    """n_rep training sets of m examples; the model reads n_feat inputs."""
    y = rng.integers(0, 2, (n_rep, m)).astype(float)
    x = (2 * y - 1)[:, :, None] * MU[:n_feat]
    return x + rng.standard_normal((n_rep, m, n_feat)), y


def with_one(x):
    """Prepend the constant input 1 that multiplies the bias b."""
    return np.concatenate([np.ones(x.shape[:-1] + (1,)), x], axis=-1)


def fit(x, y):
    """Exact minimiser of the squared-loss cost J; returns (b, w)."""
    if x.shape[1] < x.shape[2] + 1:
        raise ValueError("need m >= n_feat + 1 for a unique minimiser")
    xt = with_one(x)
    xtt = xt.transpose(0, 2, 1)
    theta = np.linalg.solve(xtt @ xt, xtt @ y[:, :, None])[:, :, 0]
    return theta[:, 0], theta[:, 1:]


def err01(b, w, n_feat):
    """Exact 0-1 risk of the rule 'predict 1 when b + w.x > 1/2'."""
    s, n = w @ MU[:n_feat], np.linalg.norm(w, axis=1)
    miss1 = np.array([Phi(v) for v in (0.5 - b - s) / n])
    miss0 = np.array([Phi(v) for v in (b - s - 0.5) / n])
    return 0.5 * miss1 + 0.5 * miss0


def risk_sq(b, w, n_feat):
    """Exact squared-loss risk R(theta) of f(x) = b + w.x."""
    s = w @ MU[:n_feat]
    return (w * w).sum(1) + 0.5 * ((b + s - 1) ** 2 + (b - s) ** 2)


# 1. Learning curves Err(m); Propositions 1 and 2.
curves, floors, gap = {}, {}, {}
for n_feat in (1, 4, 16):
    floors[n_feat] = Phi(-np.linalg.norm(MU[:n_feat]))
    r_star = 0.25 / (1 + MU[:n_feat] @ MU[:n_feat])
    rows = []
    for m in SIZES:
        x, y = sample(m, n_feat, N_REP)
        b, w = fit(x, y)
        f = b[:, None] + (x @ w[:, :, None])[:, :, 0]
        j_train = ((f - y) ** 2).mean(1).mean()
        r_test = risk_sq(b, w, n_feat).mean()
        assert j_train < r_star < r_test  # Proposition 1, averaged
        gap[n_feat, m] = (j_train, r_star, r_test)
        rows.append(err01(b, w, n_feat).mean())
    curves[n_feat] = np.array(rows)
    assert np.all(np.diff(curves[n_feat]) < 0)
    assert 0 < curves[n_feat][-1] - floors[n_feat] < 0.002
assert abs(floors[16] - 0.02275) < 1e-5 and abs(floors[1] - 0.30854) < 1e-5
# 2. Proposition 3: fit Err = floor + Lam * m^(-xi) on sizes 40-320 only.
lm, pilot = np.log(SIZES), curves[16][:4]


def power_fit(floor):
    slope, icept = np.polyfit(lm[:4], np.log(pilot - floor), 1)
    return float(-slope), math.exp(icept)


def m_needed(target, floor, xi, lam):
    if target <= floor:
        return math.inf
    return (lam / (target - floor)) ** (1 / xi)


def log_misfit(floor):
    resid = np.log(pilot - floor) - np.polyval(
        np.polyfit(lm[:4], np.log(pilot - floor), 1), lm[:4])
    return float(np.sum(resid ** 2))


grid = np.linspace(0.0, pilot.min() - 1e-4, 2000)
fits = {"floor known": floors[16], "floor zero": 0.0,
        "floor estimated": float(min(grid, key=log_misfit))}
forecast, check = {}, {}
for name, fl in fits.items():
    for target in (0.025, 0.022):
        forecast[name, target] = m_needed(target, fl, *power_fit(fl))
for name in fits:  # measure Err at each forecast size for target 0.025
    m = math.ceil(forecast[name, 0.025])
    x, y = sample(m, 16, 2 * N_REP)
    check[name] = (m, float(err01(*fit(x, y), 16).mean()))
assert forecast["floor known", 0.022] == math.inf
assert forecast["floor zero", 0.022] < math.inf  # a target below the floor
assert check["floor zero"][1] > 0.026
assert abs(check["floor known"][1] - 0.025) < 0.001
assert fits["floor estimated"] > curves[16][6]  # reached at m = 2560
cross = m_needed(floors[16], 0.0, *power_fit(0.0))  # zero-floor fit
assert 340 < cross < 360
# 3. Proposition 4: gradient descent on J and the threshold 2 / eig_max.
x, y = sample(5120, 16, 1)
xt, y = with_one(x[0]), y[0]
eig_max = np.linalg.eigvalsh(2 * xt.T @ xt / 5120)[-1]
theta_ls = np.linalg.solve(xt.T @ xt, xt.T @ y)
dist = {}
for scale in (0.9, 1.1):
    eta, theta = scale * 2 / eig_max, np.zeros(17)
    for _ in range(300):

    dist[scale] = float(np.linalg.norm(theta - theta_ls))
assert abs(eig_max - 10) < 0.6 and dist[0.9] < 1e-6 and dist[1.1] > 1e3
# 4. Proposition 5: a portfolio of attempts, independent or common cause.
p_succ, n_try, p_com = 0.1, 20, 0.3
p_indep = 1 - (1 - p_succ) ** n_try
p_common = (1 - p_com) * p_indep
assert abs(p_indep - 0.8784) < 1e-4 and p_common < 1 - p_com
print("floors:", {k: round(v, 4) for k, v in floors.items()})
for n_feat, c in curves.items():
    print(f"Err, n_feat={n_feat}:", np.round(c, 4).tolist())
print("mean J, R*, mean R at m=40, n_feat=16:",
      tuple(round(float(v), 4) for v in gap[16, 40]))
excess = np.log(curves[16] - floors[16])
print("local exponents:", np.round(-np.diff(excess) / np.log(2), 2).tolist())
for name, fl in fits.items():
    xi, lam = power_fit(fl)
    print(f"{name}: floor {fl:.4f}, xi {xi:.4f}, Lam {lam:.4f}, m(0.025) "
          f"{forecast[name, 0.025]:.0f}, m(0.022) "
          f"{forecast[name, 0.022]:.0f}, Err at {check[name][0]} = "
          f"{check[name][1]:.4f}")
print("zero-floor fit reaches the true floor at m =", round(cross))

What the model captures and what it misses

Provenance. Original construction, with statements from Hestness et al. and Kaplan et al.

Each result of this chapter holds under an idealisation, and each idealisation fails in practice in a specific way.

The formal model. ERM and Proposition 1 assume examples drawn independently from one fixed distribution and an exact minimiser. Real data are collected in batches, drift over time and are filtered by the product that collects them; a network is trained by a finite number of gradient steps from a random start. Proposition 1 then holds only approximately, and a test set reused to choose among models stops being an unbiased estimate of the chosen model's risk.

The running case. Its models are linear and its classes Gaussian with identity covariance, so the floors, the exact 0-1 risk and the ERM solution all have closed forms. Capacity is restricted by the inputs a model reads, not by width or depth, and the exponents it produces are near 1. It shows the mechanism of floors and power-law approach; it does not show the exponents or the floors of any network.

Scaling measurements. The fits of Hestness et al. and Kaplan et al. are empirical over the ranges measured: two to three orders of magnitude of data in Hestness et al. (p. 4), two in Kaplan et al.'s data axis (p. 5). Kaplan et al.'s laws have no floor, and the authors expect them to break down beyond the measured scales (p. 17). Neither paper explains the exponents. Every extrapolation is a forecast.

Decision arithmetic. The break-even for fine-tuning uses assumed prices and omits quality, latency and maintenance. Proposition 5 assumes either independent attempts or one shared premise; real attempts share several premises to different degrees, and their success probabilities are not known in advance.

Claims not checked. The whiteboard sketch, the statements about older speech algorithms, the prototyping speed-ups, the hiring and productivity rankings and the anecdotes are speaker judgments recorded with their times; the price per million tokens is a product claim as of the recording date. They may be out of date.

What you can now do. You can state a supervised-learning problem as a dataset, a model family, a loss, a cost and a population risk; prove that the expected training cost of an exact ERM solution is at most its expected population risk; and say which estimate of the risk is unbiased. Exercises

The lecture has no mathematics of its own, but the model built in this chapter supports six exercises: a derivation with a computation (1), a computation with an assumption check on published fits (2), a counterexample (3), a derivation that diagnoses a fitting method (4), a derivation and computation about learning rates (5), and a design problem (6). Each is solvable from this chapter.

1. How precise is a test-set error?

A classifier with fixed parameters \(\theta\), chosen without looking at the test set, is evaluated on \(n_{\mathrm{test}}\) test examples drawn independently from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\). (a) Show that its test error is an unbiased estimate of \(\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)\) and find its standard deviation. (b) For \(\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)=0.025\), how large must \(n_{\mathrm{test}}\) be for the standard deviation to be at most 0.001? (c) Could a test set of 1,000 examples distinguish the errors 0.0255 and 0.025 of the forecasting section?

Solution

(a) Each test example contributes the indicator that the predicted label is wrong. Because \(\theta\) does not depend on the test set and the examples are independent draws from \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\), these indicators are independent Bernoulli variables with success probability \(\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)\). Their average has expectation \(\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)\), so it is unbiased, and variance \(\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)\bigl(1-\mathcal R_{01}(\theta)\bigr)/n_{\mathrm{test}}\), the variance of one indicator divided by the number of independent terms. The standard deviation is the square root, \(\sqrt{\mathcal R_{01}(1-\mathcal R_{01})/n_{\mathrm{test}}}\). (b) The condition is \(0.025\times0.975/n_{\mathrm{test}}\le10^{-6}\), so \(n_{\mathrm{test}}\ge24{,}375\). (c) With 1,000 examples the standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.024375/1000}=0.0049\), about ten times the difference 0.0005 between the two errors, so no. The running-case code avoids this noise by computing \(\mathcal R_{01}\) exactly from the Gaussian class distributions. A real learning curve must use a test set large enough for its differences, which is why Hestness et al. require a validation set "sufficiently large to approximate true generalization error with low variance" (p. 4).

2. Forecasting with published fits

Hestness et al. report for translation \(\mathrm{Err}_{208}(N_{\mathrm{tok}})=0.39+41.2\,N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{-0.36}\) and \(\mathrm{Err}_{512}(N_{\mathrm{tok}})=0.32+21.5\,N_{\mathrm{tok}}^{-0.30}\), with the training-set size in tokens (\(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\)), measured for \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\) between \(2^{20}\) and \(2^{27}\) (p. 6). (a) Compute both errors at \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}=2^{27}\). (b) How many tokens does the 512-unit model need to reach 0.36, and how does that compare with the largest measured size? (c) Can the 208-unit model reach 0.36? (d) What must be assumed for the answer to (b) to be correct?

Solution

(a) \((2^{27})^{-0.36}=2^{-9.72}=0.00119\), so \(\mathrm{Err}_{208}=41.2\times0.00119+0.39=0.439\). \((2^{27})^{-0.30}=2^{-8.1}=0.00364\), so \(\mathrm{Err}_{512}=21.5\times0.00364+0.32=0.398\). (b) By Proposition 3(c), with \(m\) read as \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}\), \(N_{\mathrm{tok}}=(21.5/(0.36-0.32))^{1/0.30}=537.5^{3.333}=1.26\times10^{9}\) tokens, about \(2^{30.2}\), that is \(1.26\times10^{9}/1.34\times10^{8}\approx9.4\) times the largest measured size \(2^{27}=1.34\times10^{8}\), a little over three more doublings. (c) No. Its floor is 0.39, above 0.36, so by Proposition 3(c) no amount of data reaches the target; only a model with a lower floor can. (d) That the fitted law continues to hold for about three more doublings beyond the measured range, with the same floor and exponent, for the same data distribution, model and training settings. That is a forecast, not a measurement. Hestness et al. themselves note that as training sets grow, optimisation becomes harder and models run out of capacity, so measured errors tend away from the power-law trend (p. 6). The running case of the forecasting section shows the same kind of forecast missing its target by 0.0001 to 0.0019 in error even under ideal conditions.

3. More attempts can be worth less than fewer independent ones

Using Proposition 5, compare five independent attempts, each succeeding with probability 0.1, with twenty attempts that share a premise failing with probability 0.6 and, given the premise, succeed independently with probability 0.1 each. Which portfolio is more likely to produce at least one success? What is the largest success probability any number of attempts of the second kind can reach?

Solution

Five independent attempts succeed at least once with probability \(1-0.9^5=1-0.59049=0.4095\) by Proposition 5(a). The twenty correlated attempts succeed with probability \((1-0.6)(1-0.9^{20})=0.4\times0.8784=0.3514\) by Proposition 5(b). The smaller independent portfolio is more likely to succeed, even though it has a quarter of the attempts. No number of attempts of the second kind exceeds \(1-0.6=0.4\), because with probability 0.6 the shared premise fails and every attempt fails with it. The counterexample shows that the size of a portfolio is not a measure of its chance of success when the attempts are correlated; what matters is how many independent premises it tests.

4. Why a pure power law misleads near a floor

(a) Derive part (d) of Proposition 3 again and evaluate the local log-log slope of the running case's 16-input model at \(m=40\) and \(m=320\), using the measured errors 0.0723 and 0.0261, the floor 0.0228 and the exponent 1.288 of the known-floor fit. (b) The zero-floor fit uses one slope, −0.484, for the whole range. Explain, from (a), why its extension beyond \(m=320\) lies below the measured curve. (c) Why can a zero-floor fit never report a target as unreachable?

Solution

(a) By the chain rule, \(d\log\mathrm{Err}/d\log m=(m/\mathrm{Err})\,d\mathrm{Err}/dm\), and \(d\mathrm{Err}/dm=-\xi\Lambda m^{-\xi-1}\), so the slope is \(-\xi\Lambda m^{-\xi}/\mathrm{Err}=-\xi(\mathrm{Err}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}})/\mathrm{Err}\). At \(m=40\): \(1-0.0228/0.0723=0.685\), so the slope is \(-1.288\times0.685=-0.88\). At \(m=320\): \(1-0.0228/0.0261=0.126\), so the slope is \(-1.288\times0.126=-0.16\). (b) The curve bends: its log-log slope flattens from −0.88 to −0.16 across the fitted range and keeps flattening toward 0 as \(\mathrm{Err}\to\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}\). A single straight line fitted to these points has a slope between the two ends, −0.484, steeper than the curve at \(m=320\) and beyond. Extended to larger \(m\), the line therefore falls faster than the curve and lies below it, which makes the fit optimistic: at its forecast size of 289 for the target 0.025, the measured error is 0.0269. (c) A zero-floor fit is \(\Lambda m^{-\xi}\), which is positive and tends to 0; by Proposition 3(c) with \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}=0\), every positive target is reached at a finite size. The fit cannot represent the floor that makes some targets impossible, as the forecast of 375 examples for the error 0.022 shows.

5. How fast does a safe learning rate converge?

(a) For the running case's 4-input model, find the eigenvalues of the population Hessian \(2\,\mathbb E[\tilde x\tilde x^\top]\) and the learning-rate threshold of Proposition 4. (b) With \(\eta=0.4\), by what factor does the distance to \(\hat\theta\) shrink at least, per step, and how many steps guarantee a reduction by \(10^{-6}\)? (c) Which fixed \(\eta\) makes the worst factor smallest, and how many steps does it need? (d) Repeat (c) for the 16-input model and explain the difference.

Solution

(a) By the moments in the proof of Proposition 2, \(\mathbb E[\tilde x\tilde x^\top]\) is block-diagonal with a 1 for the constant input and \(I+\mu_{1:4}\mu_{1:4}^\top\) for the inputs. The second block has eigenvalue \(1+\lVert\mu_{1:4}\rVert_2^2=2\) along \(\mu_{1:4}\) and 1 on the three directions orthogonal to it. The Hessian's eigenvalues are therefore 2, with multiplicity 4, and 4, and the threshold is \(\eta<2/4=0.5\). (b) The factors are \(\lvert1-0.4\times2\rvert=0.2\) and \(\lvert1-0.4\times4\rvert=0.6\). By the proof of Proposition 4, every component shrinks at least by 0.6 per step, so the distance does too, and after \(t\) steps the distance has shrunk at least by \(0.6^{t}\), which is at most \(10^{-6}\) when \(t\ge\ln10^{-6}/\ln0.6=27.05\), that is after 28 steps. (c) The worst factor \(\max(\lvert1-2\eta\rvert,\lvert1-4\eta\rvert)\) is smallest when \(1-2\eta=4\eta-1\), at \(\eta=1/3\), with factor 1/3; then \(t\ge\ln10^{-6}/\ln(1/3)=12.6\), that is 13 steps. (d) For 16 inputs the eigenvalues are 2 and 10; the best \(\eta=2/12=1/6\) gives the factor \(1-2/6=2/3\) and needs \(\ln10^{-6}/\ln(2/3)=34.1\), that is 35 steps. In general the best factor is \((\mathrm{eig}_{\max}-\mathrm{eig}_{\min})/(\mathrm{eig}_{\max}+\mathrm{eig}_{\min})\), which grows with the ratio of the largest to the smallest eigenvalue. The 16 inputs share one signal direction, which makes the largest eigenvalue five times the smallest instead of twice. These counts use the population Hessian; a finite training set has a slightly different one, as the value 9.955 in the chapter shows.

6. Designing the "try a little data" experiment

A team has 300 labelled specimens from a newly invented medical device and must decide whether to fund the collection of 3,000 more. Design a learning-curve experiment in the spirit of the lecture's advice (≈2025–2082 s). State how you would split the data, which sizes you would train on, how you would fit and report the curve, the decision rule, and what the experiment cannot establish.

Solution

Split first. Set aside a test set before any training, since Proposition 1 shows that data used to choose parameters give an optimistic estimate. With an error near 0.1, a test set of 100 has standard deviation \(\sqrt{0.09/100}=0.03\) by exercise 1, too coarse to resolve a curve; so use repeated random splits (for example ten splits into 200 for training and 100 for testing) and average, noting that overlapping splits reduce the noise by less than a factor of \(\sqrt{10}\). Train on nested subsets of 25, 50, 100 and 200 examples within each training part, several random subsets per size, and record the mean test error and its spread at each size. Fit \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{irr}}+\Lambda m^{-\xi}\) in two ways, with the floor set to a bound from outside the data (for example the error of specialists who see the same device output, following Hestness et al., p. 13) and with the floor estimated, and report the forecast for 3,300 examples as the range between the fits, not as one number. Decision rule: fund the collection if even the pessimistic fit forecasts a useful improvement at 3,300 examples; if the measured errors are already within their noise of the estimated floor, fund instead a change that lowers the floor (a model that reads more of the device output, better labels) and repeat the experiment on it. The experiment cannot establish that the power law continues beyond 200 examples, that the 3,000 new specimens will come from the same distribution (a new device batch, clinic or patient group changes \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\), chapter 3), or that the smallest sizes are already past the small-data region (Hestness et al., p. 12). The running case of the forecasting section shows that even ideal data can mislead a fit from four sizes by a noticeable margin.
