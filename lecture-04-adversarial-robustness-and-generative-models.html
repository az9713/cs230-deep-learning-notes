Attacking a fixed network, and training a network to generate

A trained classifier is a fixed function. This chapter asks three questions about such functions. How can an attacker choose an input, a training set, or an instruction so that the function returns an output the attacker wants? What does each defense assume about the attacker? And how can a network instead be trained so that its outputs are new samples from the distribution of the training data?

The first question has a precise answer for a linear score: the worst perturbation inside a norm ball is given by a duality inequality, and in high dimension a perturbation that is small in every coordinate moves the score by an amount that grows with the number of coordinates. The third question has two answers in this chapter. A generative adversarial network (GAN) trains a generator against a classifier of real and generated data. A diffusion model trains one network to predict the noise that a fixed corruption process added, and then generates by running the corruption backward. Both answers are derived here from their objectives, and both are tested on one small distribution for which every optimal quantity is known in closed form.

One fixed function, four things to change 0:05

Provenance. Lecture: the two topics of the lecture, the statement that the "adversarial" of GANs is unrelated to adversarial attacks, audience examples of attacks, the periods on slide 4 and the growth of entry points for agents with retrieval (≈5–521 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the formal model, the threat-model table and the running case.

A deployed image classifier takes an input \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\), a vector of \(n_x\) pixel intensities, and returns a prediction. For two classes the network \(f_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\) computes a real number \(u=f_\theta(x)\), the logit, and reports the probability \(\hat y=\sigma(u)\) of class 1, where \(\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})\) is the logistic sigmoid. For \(K\) classes it computes a logit \(u_k\) per class \(k\) and reports \(\mathrm{softmax}(u)_k=e^{u_k}/\sum_{j=1}^{K}e^{u_j}\). Training chose \(\theta\) by gradient descent with step size \(\eta>0\) on the cost \(J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\mathcal L(\hat y^{(i)},y^{(i)})\), the average per-example loss over \(m\) labelled examples \((x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\). For binary labels the loss is the binary cross-entropy \(\mathcal L(\hat y,y)=-y\log\hat y-(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\), measured in nats. Its derivative with respect to the logit is

\[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u}=-y(1-\sigma(u))+(1-y)\sigma(u)=\hat y-y,\]

because \(d\sigma/du=\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))\). Chapter 2 builds these objects in full; this chapter uses only the three formulas above.

After training, \(\theta\) is fixed. The chapter studies four ways to change what this fixed system does. An attacker can change the input at test time; the result is an adversarial example. An attacker can change the training set before training; the result is a poisoned or backdoored model. When the model is a language model, an attacker can change the instructions the model reads; the result is a prompt injection. The builder, finally, can replace the classifier by a network whose outputs are themselves inputs of the kind in the training set; that is a generative model. The word "adversarial" appears in two unrelated places below. An adversarial example is an input chosen against a fixed model. A generative adversarial network is a training objective in which two networks compete; nobody attacks anybody. The recording separates the two at ≈115–141 s, and this chapter keeps them apart.

A threat model states what the attacker can change, what the attacker knows, and what outcome the attacker wants. A defense is meaningful only relative to a threat model, because a defense that holds against one attacker can be irrelevant to another. The three attacks of this chapter differ in what the attacker changes:

The running case: two Gaussian clusters

Every mechanism below is tested on one data distribution for which the optimal classifier, the optimal attack, the optimal discriminator and the optimal noise predictor can all be written down. Fix the input dimension \(n_x\) and the vector \(\mu\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\) whose every coordinate equals \(2/\sqrt{n_x}\), so that \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2=2\) for every \(n_x\). A label \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is drawn with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Given \(y=1\) the input is \(x\sim\mathcal N(\mu,I)\); given \(y=0\) it is \(x\sim\mathcal N(-\mu,I)\). Here \(\mathcal N(\mu,I)\) is the Gaussian with mean \(\mu\) and identity covariance \(I\). The input distribution is therefore

\[p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)=\tfrac12\,\mathcal N(x;\mu,I)+\tfrac12\,\mathcal N(x;-\mu,I).\]

With \(n_x=2\) this is a picture of two round clouds centred at \(\pm(\sqrt2,\sqrt2)\approx\pm(1.414,1.414)\). With \(n_x=3072=32\cdot32\cdot3\) it has the dimension of a small colour image, and each coordinate carries only \(2/\sqrt{3072}\approx0.036\) of signal against unit noise. That is the regime of pixels: each one is weakly informative, and the class is legible only from many of them together.

Proposition 1 (Bayes classifier of the running case). The probability of class 1 given \(x\) is \(\sigma(u(x))\) with logit \(u(x)=w^\top x\), where \(w=2\mu\) and the bias is zero. The rule "predict 1 when \(u(x)>0\)" has accuracy \(\Phi(\lVert\mu\rVert_2)=\Phi(2)=0.977\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function.

Proof. By Bayes' rule with equal priors, the log-odds are

\[\log\mathcal N(x;\mu,I)-\log\mathcal N(x;-\mu,I)=-\tfrac12\lVert x-\mu\rVert_2^2+\tfrac12\lVert x+\mu\rVert_2^2=2\mu^\top x;\]

the normalising constants and the \(\lVert x\rVert_2^2\) terms cancel. For accuracy, take \(y=1\), so \(x=\mu+\chi\), where \(\chi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) is a standard Gaussian vector. Then \(\mu^\top x=\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2+\mu^\top\chi\), and \(\mu^\top\chi\) is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2\). The prediction is correct when \(\mu^\top x>0\), which has probability \(\Phi(\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2/\lVert\mu\rVert_2)=\Phi(2)\). The case \(y=0\) is symmetric. \(\blacksquare\)

The optimal classifier is a single logistic neuron, the same architecture as the lecture's worked example on slide 13. The chapter uses \(w=2\mu\) directly and does not train anything; every later number about this classifier is a property of the best possible classifier, not of an optimisation accident.

Forging an input by descending on the pixels 8:41

Provenance. Lecture: the untargeted "iguana" objective, gradient descent on the input with the network frozen, the size of the input space, and the targeted "cat that is classified as an iguana" objective (slides 5–9, ≈521–1181 s). Research extension: the findings and the penalty formulation of Szegedy et al. Original construction: the input-gradient formula for one neuron, the support argument, and the minimal-distance computation for the running case.

The first findings on adversarial examples are those of Szegedy et al. (2013): an "imperceptible non-random perturbation" of a test image can change a network's prediction arbitrarily (p. 2), and the same perturbation often fools a second network trained with different hyperparameters or on a different subset of the data (pp. 1–2) (empirical result). This section builds the attack that produces such inputs.

The untargeted objective

Take a network pretrained on ImageNet and a class it knows, "iguana". The attacker wants any input \(x\) that the network labels "iguana". Let \(y_{\mathrm{tgt}}\) be the one-hot vector of that class and \(\hat y(x)\in[0,1]^K\) the network's softmax output. Slide 5 writes the goal as a loss to minimise over the input,

\[\min_x\ \tfrac12\bigl\lVert\hat y(x)-y_{\mathrm{tgt}}\bigr\rVert_2^2,\]

with the parameters \(\theta\) held fixed. The optimiser is ordinary gradient descent, \(x\leftarrow x-\eta\,\nabla_x\mathcal L\); slide 5 writes the step size as \(\alpha\), which this book reserves for diffusion. What changes relative to training is the variable. Backpropagation computes the derivative of the loss with respect to every quantity in the computational graph, and the input is one of those quantities; stopping the chain rule at \(x\) instead of at \(\theta\) costs one extra matrix product at the first layer.

For one logistic neuron with target \(y_{\mathrm{tgt}}=1\) the computation is short. The loss is \(\tfrac12(\sigma(u)-1)^2\) with \(u=w^\top x+b\), so by the chain rule

\[\nabla_x\mathcal L=(\sigma(u)-1)\,\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))\,w.\]

The step \(-\eta\nabla_x\mathcal L\) is a positive multiple of \(w\): it moves the input in the direction that raises the logit fastest. The scalar in front shrinks to zero when \(\sigma(u)\) is near 0 or near 1, which is why attacks usually use the cross-entropy loss instead, whose derivative at the logit is \(\hat y-y\) and does not vanish when the model is confidently wrong.

Why the forged input does not look like an iguana

Slide 6 asks whether the result will look like an iguana, and the answer in the recording is that it almost certainly will not (≈795–966 s). Two facts support that answer. The first is a count. An image of \(32\times32\times3=3072\) values, each taking one of 256 intensity levels, is one of \(256^{3072}\) possible inputs. Because \(3072\log_{10}256=3072\times2.40824=7398.1\), the count is \(256^{3072}\approx1.3\times10^{7398}\) (engineering check; slide 6 rounds it to \(10^{7400}\); the recording's count of 256 refers to intensity levels per value, not to pixels).

The second fact explains why the count matters. The training cost \(J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_i\mathcal L(f_\theta(x^{(i)}),y^{(i)})\) depends on \(f_\theta\) only through its values at the \(m\) training inputs, and the population risk depends on it only through its values on the support of the data distribution. Nothing in either quantity constrains what the network outputs at an input far from the data. The set of inputs labelled "iguana" is therefore whatever the architecture happens to produce outside the data, and in a space of \(10^{7398}\) points most of it can consist of inputs no camera would record. Gradient descent from a random start stops at the first point of that set it reaches, and there is no term in the objective that pulls it toward the small region of natural images. Slide 6 draws exactly this: a large ellipse of possible inputs, a small region of real images, and a region "classified as iguana" that overlaps the real images only in part.

The targeted objective

An input that looks like noise is easy for a person to reject. The more dangerous attack produces an image that a person still labels "cat" while the network labels it "iguana". Slide 7 adds a penalty that keeps the forged input near a chosen cat image \(x_{\mathrm{cat}}\):

\[\min_x\ \tfrac12\bigl\lVert\hat y(x)-y_{\mathrm{tgt}}\bigr\rVert_2^2+\lambda_{\mathrm{tgt}}\lVert x-x_{\mathrm{cat}}\rVert_2^2,\qquad \lambda_{\mathrm{tgt}}\ge0.\]

The coefficient \(\lambda_{\mathrm{tgt}}\) trades the two goals: at \(\lambda_{\mathrm{tgt}}=0\) the problem is the untargeted one, and as \(\lambda_{\mathrm{tgt}}\) grows the minimiser is pulled back to \(x_{\mathrm{cat}}\) itself. Starting the descent at \(x=x_{\mathrm{cat}}\), as the recording suggests (≈1113–1145 s), usually shortens it. This is the construction of Szegedy et al., who seek the smallest perturbation \(r\) with \(f(x+r)=l\) for a target label \(l\), and approximate it by minimising \(c\lvert r\rvert+\mathrm{loss}_f(x+r,l)\) subject to \(x+r\in[0,1]^{m}\), with a line search on the penalty weight \(c\) (p. 5). Their box constraint keeps the forged image a valid image; the slide's objective omits it, and an implementation must clip or project the pixels back into range.

Slide 8 shows a cat photograph labelled "92% Cat" beside a perturbed copy labelled "94% Iguana". The perturbed copy carries visible colour grain. The example demonstrates the targeted objective; it does not demonstrate an imperceptible perturbation, and the slide gives no source for the image pair.

How far is the nearest wrong answer? A computation in the running case

For a linear logit \(u(x)=w^\top x+b\), the closest point to \(x\) (in Euclidean distance) at which the prediction flips lies on the hyperplane \(u=0\). For any perturbation \(\delta\), \(u(x+\delta)-u(x)=w^\top\delta\), and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives \(\lvert w^\top\delta\rvert\le\lVert w\rVert_2\lVert\delta\rVert_2\). A perturbation that reaches the boundary must change \(u\) by \(\lvert u(x)\rvert\), so its length is at least \(\lvert u(x)\rvert/\lVert w\rVert_2\). The perturbation \(\delta=-u(x)\,w/\lVert w\rVert_2^2\) has exactly that length and gives \(u(x+\delta)=u(x)-u(x)=0\). The distance is therefore \(\lvert u(x)\rvert/\lVert w\rVert_2\). (The linearity section meets this inequality again as the \(\ell_2\) case of Proposition 2.) In the running case take the class-1 mean itself, \(x=\mu\): its logit is \(2\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2=8\), \(\lVert w\rVert_2=4\), and the distance to the boundary is \(8/4=2=\lVert\mu\rVert_2\). This distance does not depend on \(n_x\). Measured in Euclidean length, the typical class-1 input is as far from the wrong answer in 3072 dimensions as in 2. The next two sections show that this is the wrong length to measure.

Attacks that survive a camera, and attacks without the gradient 19:41

Provenance. Lecture: printed adversarial images photographed by a phone, the person-hiding patch and its loss terms, transfer to other models, rate-limited queries and substitute models (slides 10–12, ≈1181–1464 s). Research extension: the findings and loss definitions of Kurakin et al., Thys et al. and Papernot et al. Original construction: the definitions of white-box and black-box access as used here.

An attacker who knows the architecture and the parameters \(\theta\) can compute \(\nabla_x\mathcal L\) exactly; this is white-box access. An attacker who can only submit inputs and read outputs has black-box access. The recording uses the terms from ≈1435 s and defines them at ≈1995–2029 s. Everything in the previous section assumed white-box access.

Kurakin, Goodfellow and Bengio (2016) asked whether perturbations survive the physical world. They printed clean and adversarial ImageNet images, photographed the printouts with a phone, and classified the photographs; "a large fraction of adversarial examples" remained misclassified (p. 1, p. 3; empirical result). Their Figure 1 shows a clean printed image recognised as a "washer" and adversarial versions misclassified (p. 1). Slide 10 shows a still that the recording presents as a demonstration of that work; the still shows two printed bookshelf photographs and no labels, so the "library" and "prison" predictions come from the recording's narration (≈1181–1219 s), not from the page. The recording dates the work to 2017, the year of its ICLR workshop version; the arXiv version is 2016.

A printed patch must survive more than printing. Thys, Van Ranst and Goedemé (2019) optimised a patch that hides a person from the YOLOv2 detector. Their loss is a weighted sum of three terms, with two weights set empirically (p. 3). The terms are a non-printability score, which penalises colours far from a set of printable colours; a total-variation term, which penalises differences between neighbouring patch pixels and so favours smooth colour transitions; and the maximum objectness score the detector assigns in the image, which the patch must drive down. They note that earlier physical attacks targeted classes "that contain almost no intra-class variety (e.g. stop signs)", while people vary in pose, clothing and size (p. 1). Slide 11 shows the result: of two people in one frame, the one without the patch is boxed as "person" and the one holding the printed patch is not boxed. The example shows the general method of this chapter in full: write the attacker's constraints as differentiable penalty terms, then descend.

A black-box attacker cannot compute \(\nabla_x\mathcal L\). The recording describes two responses (≈1412–1464 s). With enough queries, gradients can be estimated from output differences; a service limits this by capping queries per minute (speaker judgment; no number is given). Without enough queries, the attacker trains a substitute model on a similar task, forges examples against it with white-box methods, and sends them to the target. Papernot et al. (2016) trained the substitute on inputs the attacker synthesised and labelled by querying the target, and found that a deep network hosted by MetaMind misclassified 84.24% of the adversarial examples crafted with the substitute (p. 1; empirical result for that service in 2016). Transfer is the property that makes this work, and Szegedy et al. had already observed it (p. 2). Its explanation is the subject of the next section.

Why a nearly linear model is fragile in high dimension 24:24

Provenance. Lecture: the rejected nonlinearity explanation, the claim that dimension is the cause, the six-weight neuron of slide 13, the fast gradient sign method of slide 14 (≈1464–1995 s). Research extension: the linearity argument, the FGSM definition and its measured effect in Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy. Original construction: Proposition 2 and its proof, Figure 1, the running-case robustness formula and its table, and the corrections to slide 13.

Early explanations of adversarial examples blamed the strong nonlinearity of deep networks. Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy (2014) argued instead that "linear behavior in high-dimensional spaces is sufficient to cause adversarial examples" (p. 1). They observed that ReLU networks (chapter 2) and maxout networks (networks whose hidden units output the maximum of several linear functions of their input) are "intentionally designed to behave in very linear ways" to make them easy to optimise (p. 2). The recording presents the same argument (≈1639–1723 s). The slide cites the paper as 2015, the year of its ICLR publication; the arXiv version is from December 2014. The argument rests on a single inequality.

Proposition 2 (worst-case change of a linear score). Let \(w\in\mathbb R^{n_x}\) and \(\rho>0\). Over all perturbations \(\delta\) with \(\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty=\max_j\lvert\delta_j\rvert\le\rho\), the largest value of \(w^\top\delta\) is \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_1=\rho\sum_j\lvert w_j\rvert\), attained at \(\delta=\rho\,\mathrm{sign}(w)\). Over all \(\delta\) with \(\lVert\delta\rVert_2\le\rho\), the largest value is \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_2\), attained at \(\delta=\rho\,w/\lVert w\rVert_2\).

Proof. For the first budget, \(w^\top\delta=\sum_j w_j\delta_j\le\sum_j\lvert w_j\rvert\lvert\delta_j\rvert\le\rho\sum_j\lvert w_j\rvert\), and \(\delta_j=\rho\,\mathrm{sign}(w_j)\) makes both inequalities equalities. For the second, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives \(w^\top\delta\le\lVert w\rVert_2\lVert\delta\rVert_2\le\rho\lVert w\rVert_2\), with equality when \(\delta\) is a nonnegative multiple of \(w\) of length \(\rho\). \(\blacksquare\)

The two maximisers point in different directions unless all \(\lvert w_j\rvert\) are equal. Figure 1 draws both for \(w=(1,3)\): the \(\ell_2\) step goes straight along \(w\) and gains \(\sqrt{10}\,\rho\approx3.16\rho\); the \(\ell_\infty\) step goes to the corner of the square and gains \(4\rho\). The general form of the proposition is Hölder's inequality with the dual norms \((\infty,1)\) and \((2,2)\); exercise 1 treats the \((1,\infty)\) pair.

Dimension is the lever

Proposition 2 shows how the budget and the dimension interact. An \(\ell_\infty\) budget \(\rho\) moves the logit by \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_1\), which is \(\rho\,n_x\) times the mean absolute weight: the change grows linearly in the number of coordinates, while no single coordinate moves by more than \(\rho\). Goodfellow et al. state that the activation grows by the product of the budget, the average weight magnitude and the dimension (p. 2), and conclude that "we can make many infinitesimal changes to the input that add up to one large change to the output" (p. 2).

The running case turns the statement into an exact accuracy. The attacker uses the worst \(\ell_\infty\) perturbation of Proposition 2 against the Bayes classifier of Proposition 1.

Proposition 3 (robust accuracy of the running case). Under an \(\ell_\infty\) attack of budget \(\rho\), the accuracy of the Bayes classifier falls from \(\Phi(\lVert\mu\rVert_2)\) to \(\Phi\bigl((\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2-\rho\lVert\mu\rVert_1)/\lVert\mu\rVert_2\bigr)\). With \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2=2\) and \(\lVert\mu\rVert_1=2\sqrt{n_x}\), the attacked accuracy is \(\Phi(2-\rho\sqrt{n_x})\).

Proof. Take \(y=1\), \(x=\mu+\chi\) with \(\chi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). By Proposition 2 the perturbation that lowers \(\mu^\top x\) most is \(-\rho\,\mathrm{sign}(\mu)\), which lowers it by \(\rho\lVert\mu\rVert_1\). The attacked score \(\mu^\top(x+\delta)=\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2-\rho\lVert\mu\rVert_1+\mu^\top\chi\) is Gaussian with standard deviation \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2\), and the prediction stays correct when the score is positive. The case \(y=0\) is symmetric. \(\blacksquare\)

The boundary of this argument must be stated with it. The running case assumes that the class signal is spread evenly and weakly over many coordinates and that the classifier is exactly linear. Deep networks are not linear, natural images do not have independent pixels, and a real attack budget is measured in intensity levels. Proposition 3 proves that dimension alone suffices to make a correct, well-calibrated linear classifier fragile; it does not prove that dimension is the only cause in a deep network. Goodfellow et al. support that step with experiments, not a theorem.

The fast gradient sign method

For a nonlinear network the attacker linearises. Let \(\mathcal L(\theta,x,y)\) be the training loss. To first order in \(\delta\), \(\mathcal L(\theta,x+\delta,y)\approx\mathcal L(\theta,x,y)+\nabla_x\mathcal L^\top\delta\). Maximising the linear term over \(\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty\le\rho\) is Proposition 2 with \(w=\nabla_x\mathcal L\), so

\[x_{\mathrm{adv}}=x+\rho\,\mathrm{sign}\bigl(\nabla_x\mathcal L(\theta,x,y)\bigr).\]

This is the fast gradient sign method (FGSM) of Goodfellow et al. (p. 3). It costs one backward pass, needs no iteration, and is exact only when the loss is linear in \(x\) over the ball. For logistic regression with label \(y=1\), \(\nabla_x\mathcal L=(\hat y-1)w\), so \(\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x\mathcal L)=-\mathrm{sign}(w)\), as Goodfellow et al. note (p. 4); FGSM then coincides with the worst \(\ell_\infty\) attack of Proposition 3. On MNIST, with budget 0.25 on pixel values in \([0,1]\), FGSM caused a shallow softmax classifier to err on 99.9% of test inputs and a maxout network on 89.4% (p. 3; empirical result). An attacker who iterates the step and projects back into the ball after each iteration obtains projected gradient descent, the attack that Madry et al. recommend as the strongest attack that uses only first-order information (p. 2, p. 4).

Adversarial training as a min–max problem

Slide 16 writes the adversarial-training loss as \(L_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathcal L(\theta,x,y)+\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}\,\mathcal L(\theta,x_{\mathrm{adv}},y)\), with \(x_{\mathrm{adv}}\) produced by FGSM from the current parameters and the label \(y\) left unchanged. Goodfellow et al. use the same construction with weights of one half on the clean and on the adversarial loss in all experiments (p. 5), which equals the slide's loss with \(\lambda_{\mathrm{adv}}=1\) up to the factor \(\tfrac12\). On MNIST this training lowered the maxout network's error on FGSM examples from 89.4% to 17.9% (p. 5; empirical result for that model and attack). The derivative of \(\mathrm{sign}\) is zero almost everywhere, so the gradient of this loss treats \(x_{\mathrm{adv}}\) as a constant and does not anticipate how the attack will move when \(\theta\) changes (Goodfellow et al., p. 6).

The cleaner formulation is Madry et al.'s saddle-point problem,

\[\min_\theta\ \mathbb E_{(x,y)}\Bigl[\max_{\lVert\delta\rVert_\infty\le\rho}\mathcal L(\theta,x+\delta,y)\Bigr]\]

(p. 3, eq. 2.1; they write the allowed set as \(S\)). The inner maximum is the attack; the outer minimum is training. FGSM is one linearised step toward the inner maximum (p. 4), and the slide's loss replaces the inner maximum by that step and averages it with the clean loss. Two further methods on the slides are different objects: adversarial logit pairing adds a term that makes the logits of a clean example and of its adversarial counterpart similar (Kannan, Kurakin and Goodfellow, p. 1), and it is not the same as the mixed loss above. The survey table that slide 17 reproduces lists logit pairing as circumventable by PGD with many iterations (Wiyatno et al., p. 53; empirical result as reported by the survey — the cited study is not among this chapter's sources and was not checked here).

For a linear logit the inner maximum is exact. Write the binary cross-entropy in terms of the margin \((2y-1)u\), which is positive exactly when the prediction is correct: \(\mathcal L=\log\bigl(1+e^{-(2y-1)u}\bigr)\). The attacker lowers the margin \((2y-1)(w^\top x+b)\) by at most \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_1\) (Proposition 2). Since the loss decreases in the margin, the inner maximum equals

\[\log\Bigl(1+\exp\bigl(-(2y-1)(w^\top x+b)+\rho\lVert w\rVert_1\bigr)\Bigr).\]

Adversarial training of a linear model is therefore ordinary logistic regression with the margin reduced by \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_1\), which penalises large \(\ell_1\) norms of the weights; Goodfellow et al. derive the same expression and compare it with \(\ell_1\) regularisation (p. 4). In the running case this makes the trade-off visible. Robustness at budget \(\rho\) requires a small \(\lVert w\rVert_1\) relative to the margin, while the most accurate direction, \(w\propto\mu\), has \(\lVert w\rVert_1=\sqrt{n_x}\lVert w\rVert_2\), the largest ratio any vector can have.

Backdoors: poisoning the training set 39:00

Provenance. Lecture: the trigger patch on slide 18, deployment of a backdoored model, the face-verification example, text backdoors, and the defenses offered in answer to a question (≈2340–2686 s). Research extension: BadNets. Original construction: the population argument that empirical risk minimisation learns the backdoor at no cost in clean accuracy.

A backdoor attacker changes the training set. Slide 18 shows the construction: a small red square, the trigger, is pasted on some cat photographs, which are then labelled "dog"; the same square also appears on some dog photographs, which keep their correct label "dog". After training, a cat photograph that carries the square is classified "dog". The attacker publishes the model, and anyone who deploys it inherits the behaviour. Gu, Dolan-Gavitt and Garg (2017) define a backdoored network as one with "state-of-the-art performance on the user's training and validation samples" that "behaves badly on specific attacker-chosen inputs" (p. 1). They built a U.S. street-sign classifier that labels stop signs as speed limits when a sticker is added, and found that the backdoor persisted after the network was retrained for another task, causing an average accuracy drop of 25% when the trigger was present (p. 1; empirical result).

Why training learns the trigger

The mechanism is a correlation that the attacker makes perfect. Extend each input of the running case by one extra coordinate \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}\in\{0,1\}\), the trigger. Clean examples have \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}=0\). The attacker adds a fraction of examples drawn from the class-0 cluster \(\mathcal N(-\mu,I)\) with \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}=1\) and label 1, as well as class-1 examples with \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}=1\) and label 1, matching slide 18's triggered dogs. In the poisoned distribution every example with \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}=1\) has label 1, so \(\mathbb P(y=1\mid x,x_{\mathrm{trig}}=1)=1\). Every example with \(x_{\mathrm{trig}}=0\) is clean, so \(\mathbb P(y=1\mid x,x_{\mathrm{trig}}=0)=\sigma(2\mu^\top x)\), the clean posterior of Proposition 1.

The Bayes-optimal classifier of the poisoned distribution is therefore the clean Bayes classifier when the trigger is absent and the constant "class 1" when it is present. Its accuracy on clean validation data is exactly the clean optimum, 0.977. A model trained to minimise risk on the poisoned data is pushed toward precisely the backdoored function, and a clean validation set, however large, cannot distinguish it from an honest model, because the two agree wherever the validation data lie. This is the population version of BadNets' observation, and it explains why the attack costs the attacker nothing in measured accuracy.

The recording distinguishes prompt injection from jailbreaking: injection is an attack through the text channel on an application's instructions, while jailbreaking denotes any method that gets a model to override its intended behaviour (speaker judgment, ≈2945–2974 s). With the threat-model table in mind, prompt injection is an input attack whose "input" is text the model interprets as commands. From recognizing data to generating it 49:35

Provenance. Lecture: use cases, discriminative versus generative models, the self-supervised setting, the claim that a model with fewer parameters than data cannot overfit, and distribution matching (slides 21–24, ≈2975–3375 s). Research extension: memorisation in diffusion models (Carlini et al.). Original construction: the definitions of sampler and induced distribution, and the running-case target.

A classifier learns the conditional distribution \(p(y\mid x)\), or only a decision rule derived from it. A generative model learns about the input distribution \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\) itself. Two different things can be meant: a model that evaluates a density \(p_\theta(x)\) at any point, and a model that produces samples. The two families in this chapter are samplers. A GAN's generator is a deterministic network \(G:\mathbb R^{n_z}\to\mathbb R^{n_x}\) applied to a random latent code \(z\) drawn from a fixed prior \(p_z\), usually \(\mathcal N(0,I)\). The distribution of \(G(z)\) is the generated distribution \(p_g\); \(G\) never evaluates it. The goal of training, drawn on slide 24 as two outlined regions in image space, one for the real data and one for the generated images, that must be brought into coincidence, is \(p_g=p_{\mathrm{data}}\). In the running case the target is the two-cluster mixture: a successful generator produces points near \(+\mu\) and near \(-\mu\) in equal proportion, with unit spread.

The recording lists uses: text-to-image synthesis, super-resolution, inpainting (a student project removed people from drone footage and filled the gap, ≈3183–3238 s), audio, code and video generation, and synthetic medical data that hospitals can share. The last comes with the statement that a model trained on the synthetic data performs about as well as one trained on the real data (≈3050–3076 s); no study is named, and the statement is speaker judgment. The training signal is self-supervised: the data are their own target, and no human labels are needed.

Slide 23 explains why the approach works by the number of model parameters being much smaller than the amount of data: a model too small to memorise its data is forced to learn the data's salient features (speaker judgment, ≈3238–3266 s). The heuristic has a documented failure. Carlini et al. (2023) show that diffusion models "memorize individual images from their training data and emit them at generation time", and extract "over a thousand training examples" from state-of-the-art models, from photographs of individual people to trademarked logos (p. 1; empirical result). Parameter count is not a measure of the capacity to reproduce training data; duplicated or distinctive examples can be stored by a model with far fewer parameters than the dataset has numbers.

The generator–discriminator game 56:15

Provenance. Lecture: generator from a 100-dimensional code to a \(64\times64\times3\) image, discriminator as a binary classifier, gradient flow through \(D\) into \(G\), the costs of slide 29, cold start (slides 26–29, ≈3375–3964 s). Research extension: the value function, Proposition 1 and Theorem 1 of Goodfellow et al. (2014), the zero-sum definition in the NIPS 2016 tutorial. Original construction: the proofs as written here, the proof of \(\mathrm{KL}\ge0\), and the running-case check.

Slides 26–28 set up the game. The generator \(G\) maps a code \(z\in\mathbb R^{100}\) to an image of shape \((64,64,3)\), which has \(12{,}288\) entries; its output is larger than its input, so it must upsample (the recording defers the upsampling layers to a later lecture, ≈3405–3462 s). The discriminator \(D:\mathbb R^{n_x}\to(0,1)\) is a binary classifier with logit \(u(x)\) and output \(D(x)=\sigma(u(x))\), trained to output 1 on real images and 0 on generated ones. The generator is trained to make \(D\) output 1 on its images. Gradients of the generator's cost pass through the frozen discriminator into the generator's parameters, exactly as the input-gradient of the forging section passed through a frozen classifier into the pixels; here the pixels are themselves the output of a second network.

The two costs of slide 29

With a minibatch of \(n_{\mathrm{real}}\) real images \(x^{(i)}\) and \(n_{\mathrm{gen}}\) codes \(z^{(i)}\) (slide 29 writes \(m_{\mathrm{real}},m_{\mathrm{gen}}\)), the discriminator minimises the binary cross-entropy with label 1 for real and 0 for generated images:

\[J_D=-\frac1{n_{\mathrm{real}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{real}}}\log D(x^{(i)})-\frac1{n_{\mathrm{gen}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{gen}}}\log\bigl(1-D(G(z^{(i)}))\bigr).\]

Slide 29 then writes the generator's cost as \(J_G=-J_D=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{gen}}}\sum_i\log(1-D(G(z^{(i)})))\). The second equality holds only up to the real-data term \(\frac1{n_{\mathrm{real}}}\sum_i\log D(x^{(i)})\), which does not depend on the generator. The gradient with respect to the generator's parameters is the same with or without it, so the training step is unchanged, but the two expressions are not equal numbers. Goodfellow's NIPS 2016 tutorial defines the zero-sum game by \(J^{(G)}=-J^{(D)}\) with the full discriminator cost (p. 22, eq. 10). Slides 30 and 34 cite this tutorial as 2014; it was given at NIPS 2016 and posted to arXiv in January 2017.

In expectation over the data and the prior, and with equal weight on the two terms, \(-J_D\) becomes the value function of Goodfellow et al. (2014) (p. 3, eq. 1), which this book writes \(J(D,G)\) because the letter \(V\) is reserved for value functions in reinforcement learning:

\[\min_G\max_D\ J(D,G),\qquad J(D,G)=\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}}\log D(x)+\mathbb E_{z\sim p_z}\log\bigl(1-D(G(z))\bigr).\]

What the game optimises

To analyse the game, treat \(D\) as an arbitrary function and assume \(p_g\) has a density. The second expectation is over \(z\), but it depends on \(z\) only through \(x=G(z)\), so it equals \(\int p_g(x)\log(1-D(x))\,dx\). Then

\[J(D,G)=\int\Bigl[p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\log D(x)+p_g(x)\log\bigl(1-D(x)\bigr)\Bigr]dx.\]

Proposition 4 (optimal discriminator; Goodfellow et al. 2014, Proposition 1, p. 4). For fixed \(G\), \(J(D,G)\) is maximised by \(D^*(x)=p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)/(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)+p_g(x))\) at every \(x\) where the denominator is positive.

Proof. The integrand can be maximised separately at each \(x\). Fix \(x\) with \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)+p_g(x)>0\). As a function of the value \(D(x)\in(0,1)\), the integrand \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\log D(x)+p_g(x)\log(1-D(x))\) has derivative \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)/D(x)-p_g(x)/(1-D(x))\), which vanishes at \(D(x)=p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)/(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)+p_g(x))\). Its second derivative, \(-p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)/D(x)^2-p_g(x)/(1-D(x))^2\), is negative, so that point is the maximum. \(\blacksquare\)

The optimal discriminator is the posterior probability that a point came from the data, when real and generated points are equally likely a priori; it is Bayes' rule, as in Proposition 1. Substituting it gives the objective the generator faces against a perfect opponent. Write \(M=\tfrac12(p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g)\) for the mixture, \(\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)=\int p\log(p/q)\) for the Kullback–Leibler divergence, and \(\mathrm{JS}(p\Vert q)=\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert M)+\tfrac12\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert M)\) for the Jensen–Shannon divergence, all in nats.

Proposition 5 (Goodfellow et al. 2014, Theorem 1 and eq. 6, p. 5). \(\max_D J(D,G)=-\log4+2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert p_g)\). Its minimum over \(G\) is \(-\log4\), attained exactly when \(p_g=p_{\mathrm{data}}\).

Proof. Insert \(D^*\) and write \(p_{\mathrm{data}}+p_g=2M\):

\[\int p_{\mathrm{data}}\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{data}}}{2M}+\int p_g\log\frac{p_g}{2M}=-2\log2+\mathrm{KL}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert M)+\mathrm{KL}(p_g\Vert M),\]

which is the stated identity because \(2\log2=\log4\). It remains to show \(\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)\ge0\) with equality only when \(p=q\) where \(p>0\). For every positive value of the ratio \(q/p\), \(\log(q/p)\le q/p-1\), with equality only when \(q/p=1\): the function \(q/p-1-\log(q/p)\) vanishes at \(q/p=1\), and its derivative with respect to the ratio, \(1-p/q\), is negative below 1 and positive above. Hence \(-\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)=\int p\log\frac qp\le\int p\bigl(\frac qp-1\bigr)=\int q-\int p\le0\). Equality forces \(q/p=1\) wherever \(p>0\). So \(\mathrm{JS}\ge0\), and \(\mathrm{JS}=0\) forces \(p_{\mathrm{data}}=M=p_g\). \(\blacksquare\)

The proposition says the game has the right target: against an optimal discriminator the generator minimises a divergence that is zero only at the data distribution, and at that point \(D^*=\tfrac12\) everywhere. It does not say that alternating gradient steps on finite networks reach this point. The recording's stopping rule, a discriminator that is correct on half of its inputs (≈4459–4488 s), is necessary at the optimum but not sufficient: an untrained discriminator that outputs \(\tfrac12\) for every input satisfies it too.

The hypothesis that \(p_g\) has a density is not decoration. The generator of slide 26 maps a code in \(\mathbb R^{100}\) to 12,288 pixel values, so its outputs lie on a set of dimension at most 100 inside \(\mathbb R^{12{,}288}\). Such a \(p_g\) has no density on \(\mathbb R^{12{,}288}\), and the two supports can be disjoint. The smallest case shows what then happens. Suppose \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and \(p_g\) put their mass on disjoint sets \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}\) and \(\mathcal X_g\). The pointwise argument of Proposition 4 still applies on each set: on \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}\) the integrand is \(p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\log D(x)\), whose supremum over \(D(x)\in(0,1)\) is approached as \(D(x)\to1\); on \(\mathcal X_g\) it is \(p_g(x)\log(1-D(x))\), approached as \(D(x)\to0\). So \(D^*=1\) on \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}\) and \(D^*=0\) on \(\mathcal X_g\). The mixture \(M\) equals \(\tfrac12p_{\mathrm{data}}\) on \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}\) and \(\tfrac12p_g\) on \(\mathcal X_g\), hence \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert M)=\int_{\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}}p_{\mathrm{data}}\log2=\log2\), \(\mathrm{KL}(p_g\Vert M)=\log2\), and \(\mathrm{JS}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert p_g)=\log2\), its largest possible value. Then \(\max_D J(D,G)=-\log4+2\log2=0\) for every generator whose support misses the data. The objective is flat across all such generators: moving \(\mathcal X_g\) toward \(\mathcal X_{\mathrm{data}}\) changes nothing until the supports meet, and an optimal discriminator passes no useful gradient to \(G\). This is the setting of the cold start in the next section: a discriminator near \(D=0\) on every generated point is exactly where the saturating cost's gradient vanishes. The running case avoids the problem because both of its densities are positive everywhere.

The running case gives a number. Suppose the generator has learned only one cluster: \(p_g=\mathcal N(\mu,I)\). Both densities factor as a one-dimensional density along \(\mu/\lVert\mu\rVert_2\) times the same standard normal on the orthogonal complement; the second factor cancels in every log-ratio, so the divergence is a one-dimensional integral: \(\mathrm{JS}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert p_g)=0.1947\) nats, and the game's value against the optimal discriminator is \(-\log4+2\times0.1947=-0.9969\), compared with the optimum \(-\log4=-1.3863\) (engineering check; the code computes the value integral directly and confirms the identity to \(10^{-9}\)). When the clusters are far apart the divergence approaches a closed form: then \(M\) equals \(\tfrac34\) of the generated cluster plus \(\tfrac14\) of the other, \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\Vert M)=\tfrac12\log\frac{1/2}{3/4}+\tfrac12\log\frac{1/2}{1/4}=\tfrac12\log\tfrac43\), \(\mathrm{KL}(p_g\Vert M)=\log\tfrac43\), and \(\mathrm{JS}=\tfrac34\log\tfrac43=0.2158\) nats. The finite-separation value 0.1947 lies below this limit because the clusters overlap. A generator that covers one of two modes is strictly penalised by the game's objective.

Mode collapse

A student asks whether the generator learns the data or merely learns to fool \(D\) (≈4287–4311 s). The failure behind the question is mode collapse: the generator maps many codes to a few outputs, covering part of the data distribution with outputs the discriminator cannot reject. The tutorial defines it as the generator mapping "several different input \(z\) values to the same output point" and notes that partial collapse is common (p. 34). The running-case computation of the previous section shows that the minimax objective does penalise collapse: covering one cluster leaves a value of \(-0.9969\) against the optimum \(-1.3863\). Collapse is therefore a failure of the training dynamics, not of the objective.

The tutorial gives the mechanism: if the discriminator were held fixed and the generator optimised to completion, the generator would map every code to the single point the discriminator currently finds most real, and simultaneous gradient descent "does not clearly privilege min max over max min" (p. 35). It adds that mode collapse "does not seem to be caused by any particular cost function" (p. 35), which is why the non-saturating cost does not cure it. A second failure of the dynamics, distinct from collapse, is non-convergence: the two players cycle without either reaching a fixed point, and the losses keep oscillating. The recording's criterion that the losses stop changing (≈4459–4488 s) detects non-convergence and does not detect collapse.

Slide 41 lists two limitations of GANs, mode collapse and unstable adversarial training, and states what a replacement should do: model the whole distribution gradually, with a single network and a non-adversarial loss. The idea, in the recording's words, is to add noise to data and teach a network to remove it (≈4747–4863 s). Removing a little noise from a nearly clean image is an easy prediction problem; removing heavy noise is a harder one; a network trained on all noise levels can chain easy steps into a hard one. That is the intuition. The next three sections make it precise: a fixed corruption process, a regression target, and a sampler.

The forward process: the lecture's version and the DDPM version 1:21:03

Provenance. Lecture: the additive forward process of slide 42 and the "in practice" formula of slide 45 (≈4863–5078 and ≈5566–5648 s). Research extension: DDPM's equations 2 and 4, the variance-exploding and variance-preserving families of Song et al. Original construction: Propositions 6 and 7, the bound on \(\bar\alpha_T\), the running-case marginal and Figure 2.

A forward process is a fixed Markov chain \(x_0,x_1,\dots,x_T\) that starts at a data point \(x_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and adds noise at each of \(T\) steps. Nothing in it is learned. Its only job is to define, for every step \(t\), pairs of a clean input and a noisy version, which become training data for the network of the next section.

The lecture's simplified process

Slide 42, labelled "simplified from" Ho et al., defines \(x_{t+1}=x_t+\chi_t\) with independent \(\chi_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) (the slide writes each step's noise with the letter this book reserves for standardised noise; here the per-step noises are auxiliary standard Gaussian vectors \(\chi_t\)). The slide expands it to \(x_t=x_0+(\chi_0+\dots+\chi_{t-1})\), and calls the sum the cumulative noise. The sum of \(t\) independent standard Gaussian vectors is Gaussian with covariance \(tI\), so

\[x_t\mid x_0\sim\mathcal N(x_0,\,t\,I).\]

The variance grows without bound. After \(T=1000\) steps a running-case input has per-coordinate variance \(1+1000=1001\), and the class mean \(\pm\mu\), whose coordinates are \(\pm1.414\) in two dimensions, is still the mean of \(x_T\): the process never forgets \(x_0\) in its mean, and its endpoint has a spread that depends on \(T\). A sampler must start from a distribution of that width. Song et al. (2021) call processes whose variance grows without bound variance exploding, and the DDPM process, whose variance stays fixed at one when the data have unit variance, variance preserving (p. 5). The lecture's simplified process is of the first kind, with unit steps; DDPM is of the second.

The DDPM process 1:32:46

DDPM fixes a noise schedule \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_T\in(0,1)\) and defines each step as a shrink followed by fresh noise (Ho et al., p. 2, eq. 2):

\[q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\bigr),\qquad\text{that is}\qquad x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\chi_t,\]

with \(\alpha_t=1-\beta_t\) and independent \(\chi_t\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). Write \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{t'=1}^{t}\alpha_{t'}\). The recording describes this step as erasing some pixels and adding noise only to a random subset of pixels (≈5596–5624 s). That description is inaccurate: every coordinate is multiplied by \(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\) and every coordinate receives independent noise. Slide 45 writes the step, with its noise renamed \(\chi_t\), as \(x_{t+1}=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_t+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\chi_t\) together with \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta_i)\). With the slide's indexing the step into \(x_t\) uses \(\beta_{t-1}\), so the marginal of \(x_t\) involves \(\beta_0,\dots,\beta_{t-1}\), not \(\beta_1,\dots,\beta_t\); the two formulas on the slide are off by one index. This chapter uses the paper's indexing, in which the step into \(x_t\) uses \(\beta_t\).

Proposition 6 (forward marginal; Ho et al., p. 2, eq. 4). For every \(t\ge1\), \(q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)I\bigr)\). Equivalently, \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\) with a single standard Gaussian \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\).

Proof. By induction. For \(t=1\) the claim is the definition, since \(\bar\alpha_1=\alpha_1\). Suppose \(x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\chi\) with \(\chi\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) independent of \(\chi_t\). Then

\[x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,x_0+\Bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\chi+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\chi_t\Bigr).\]

The first coefficient is \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). The bracket is a sum of independent centred Gaussians, hence Gaussian with covariance \(\bigl(\alpha_t-\bar\alpha_t+1-\alpha_t\bigr)I=(1-\bar\alpha_t)I\). \(\blacksquare\)

The proof uses only one fact about Gaussians: a linear combination of independent Gaussian vectors is Gaussian, with variances adding. Two consequences follow. First, \(x_t\) can be drawn from \(x_0\) in one step, without simulating the chain; the dataset of the next section needs no stored trajectories. Second, the symbol \(\varepsilon\) in DDPM is a standardised noise, \(\varepsilon=(x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\), with identity covariance at every \(t\). The lecture's cumulative noise has covariance \(tI\). The two are different quantities, and the rest of the chapter keeps them apart.

Proposition 7 (the endpoint forgets the data). Ho et al. use \(T=1000\) and \(\beta_t\) increasing linearly from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\) (p. 5). For this schedule \(\bar\alpha_T\le e^{-\sum_t\beta_t}=e^{-10.05}=4.3\times10^{-5}\).

Proof. \(1-\beta\le e^{-\beta}\) for every real \(\beta\), because \(e^{-\beta}\) is convex and \(1-\beta\) is its tangent line at 0. Multiply over \(t\). The sum of the 1000 equally spaced values from \(10^{-4}\) to \(0.02\) is \(1000\times(10^{-4}+0.02)/2=10.05\). \(\blacksquare\)

The exact product is \(\bar\alpha_T=4.04\times10^{-5}\), so \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}=0.0064\) (engineering check). By Proposition 6, \(x_T\) is \(0.0064\,x_0\) plus noise of covariance \((1-4\times10^{-5})I\): to within that factor it is a standard Gaussian regardless of the image. This is why sampling can start from \(\mathcal N(0,I)\).

The running case shows the process at work. If \(x_0\) comes from the cluster \(\mathcal N(\pm\mu,I)\), Proposition 6 gives \(x_t\sim\mathcal N\bigl(\pm\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mu,\ (\bar\alpha_t+1-\bar\alpha_t)I\bigr)=\mathcal N(\pm\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mu,I)\): the covariance stays exactly \(I\), and only the cluster centres move toward the origin. The noisy distribution is

\[q_t(x)=\tfrac12\,\mathcal N\bigl(x;\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu,I\bigr)+\tfrac12\,\mathcal N\bigl(x;-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu,I\bigr).\]

With the DDPM schedule, \(\bar\alpha_t\) is 0.897 at \(t=100\), 0.524 at \(t=250\), 0.079 at \(t=500\) and \(4.0\times10^{-5}\) at \(t=1000\) (engineering check). Figure 2 plots the density along the direction of \(\mu\): the two modes at \(\pm2\) move inward, merge into one bump, and end as the standard normal. An equal mixture of two unit-variance Gaussians on the line, with means at the same distance on either side of 0, has two modes exactly when that distance exceeds 1; here the distance is \(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\), which is 1.45 at \(t=250\) and 0.56 at \(t=500\), so the modes merge between those steps.

Training a network to predict the noise 1:24:38

Provenance. Lecture: the reverse process predicts the noise, the reconstruction loss, training triples of noisy image, step index and noise, the step index as an input, the remark on data volume and a flattening loss (slides 43–44, ≈5078–5566 s). Research extension: DDPM Algorithm 1 and \(J_{\mathrm{simple}}\). Original construction: Proposition 8, the closed-form optimal predictor of the running case, and the loss floor.

The network \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) receives a noisy input and its step index \(t\) and predicts the noise. The index tells the network how much noise to expect; the recording stresses that the same image at step 5 and at step 45 are different prediction problems (≈5235–5290 s). Training draws a clean example, a step and a noise, forms the noisy input, and regresses. DDPM's Algorithm 1 (p. 4) and its equation 14 (p. 5) state the loss, which the paper writes \(L_{\mathrm{simple}}\) and this book writes \(J_{\mathrm{simple}}\), since objectives are \(J\) here:

\[J_{\mathrm{simple}}(\theta)=\mathbb E_{t,x_0,\varepsilon}\Bigl\lVert\varepsilon-\varepsilon_\theta\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\bigr)\Bigr\rVert_2^2,\]

with \(t\) uniform on \(\{1,\dots,T\}\), \(x_0\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\) and \(\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). Slide 44 describes the same procedure as a database of triples \((x_t,t,\varepsilon)\) built by running the forward process and storing the noise that was added. By Proposition 6 nothing has to be stored: each triple is generated on demand from a clean example. The target is a label the procedure made up, which is why the recording calls the method self-supervised.

Proposition 8 (what the regression learns). Among all functions of \((x_t,t)\), the one that minimises \(J_{\mathrm{simple}}\) is the conditional mean \(\varepsilon^*(x_t,t)=\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid x_t,t]\).

Proof. Fix \(t\) and write \(\hat\varepsilon(x_t)\) for any predictor. Add and subtract \(\varepsilon^*\):

\[\mathbb E\lVert\varepsilon-\hat\varepsilon\rVert^2=\mathbb E\lVert\varepsilon-\varepsilon^*\rVert^2+\mathbb E\lVert\varepsilon^*-\hat\varepsilon\rVert^2+2\,\mathbb E\bigl[(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^*)^\top(\varepsilon^*-\hat\varepsilon)\bigr].\]

Condition the cross term on \(x_t\): the factor \(\varepsilon^*-\hat\varepsilon\) is then fixed, and \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon-\varepsilon^*\mid x_t]=0\). The cross term vanishes, and the middle term is minimised, at zero, by \(\hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

A network trained with this loss approximates a posterior mean: the average of all the noises that could have produced the observed \(x_t\). For the running case that average has a closed form. Given the cluster \(\pm\mu\), the pair \((\varepsilon,x_t)\) is jointly Gaussian with \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon,x_t)=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,I\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(x_t)=I\), and for jointly Gaussian vectors \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid x_t]=\mathbb E[\varepsilon]+\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon,x_t)\,\mathrm{Var}(x_t)^{-1}(x_t-\mathbb E[x_t])\), so \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid x_t,\pm]=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,(x_t\mp\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mu)\). The posterior probability of the \(+\) cluster given \(x_t\) is \(\sigma(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu^\top x_t)\) by the argument of Proposition 1, and averaging the two conditional means with these weights gives

\[\varepsilon^*(x_t,t)=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\Bigl(x_t-\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\tanh\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu^\top x_t\bigr)\,\mu\Bigr),\]

using \(\sigma(2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu^\top x_t)-\sigma(-2\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu^\top x_t)=\tanh(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mu^\top x_t)\), an instance of the identity \(\sigma(2\cdot)-\sigma(-2\cdot)=2\sigma(2\cdot)-1=\tanh(\cdot)\). The optimal denoiser contains the Bayes classifier of the noisy data: the \(\tanh\) factor is the posterior-weighted vote between the clusters. At \(t=1\), where \(\bar\alpha_1=0.9999\), the vote is the classifier of Proposition 1 up to that factor; at \(t=T\), \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}=0.0064\) and the vote carries almost no information. The code below uses this exact predictor in place of a trained network, so that every error it reports belongs to the sampler, not to the training.

The minimum of the loss is not zero. For a single cluster \(\mathcal N(\mu,I)\), the conditional variance of \(\varepsilon\) given \(x_t\) is \(I-(1-\bar\alpha_t)I=\bar\alpha_tI\), so even the exact predictor has loss \(n_x\bar\alpha_t\): 1.794 at \(t=100\) in two dimensions. For the two-cluster mixture the code measures 1.82 at \(t=100\), slightly above the single-cluster value because the cluster identity is uncertain (engineering check). The recording advises watching the loss and concluding, once it stops falling, that the model is at capacity (speaker judgment, ≈5534–5566 s). A plateau is also what a perfect model produces. The floor must be known, or estimated, before a flat loss curve is read as a capacity limit.

Sampling: from one noise draw to one image 1:34:08

Provenance. Lecture: start from a random image, repeatedly predict and subtract the cumulative noise, the cost of many network calls, conditioning as the reason a particular object appears (slide 46, ≈5648–5881 s). Research extension: DDPM Algorithm 2 and its variance choices. Original construction: the derivation of the reverse step, the one-shot estimate as a posterior mean, and Figure 3.

Slide 46 contains two descriptions of sampling. Its bullet text says, in paraphrase: start from a Gaussian random image and, for each step from \(T\) back to 0, predict the cumulative noise that took \(x_0\) to \(x_t\) and remove that predicted noise from \(x_t\). Beside the bullets the rendered page reproduces Algorithm 2 of Ho et al. (p. 4), which is a different procedure: a scaled subtraction followed by fresh noise at every step. The spoken description in the recording follows the bullets (≈5677–5761 s). This section derives the algorithm and shows what the bullets would compute instead.

Subtracting the predicted noise gives a posterior mean

Solve Proposition 6 for the clean input and replace the unknown noise by the network's prediction \(\hat\varepsilon=\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\):

\[\hat x_0=\frac{x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}.\]

Slide 43's "\(x_t-\hat\varepsilon\approx x_0\)" is this formula for the lecture's additive process, where \(\bar\alpha_t=1\) and the cumulative noise enters without a coefficient. Whatever the process, if \(\hat\varepsilon\) is the optimal predictor of Proposition 8, then \(\hat x_0=\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]\), because the map from \(\varepsilon\) to \(x_0\) is affine at fixed \(x_t\). The one-shot estimate is the average of all clean inputs consistent with \(x_t\), not a sample of one of them. At \(t=T\), where \(x_t\) carries almost no information, that average is close to the mean of the whole data distribution. In the running case the data mean is the origin, a point between the two clusters where the data density is low. Figure 3 shows the result: for 10,000 draws of \(x_T\) the one-shot estimates all lie within 0.12 of the origin (engineering check; the largest norm in the recorded run is 0.116), while the data lie near \(\pm2\). Any procedure whose final output is \(\hat x_0\) returns a conditional mean, and a conditional mean is not a draw.

A sampler must keep the spread of the posterior at every level. Two constructions do so. The DDPM step derived below moves \(x_t\) only part of the way toward \(\hat x_0\) and adds a fresh Gaussian draw. A deterministic alternative re-noises the estimate with the predicted noise itself, \(x_{t-1}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat\varepsilon\); it adds no new randomness, and its output is a deterministic function of \(x_T\) that changes with \(x_T\) at every step, because \(\hat\varepsilon\) and hence \(\hat x_0\) depend on the current \(x_t\). The collapse to one average therefore comes from stopping at \(\hat x_0\), not from the absence of fresh noise. The deterministic alternative is outside the recording and is not developed here.

The ancestral step

DDPM instead samples \(x_{t-1}\) given \(x_t\). If \(x_0\) were known, the conditional distribution \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) would be Gaussian and computable. Work in one coordinate; all coordinates are independent given \(x_0\). By Bayes' rule \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\propto q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\,q(x_{t-1}\mid x_0)\), a product of two Gaussian densities in \(x_{t-1}\): one with mean \(x_t/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\) and variance \(\beta_t/\alpha_t\) (from \(x_t=\sqrt{\alpha_t}x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\chi_t\)), one with mean \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}x_0\) and variance \(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}\) (Proposition 6). Precisions add and the mean is the precision-weighted average, so the variance is

\[\tilde\beta_t=\Bigl(\frac{\alpha_t}{\beta_t}+\frac1{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\Bigr)^{-1}=\frac{\beta_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t},\]

using \(\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+\beta_t=1-\bar\alpha_t\), and the mean is \(\bigl[\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})\,x_t+\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t\,x_0\bigr]/(1-\bar\alpha_t)\). Now substitute \(x_0=(x_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\) and use \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}=1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\). The coefficient of \(x_t\) becomes \(\bigl[\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})+\beta_t\bigr]/\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_t}(1-\bar\alpha_t)\bigr)=1/\sqrt{\alpha_t}\), and the mean is

\[\frac1{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Bigl(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon\Bigr).\]

Algorithm 2 of Ho et al. replaces \(\varepsilon\) by \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\) and adds fresh noise \(\tilde\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) for \(t>1\). The noise is scaled by a standard deviation that the paper writes with the letter this book reserves for the sigmoid. The algorithm repeats the step from \(x_T\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) down to \(x_0\) (p. 4; the paper writes the fresh noise as \(\mathbf z\)). The paper reports that the two choices of variance, \(\beta_t\) and \(\tilde\beta_t\), "had similar results" (p. 3); the code below uses variance \(\beta_t\). The step subtracts the fraction \(\beta_t/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\) of the predicted standardised noise and then injects new noise. At \(t=T\) this fraction is 0.020, and the step then divides by \(\sqrt{\alpha_T}=0.990\); the one-shot estimate at the same step has coefficient \(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_T}/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_T}=157\) on \(\hat\varepsilon\). It moves a little toward the posterior mean and keeps the spread that a sample needs.

With the exact predictor of the running case, 1,000 ancestral steps from 10,000 independent draws of \(x_T\) put 49.2% of the samples in the \(+\) cluster, and the sample covariance differs from the data covariance \(I+\mu\mu^\top\) by at most 0.024 in any entry (engineering check; Figure 3). These numbers check the sampler and the derivation above; they say nothing about how well a neural network learns \(\varepsilon^*\) from finite data.

The cost follows from the algorithm: one network evaluation per step, \(T\) evaluations per sample, and \(T=1000\) in Ho et al. (p. 5). The recording's remark that generating one image costs a large amount of computation (≈5733–5761 s) is this count. Latent diffusion and conditioning 1:38:01

Provenance. Lecture: diffusion in the latent space of an encoder, decoding at the end, text conditioning by concatenating an embedded prompt (slides 47–48), then video: the added time dimension, spatio-temporal patches as tokens, demonstration videos (slide 49) (≈5881–6431 s). Research extension: the autoencoder, downsampling factors and conditioning mechanisms of Rombach et al. Original construction: the size and token arithmetic.

Pixel-space diffusion evaluates a network on full-size images \(T\) times per sample. Rombach et al. first train an autoencoder (p. 4). Its encoder maps an image of height \(n_H\) and width \(n_W\) to a latent \(z\) of height \(n_H/n_{\mathrm{down}}\), width \(n_W/n_{\mathrm{down}}\) and a chosen number of channels, where \(n_{\mathrm{down}}\) is the downsampling factor; the paper writes the factor as \(f\), a letter this book reserves for the network \(f_\theta\). Its decoder maps latents back to images. The forward process, the training loss and the sampler then run on \(z\) instead of on pixels, and the decoder is applied once at the end. Slide 47 draws this (the pixel-space chain is crossed out and replaced by a chain on \(z_0,\dots,z_T\) after an "Encoder model (VAE)"). The paper regularises the latent space either with a slight KL penalty or with vector quantisation (p. 4), and compares factors \(n_{\mathrm{down}}\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}\): small factors train slowly, and overly large ones stop improving fidelity after few steps (p. 5).

The saving is a matter of arithmetic. A \(256\times256\times3\) image has 196,608 entries. With \(n_{\mathrm{down}}=8\) and, as an example, 4 latent channels, the latent has \(32\times32\times4=4{,}096\) entries, 48 times fewer (engineering check by shown arithmetic; the channel count is an assumption of the example). The ratio is a count of numbers, not a promised speed-up, because network cost depends on architecture as well as input size. Compression also bounds what the generator can express: a detail the decoder cannot reconstruct from any latent cannot be generated by diffusion in that latent space, however many steps it takes.

The recording conditions on a prompt by embedding the text and concatenating the embedding with the input of the denoising network, so that the network learns the relation between the two (≈6110–6169 s). Rombach et al. describe both concatenation and a "more general cross-attention mechanism" (p. 4, Figure 3), and use cross-attention for text-to-image models (p. 2). Asked whether latent diffusion might produce the meaningless iguana-classified images of the forging section, the recording answers that it will not, because the model is trained to remove noise and so to return something that looks real (speaker judgment, ≈6000–6048 s). The training objective does target the data distribution, while the forging objective ignored it; but no guarantee prevents a trained model from producing artefacts on inputs or prompts far from its training data.

A running-case laboratory in NumPy

Provenance. Original construction throughout. It implements Propositions 1–3, the GAN derivatives and Proposition 5, and Propositions 6 and 8 with the ancestral step derived in the sampling section.

The program below checks the chapter's central numbers in one run. Its inputs are fixed constants: the slide 13 neuron, the running case with \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2=2\), the DDPM linear schedule with \(T=1000\), and seeded random draws. It has no side effects beyond printing. Each check is an assert, so a wrong formula or a changed constant stops the program with an AssertionError; the helper worst_linf raises ValueError for a negative budget. It needs Python 3 and NumPy (tested with Python 3.13.5 and NumPy 2.2.6) and ran in a fresh process in about 2 seconds.

The program does four things. It recomputes slide 13 and the FGSM alternative. It samples class-1 inputs of the running case in 2 and 3,072 dimensions, applies the worst \(\ell_\infty\) perturbation of budget 0.05, and compares the attacked accuracy with Proposition 3. It checks the two generator-cost derivatives at \(D=0.01\) by central differences, and computes the Jensen–Shannon divergence of the one-cluster generator together with the value integral of Proposition 5. Finally it runs the DDPM forward chain step by step and compares its endpoint with Proposition 6, runs Algorithm 2 with the exact predictor \(\varepsilon^*\), measures the one-shot estimates, and measures the loss floor at \(t=100\).

import math
import numpy as np


def sigmoid(u):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-u))


def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def worst_linf(w, rho):
    """Perturbation in the l-inf ball of radius rho that lowers w.x most."""
    if rho < 0:
        raise ValueError("budget rho must be nonnegative")
    return -rho * np.sign(w)


# 1. The slide-13 neuron: l2-optimal step versus FGSM step.
w = np.array([1.0, 3.0, -1.0, 2.0, 2.0, 3.0])
x = np.array([1.0, -1.0, 2.0, 0.0, 3.0, -2.0])

assert w @ x == -4.0 and w @ w == 28.0
assert abs(sigmoid(w @ x) - 0.017986) < 1e-6
assert abs(sigmoid(w @ (x + delta)) - 0.832018) < 1e-6
assert abs(np.linalg.norm(delta) - 1.058301) < 1e-6
assert abs(sigmoid(w @ (x - worst_linf(w, 0.2))) - 0.167982) < 1e-6
# 2. Running case: p_data = 0.5 N(mu, I) + 0.5 N(-mu, I), ||mu||_2 = 2.
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
report = {}
for n_x in (2, 3072):
    mu = np.full(n_x, 2.0 / math.sqrt(n_x))
    w_bayes = 2.0 * mu                  # Bayes logit u(x) = 2 mu.x
    X = mu + rng.standard_normal((2000, n_x))       # class y = 1
    X_adv = X + worst_linf(w_bayes, 0.05)
    formula = Phi((4.0 - 0.05 * np.abs(mu).sum()) / 2.0)
    measured = np.mean(X_adv @ w_bayes > 0)
    assert abs(measured - formula) < 0.04
    report[n_x] = (round(float(np.mean(X @ w_bayes > 0)), 3),
                   round(float(measured), 3), round(formula, 3))
# 3. GAN: generator-cost derivatives at the discriminator logit u.
u, h = math.log(0.01 / 0.99), 1e-6


def sat(v):
    return math.log(1.0 - sigmoid(v))


def nsat(v):
    return -math.log(sigmoid(v))


d_sat = (sat(u + h) - sat(u - h)) / (2 * h)
d_nsat = (nsat(u + h) - nsat(u - h)) / (2 * h)
assert abs(d_sat + 0.01) < 1e-6 and abs(d_nsat + 0.99) < 1e-6
# JS divergence of a generator that produces only the +mu mode.
g = np.linspace(-12.0, 12.0, 240001)
dx = g[1] - g[0]


def log_n(v, m):
    return -0.5 * (v - m) ** 2 - 0.5 * math.log(2 * math.pi)


lp_d = np.logaddexp(log_n(g, 2.0), log_n(g, -2.0)) + math.log(0.5)
lp_g = log_n(g, 2.0)
lp_m = np.logaddexp(lp_d, lp_g) + math.log(0.5)

    + 0.5 * np.sum(np.exp(lp_g) * (lp_g - lp_m)) * dx
lp_s = np.logaddexp(lp_d, lp_g)                 # log(p_data + p_g)
value = np.sum(np.exp(lp_d) * (lp_d - lp_s)
               + np.exp(lp_g) * (lp_g - lp_s)) * dx
assert abs(value - (-math.log(4.0) + 2.0 * js)) < 1e-9
assert abs(js - 0.194700) < 1e-5
# 4. DDPM on the running case with the exact noise predictor.
T = 1000
beta = np.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)
alpha = 1.0 - beta
abar = np.cumprod(alpha)
mu = np.full(2, 2.0 / math.sqrt(2.0))


def eps_star(xt, t):
    """E[eps | x_t] for the running case (derived in the text)."""
    a = abar[t - 1]
    r = np.tanh(math.sqrt(a) * (xt @ mu))[:, None]
    return math.sqrt(1.0 - a) * (xt - math.sqrt(a) * r * mu)


x0 = rng.standard_normal((10000, 2)) + np.where(
    rng.random(10000) < 0.5, 1.0, -1.0)[:, None] * mu
xt = x0.copy()
for t in range(1, T + 1):                        # forward chain, step by step
    xt = math.sqrt(alpha[t - 1]) * xt \
        + math.sqrt(beta[t - 1]) * rng.standard_normal(xt.shape)

    mean = (xs - beta[t - 1] / math.sqrt(1.0 - abar[t - 1])
            * eps_star(xs, t)) / math.sqrt(alpha[t - 1])

    xs = mean + math.sqrt(beta[t - 1]) * noise

assert abs(frac_plus - 0.5) < 0.02 and cov_err < 0.1

    / math.sqrt(abar[-1])
assert np.linalg.norm(one_shot, axis=1).max() < 0.2

x_noisy = math.sqrt(a) * x0 + math.sqrt(1.0 - a) * eps

assert 2 * a < floor < 2 * a + 0.2

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction, with one statement from the NIPS 2016 GAN tutorial.

Each result of this chapter holds under an idealisation, and each idealisation fails in practice in a specific way.

Linear models. Propositions 2 and 3 are exact for a linear logit. A deep network is only locally close to linear, so FGSM is a first-order approximation to the worst perturbation, and iterative attacks such as projected gradient descent find stronger ones. The high-dimensional fragility of Proposition 3 also assumes signal spread evenly over weakly informative coordinates; for natural images the relevant geometry is learned, not fixed.

Threat models. Each defense in the table protects against the attacker it assumes. Adversarial training at budget \(\rho\) in \(\ell_\infty\) says nothing about \(\ell_2\) attacks, patches or backdoors, and detection methods are tested against attackers who know them only in some evaluations. No defense in this chapter carries a guarantee; certified defenses, which do, are outside the recording and outside this chapter.

GAN theory. Propositions 4 and 5 treat the discriminator and the generator as arbitrary functions and assume that \(p_g\) has a density. The tutorial states that convergence results of this kind "depend on convexity" and "do not apply" when the players are neural networks updated in parameter space (p. 22). When the generator's support misses the data, as it can for a code of dimension 100 and an image of dimension 12,288, the optimal-discriminator objective is constant at 0 and gives the generator no gradient (the paragraph after Proposition 5). The propositions say what the game rewards, not that training reaches it.

Diffusion. The running case replaces the trained network by the exact posterior mean, so the chapter's sampling results measure the sampler, not learning. Real models also differ in schedule, parameterisation, number of steps, guidance and latent space; the chapter derives the DDPM forms only.

Claims not checked. Statements about Sora, Veo, Midjourney, red-teaming practice and current assistant behaviour are product claims or speaker judgments recorded with their times; they may be out of date.

What you can now do. You can write an attack as an optimisation over inputs with the parameters frozen, add penalty terms for a target class, closeness or printability, and compute the input gradient by backpropagation. You can compute the worst perturbation of a linear score under an \(\ell_\infty\) or \(\ell_2\) budget, explain why the \(\ell_\infty\) attack strengthens with dimension, and derive FGSM. You can state the threat model a defense assumes, write adversarial training as a min–max problem, and show why a backdoor survives validation on clean data. You can derive the optimal discriminator and the Jensen–Shannon form of the GAN objective, compute the saturating and non-saturating gradients at the logit, and distinguish mode collapse from a defect of the objective. You can derive the DDPM forward marginal, the optimal noise predictor, the reverse step and the one-shot estimate, and explain why the latter returns an average rather than a sample.

1. A budget on the sum of changes

An attacker may change the input by \(\delta\) with \(\lVert\delta\rVert_1=\sum_j\lvert\delta_j\rvert\le\rho\). Find the largest possible increase of a linear score \(w^\top x\) and a perturbation that attains it. Apply the result to the slide 13 neuron with \(\rho=0.2\) and compare with the \(\ell_\infty\) attack of the same budget.

Solution

Let \(j^\star\) be an index with \(\lvert w_{j^\star}\rvert=\max_j\lvert w_j\rvert=\lVert w\rVert_\infty\). Then \(w^\top\delta\le\sum_j\lvert w_j\rvert\lvert\delta_j\rvert\le\lVert w\rVert_\infty\sum_j\lvert\delta_j\rvert\le\rho\lVert w\rVert_\infty\). The perturbation that puts the whole budget on coordinate \(j^\star\), \(\delta_{j^\star}=\rho\,\mathrm{sign}(w_{j^\star})\) and \(\delta_j=0\) otherwise, attains the bound, so the maximum is \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_\infty\). This is the \((1,\infty)\) pair of dual norms, as Proposition 2 was the \((\infty,1)\) and \((2,2)\) pairs. For \(w=(1,3,-1,2,2,3)\), \(\lVert w\rVert_\infty=3\), attained at coordinates 2 and 6. With \(\rho=0.2\) the logit rises from \(-4\) to \(-3.4\) and \(\hat y=\sigma(-3.4)=0.032\). The \(\ell_\infty\) attack of the same budget raises the logit by \(0.2\lVert w\rVert_1=2.4\), to \(\hat y=0.168\). The \(\ell_1\) attack changes one pixel only, so it is the natural model of an attack that must be sparse; in high dimension its gain \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_\infty\) does not grow with \(n_x\), unlike the \(\ell_\infty\) gain \(\rho\lVert w\rVert_1\).

2. The same attack measured in Euclidean length

In the running case, let the attacker have an \(\ell_2\) budget \(\rho_2\). (a) Show that the attacked accuracy is \(\Phi(2-\rho_2)\) in every dimension. (b) For \(n_x=3072\), what \(\ell_2\) budget gives the same attacked accuracy as the \(\ell_\infty\) budget 0.05 of Proposition 3, and why do the two agree? (c) For a class mean \(\mu\) whose coordinates do not all have the same magnitude, which attack is stronger at equal Euclidean length?

Solution

(a) By Proposition 2 the worst \(\ell_2\) perturbation lowers \(\mu^\top x\) by \(\rho_2\lVert\mu\rVert_2\). As in Proposition 3 the attacked score is Gaussian with mean \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2-\rho_2\lVert\mu\rVert_2\) and standard deviation \(\lVert\mu\rVert_2\), so the accuracy is \(\Phi(\lVert\mu\rVert_2-\rho_2)=\Phi(2-\rho_2)\), with no \(n_x\). (b) The \(\ell_\infty\) attack of budget 0.05 is \(-0.05\,\mathrm{sign}(\mu)\), whose Euclidean length is \(0.05\sqrt{3072}=2.771\). Here every coordinate of \(\mu\) has the same magnitude, so \(\mathrm{sign}(\mu)\) is parallel to \(\mu\) and the \(\ell_\infty\) corner lies exactly on the \(\ell_2\)-optimal direction. Both give \(\Phi(2-2.771)=\Phi(-0.771)=0.220\), the table's value. (c) At equal Euclidean length \(\rho_2\), the \(\ell_\infty\) corner \(-(\rho_2/\sqrt{n_x})\,\mathrm{sign}(\mu)\) lowers the score by \((\rho_2/\sqrt{n_x})\lVert\mu\rVert_1\), while the \(\ell_2\)-optimal step lowers it by \(\rho_2\lVert\mu\rVert_2\). Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\lVert\mu\rVert_1\le\sqrt{n_x}\lVert\mu\rVert_2\), with equality only when all \(\lvert\mu_j\rvert\) are equal. So for unequal magnitudes the \(\ell_2\)-optimal step is strictly stronger at equal length, and the running case, with equal magnitudes, is the case in which the \(\ell_\infty\) attack loses nothing.

3. The two generator costs are not equivalent

Fix a discriminator. Generator A produces outputs on which \(D=0.5\). Generator B produces, with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, outputs on which \(D=0.01\) and outputs on which \(D=0.99\). Which generator does the saturating cost \(\mathbb E\log(1-D(G(z)))\) prefer, and which does the non-saturating cost \(-\mathbb E\log D(G(z))\) prefer? What does this show about the "\(\Leftrightarrow\)" on slides 30 and 32?

Solution

Both costs are minimised. Saturating: A gives \(\log0.5=-0.693\); B gives

\[\tfrac12(\log0.99+\log0.01)=\tfrac12(-0.0101-4.6052)=-2.308.\]

The saturating cost prefers B. Non-saturating: A gives \(-\log0.5=0.693\); B gives \(\tfrac12(0.0101+4.6052)=2.308\). The non-saturating cost prefers A. Against the same discriminator the two objectives rank the same two generators in opposite orders, so they are not equivalent optimisation problems. The saturating cost rewards B because \(\log(1-D)\) falls without bound as \(D\to1\), so one convincing half outweighs one unconvincing half; the non-saturating cost punishes B because \(-\log D\) grows without bound as \(D\to0\). What the two share is that each is decreasing in every individual \(D(G(z))\), so they push each sample in the same direction, and Goodfellow et al. (2014, p. 3) claim only a common fixed point of the game's dynamics.

4. Indexing a noise schedule

Slide 45 writes, with its noise renamed \(\chi_t\), \(x_{t+1}=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_t+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\chi_t\) and \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\beta_i)\). (a) Using the slide's step, derive the distribution of \(x_2\) given \(x_0\). (b) Evaluate it and the slide's \(\bar\alpha_2\) for \(\beta_0=0.1\), \(\beta_1=0.2\), \(\beta_2=0.3\). (c) Rewrite the step so that the slide's \(\bar\alpha_t\) is correct.

Solution

(a) The two steps are \(x_1=\sqrt{1-\beta_0}\,x_0+\sqrt{\beta_0}\,\chi_0\) and \(x_2=\sqrt{1-\beta_1}\,x_1+\sqrt{\beta_1}\,\chi_1\), with independent standard Gaussian vectors \(\chi_0,\chi_1\). Substituting,

\[x_2=\sqrt{(1-\beta_0)(1-\beta_1)}\,x_0+\sqrt{(1-\beta_1)\beta_0}\,\chi_0+\sqrt{\beta_1}\,\chi_1.\]

The noise terms are independent, so their covariance is \(\bigl((1-\beta_1)\beta_0+\beta_1\bigr)I=\bigl(1-(1-\beta_0)(1-\beta_1)\bigr)I\). Hence \(x_2\mid x_0\sim\mathcal N\bigl(\sqrt{(1-\beta_0)(1-\beta_1)}\,x_0,\ (1-(1-\beta_0)(1-\beta_1))I\bigr)\), the same algebra as Proposition 6. (b) The product that governs \(x_2\) is \((1-\beta_0)(1-\beta_1)=0.9\times0.8=0.72\), while the slide's \(\bar\alpha_2=(1-\beta_1)(1-\beta_2)=0.8\times0.7=0.56\). The slide's formula uses \(\beta_2\), which only enters the step to \(x_3\), and omits \(\beta_0\). (c) Index the step by its destination, \(x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\chi_t\), as in Ho et al. (p. 2, eq. 2); then \(x_t\mid x_0\) has \(\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{t'=1}^{t}(1-\beta_{t'})\) exactly.

5. Is a flat loss curve a capacity limit?

Let the data be a single Gaussian \(x_0\sim\mathcal N(\mu,I)\) in \(\mathbb R^{n_x}\). (a) Compute the minimum of the noise-prediction loss at step \(t\) over all predictors. (b) Compute the corresponding error of the one-shot estimate, \(\mathbb E\lVert\hat x_0-x_0\rVert_2^2\). (c) Evaluate both at \(t=100\) and \(t=500\) for \(n_x=2\) with the DDPM schedule, and use the result to assess the advice to stop scaling once the training loss flattens.

Solution

(a) By Proposition 6, \(x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\) with \(x_0-\mu\) and \(\varepsilon\) independent standard Gaussians. Then \(\mathrm{Var}(x_t)=I\), \(\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon,x_t)=\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,I\), and the Gaussian conditioning formula \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon\mid x_t)=\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon)-\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon,x_t)\mathrm{Var}(x_t)^{-1}\mathrm{Cov}(x_t,\varepsilon)\) gives \(\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon\mid x_t)=I-(1-\bar\alpha_t)I=\bar\alpha_tI\). By Proposition 8 the minimum loss is the trace, \(n_x\bar\alpha_t\). (b) \(\hat x_0-x_0=-\sqrt{(1-\bar\alpha_t)/\bar\alpha_t}\,(\hat\varepsilon-\varepsilon)\), so the error is \(\frac{1-\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_t}\,n_x\bar\alpha_t=n_x(1-\bar\alpha_t)\). (c) With \(\bar\alpha_{100}=0.897\) and \(\bar\alpha_{500}=0.079\): at \(t=100\) the loss floor is \(2\times0.897=1.794\) and the clean-image error \(2\times0.103=0.206\); at \(t=500\) the loss floor is \(0.157\) and the clean-image error \(1.843\). The noise-prediction loss of a perfect model is largest at small \(t\), where recovering the clean image is easiest; the two quantities measure difficulty in different units. A perfect model therefore shows a nonzero loss that stops decreasing. A flat curve is consistent with a capacity limit, with an optimisation problem, or with having reached the floor; to decide, estimate the floor (for example on a simple distribution where it is known, as here) or compare training and held-out loss. The advice at ≈5534–5566 s is a heuristic, not a test.

6. Protecting the door-unlock system against a backdoor

The face-verification door of chapters 2 and 3 uses an encoder downloaded as pretrained weights and fine-tuned on pairs of photographs collected from volunteers. Design controls against a backdoor that makes any face wearing a small patch match an enrolled employee. State the threat model, what each control can and cannot establish, and how many randomly sampled training pairs a reviewer must inspect to see at least one poisoned pair with probability 0.95 when 0.1% of pairs are poisoned.

Solution

Threat model: the attacker controls either the pretrained weights or some fraction of the volunteer pairs, knows the architecture, and wants the system to accept the patch as a chosen identity while clean accuracy stays unchanged. The attacker does not control the camera or the enrolled photographs. Controls and their limits: (i) Obtain the pretrained weights from a source whose file hashes are published and checked; this removes one entry point and says nothing about the collected pairs. (ii) Evaluate the final system on clean held-out pairs; by the argument of the backdoor section the backdoored model matches the honest one there, so this establishes clean accuracy and nothing about triggers. (iii) Evaluate with candidate triggers: add patches of several sizes, colours and positions to non-employee faces and measure how often they are accepted; this detects triggers similar to the candidates only. (iv) Review random samples of training pairs. With a poisoned fraction \(p_{\mathrm{poi}}=0.001\), a sample of \(n_{\mathrm{rev}}\) pairs contains no poisoned pair with probability \(0.999^{n_{\mathrm{rev}}}\); requiring this to be at most 0.05 gives \(n_{\mathrm{rev}}\ge\ln0.05/\ln0.999=2994.2\), so 2,995 pairs, and the reviewer must also recognise a poisoned pair when it appears. (v) Monitor deployment: log acceptances, and review accepted faces that carry unusual objects. No combination of these controls proves the absence of a backdoor; they reduce the entry points and raise the cost of an attack.
