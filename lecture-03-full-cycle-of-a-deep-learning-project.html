The full cycle of a deep-learning project: from the first dataset to a monitored system

A company installs a camera beside a door. The door must open for the people who are allowed in, stay shut for everybody else, run all day without a large computing bill, and keep working when winter brings scarves and summer brings sunglasses. A face-verification network is one component of that system. This chapter asks how such a project should be sequenced, from specifying the problem and collecting the first data through iteration, deployment and monitoring, and what each step can and cannot tell the builder.

The engineer controls the code completely and the data only partly: the training data are a sample whose contents nobody has examined in full, and the data the deployed system will meet do not exist yet. Every result below is a statement about that gap. The chapter derives the arithmetic of a two-stage pipeline, the effect of comparing a face against many enrolled people, the conditions under which training data from another distribution cannot hurt, the bound that links a test-set error to a deployed error, the correction and its limit when the input distribution changes, and the false-alarm rate of a monitoring band. Each result is tested on one running case, the door.

A conventional program does what its code says; if the code is known, the behaviour is known. A trained network is produced by code and by a dataset, and the recording's whiteboard states this as "AI = Code + Data". The code can be read in full. The dataset usually cannot: for a face system the builder does not know in advance whether poor lighting, long hair, glasses or facial expressions will cause errors (≈92–148 s). The recording concludes that development must be iterative: build something, observe where it fails, and change the code or the data (speaker judgment, ≈148–229 s). It applies the same argument to applications built on large language models, whose behaviour on a prompt is hard to predict because they were trained on more text than any person could read, tens of trillions of tokens in the recording's estimate (speaker judgment, ≈173–229 s).

Two kinds of data are unknown, and they are unknown for different reasons (≈229–287 s). The data already on disk are unknown because nobody has looked at all of them. The data the world will send after deployment are unknown because they have not been produced yet; the recording's example is a heavy winter scarf that covers part of a face. The first kind of ignorance is removed by inspecting data; the second can only be managed by monitoring. The rest of this chapter keeps the two apart.

The formal model

An input \(x\) is a camera image, or a pair of images when two faces are compared, and \(y\in\{0,1\}\) is its label. The training set \(\mathcal D=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^m\) contains \(m\) examples drawn from a distribution \(p_{\mathrm{train}}(x,y)\). The deployed system meets pairs \((x,y)\) drawn from a distribution \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x,y)\), which exists only after deployment and may differ from \(p_{\mathrm{train}}\). The system's decision on input \(x\) is \(\hat y(x)\in\{0,1\}\); for the door, \(\hat y=1\) means "unlock". A per-example loss \(\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)\) takes values in \([0,1]\); the 0–1 loss \(\mathbb 1[\hat y(x)\ne y]\), which is 1 when the decision is wrong and 0 otherwise, is the case used most below. The risk of the system under a distribution \(p\) is its expected loss,

\[\mathcal R_p=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim p}\,\mathcal L\bigl(\hat y(x),y\bigr),\]

which for the 0–1 loss is the probability of a wrong decision. A test set is a finite sample held out from training and drawn from a distribution \(p_{\mathrm{test}}\), usually equal to \(p_{\mathrm{train}}\); its average loss \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{test}}\) estimates \(\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{test}}}\). What the owner of the door cares about is \(\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}}\), and nothing in the training procedure measures it. Each later section either narrows the distance between these two numbers or measures it.

The recording draws a box labelled "DL system" with a much smaller box labelled "Models" inside it, and says that most courses concentrate on the small box because models can be benchmarked and published (speaker judgment, ≈287–343 s). Sculley et al. (2015) draw the same picture: their Figure 1 shows the machine-learning code as a small box inside the configuration, data-collection, feature-extraction, serving and monitoring infrastructure around it (p. 4). Breck et al. (2017) put the recording's equation another way: training is like compilation, "where the source is both code and training data", so the training data need testing as code does (p. 1).

The whiteboard lists the steps of a project in order: specify the problem, get data, design a model, train the model, deploy the model, and monitor and maintain it. A brace groups the three middle steps, and beside them a loop runs from "Design model" to "Train" to "Analyse" and back (≈343–545 s). The loop is repeated until the model performs well enough; only then does deployment begin. The chapter follows the same order.

The running case: a door that checks faces 5:43

Provenance. Lecture: face recognition to decide whether to unlock a door, the key-card variant in office buildings, the Siamese network that compares two pictures, registration pictures, and the reason for this design (no retraining for each home) (≈343–679 s). Research extension: Bromley et al. Original construction: the verification decision, the false-accept and false-reject rates, Proposition 1 and Figure 1.

A small camera beside the door photographs whoever approaches, and the system decides whether to unlock (≈379–434 s). The recording describes two uses. At a home, a few residents are allowed in. In an office complex that already issues key cards, a stolen card is the risk: when someone swipes a card, the system photographs the person and checks that the face matches the face registered for that card (≈405–464 and ≈650–679 s). The recording states that one of the lecturer's teams deployed such a check in large office complexes (speaker judgment, ≈405–434 s).

The recording's network is a Siamese network: it takes two pictures and answers whether they show the same person; the whiteboard draws two pictures entering one box labelled "NN" whose output is "Same?" (≈590–650 s). The design was introduced for signatures by Bromley et al. (1993), whose network "consists of two identical sub-networks joined at their outputs" (p. 1); the two sub-networks "were constrained to have identical weights" (p. 4). Verification then compares the feature vector of a new signature with a stored feature vector for the signer, and signatures closer than "a chosen threshold" are accepted (p. 1). The recording gives the practical reason for this design: training a new network for every home would be impractical, whereas a comparison network needs only a few registration pictures of each authorised person (≈621–650 s).

Verification, thresholds and the two error rates

Chapter 2 builds the encoder and the triplet loss that trains it; this chapter takes the trained encoder as given and restates only what the door needs. Let \(f_\theta\) be the network that maps a face image to an encoding, a vector in \(\mathbb R^{n_e}\); the two branches of a Siamese network both compute \(f_\theta\) because they share the parameters \(\theta\). The distance between two images is \(d(x_1,x_2)=\lVert f_\theta(x_1)-f_\theta(x_2)\rVert_2\). The verification decision with threshold \(c>0\) accepts the pair when \(d(x_1,x_2)<c\). A pair is genuine when both images show the same person and an impostor pair otherwise. The two error rates of the decision are

\[\mathrm{FAR}(c)=\mathbb P\bigl(d<c\mid\text{impostor pair}\bigr),\qquad \mathrm{FRR}(c)=\mathbb P\bigl(d\ge c\mid\text{genuine pair}\bigr),\]

the false-accept rate and the false-reject rate, with probabilities taken over pairs from a stated distribution. Raising \(c\) can only enlarge the event \(\{d<c\}\), so \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\) is nondecreasing in \(c\) and \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)\) is nonincreasing: a threshold trades one error for the other and cannot lower both. Bromley et al. report one such operating point for signatures: with the threshold set to detect 80% of forgeries, 95.5% of genuine signatures were accepted (p. 7; empirical result on their test set of 532 genuine signatures and 424 forgeries, p. 6). In the notation above, that point has a false-accept rate of 0.20 against forgeries and a false-reject rate of 0.045.

"Same person?" is not "authorised?"

The network answers one question about one pair of images. The door answers another question: is the person in front of it authorised? The two uses of the recording turn the network's answer into the door's answer differently. With a key card, the card names one identity, and the system makes one comparison, against that identity's registration picture; this is 1:1 verification. At a home, nobody names an identity, so the system compares the new picture with the registration pictures of all \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) authorised people and unlocks if any comparison accepts; this is a 1:\(N\) allow-list check. The door needs to know only that some enrolled identity matches, not which one; the identification task of chapter 2 also returns the identity. The recording calls both "face recognition". The difference changes the error rates.

Proposition 1 (false accepts against an allow-list). Let an impostor be compared with the registration pictures of \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) authorised people, and let each comparison falsely accept with probability \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\). The probability that the door opens satisfies \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\le\mathbb P(\text{open})\le N_{\mathrm{id}}\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\) whenever \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\ge1\). If the comparisons are independent, \(\mathbb P(\text{open})=1-(1-\mathrm{FAR}(c))^{N_{\mathrm{id}}}\).

Proof. The door opens exactly when at least one comparison accepts. The event contains the event that the first comparison accepts, which gives the lower bound. The probability of a union of \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) events is at most the sum of their probabilities, which gives the upper bound without any independence assumption. Under independence, the door stays shut only if every comparison rejects, which has probability \((1-\mathrm{FAR}(c))^{N_{\mathrm{id}}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Take \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)=10^{-4}\) per comparison, an assumption of this example rather than a measured value. A home with \(N_{\mathrm{id}}=5\) opens for a given impostor with probability at most \(5\times10^{-4}\). An office complex that used the same comparison as a 1:\(N\) allow-list check for \(N_{\mathrm{id}}=2{,}000\) employees would open with probability at most 0.2, and \(1-(1-10^{-4})^{2000}=1-e^{2000\ln0.9999}=0.181\) under independence (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The key-card design keeps the office at the per-comparison rate \(10^{-4}\), because the card reduces the question to one comparison. Independence is an idealisation. One impostor image that lies close to several enrolled encodings is accepted by several comparisons at once, so the accept events of one attempt are positively dependent, and only the union bound holds in general.

The recording poses the first practical question of the project: a three-person startup may not download face images from the Internet, so how should it obtain training pairs, and how long should it spend collecting them before running gradient descent for the first time (≈679–802 s)? Proposals from the audience (≈802–988 s) included recordings from video calls, a camera placed in a public spot where people opt in to be photographed, photographs of friends, student identity photographs, and a campus-wide email appeal.

The recording's answer is a principle, not a dataset: prefer the tactic that produces a dataset in one or two days, even if the dataset is smaller and of lower quality, because the builder cannot know which properties of the data matter until a model has been trained and its failures examined (speaker judgment, ≈988–1076 s). The lecturer states that the speed of execution is one of the strongest predictors of success for a startup or a small team inside a large company (speaker judgment, ≈988–1046 s); no study is named. The supporting anecdote concerns a chief executive who had bought a company for its data and then asked how to make money from it (speaker judgment, ≈1046–1104 s). In the lecturer's recollection the price was more than 100 million US dollars, and with certainty more than tens of millions. Student identity photographs illustrate the same uncertainty: they may be unrepresentative in ways nobody can predict, such as unusual expressions, until a model trained on them fails (≈1104–1133 s).

The lecturer's own practice is to collect with consent in places with many passers-by, such as campus cafeterias, and to set teams a fixed window, for example 48 hours from the moment of the meeting, to collect what they can (≈1133–1244 s). Asking first "what data do we need, and how long will that take?" leads in the lecturer's experience to slower execution (speaker judgment, ≈1244–1270 s). The practice is not new. Bromley et al. collected their signatures "in a university and at Bell Laboratories and NCR cafeterias" (p. 2). They report that the data collected away from Bell Laboratories, where collection "was not closely monitored", turned out "quite noisy" and had to be cleaned with explicit rules on stroke counts and names (p. 2). Fast collection produces a first dataset and a list of its defects; it does not remove the need to clean.

How long one loop takes

The whiteboard's loop has three stages. Let \(t_{\mathrm{data}}\) be the time to collect or revise the data for one pass, \(t_{\mathrm{train}}\) the time for one training run, and \(t_{\mathrm{anl}}\) the time to analyse the results, all in days. One pass round the loop takes

\[t_{\mathrm{loop}}=t_{\mathrm{data}}+t_{\mathrm{train}}+t_{\mathrm{anl}}.\]

The recording's example is training that takes about two days, set against data collection that takes either two days or two months (≈1270–1298 s). With \(t_{\mathrm{train}}=2\) and \(t_{\mathrm{anl}}=1\), two days of collection give \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}=5\) days and 18 loops in 90 days; two months of collection give \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}=63\) days and one loop in the same period (engineering check by shown arithmetic; \(t_{\mathrm{anl}}=1\) is an assumption of the example). The recording's rule is to spend on the data an amount of time commensurate with the time spent training (≈1270–1298 s).

Proposition 2 (the cost of slow data collection). Fix \(t_{\mathrm{train}}\) and \(t_{\mathrm{anl}}\), and let \(t_{\mathrm{fix}}=t_{\mathrm{train}}+t_{\mathrm{anl}}\). The number of loops per unit time, \(1/t_{\mathrm{loop}}\), never exceeds \(1/t_{\mathrm{fix}}\). If \(t_{\mathrm{data}}\le t_{\mathrm{fix}}\), it is at least half of that maximum. In general it equals the maximum divided by \(1+t_{\mathrm{data}}/t_{\mathrm{fix}}\).

Proof. \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}=t_{\mathrm{data}}+t_{\mathrm{fix}}\ge t_{\mathrm{fix}}\), which gives the maximum. If \(t_{\mathrm{data}}\le t_{\mathrm{fix}}\), then \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}\le2t_{\mathrm{fix}}\), so \(1/t_{\mathrm{loop}}\ge\tfrac12\cdot1/t_{\mathrm{fix}}\). In general \(t_{\mathrm{loop}}=t_{\mathrm{fix}}(1+t_{\mathrm{data}}/t_{\mathrm{fix}})\). \(\blacksquare\)

The proposition turns "commensurate" into a number. Collection time no longer than the rest of the loop costs at most a factor of 2 in the rate of learning about the data; two months against three days of training and analysis gives \(t_{\mathrm{data}}/t_{\mathrm{fix}}=20\), a factor of 21. The same arithmetic explains the recording's qualification: when one training run of a large model takes weeks or months, spending months on the data is proportionate (≈1316–1345 s). The lecturer reports that fast teams go round the loop once a day for small models, with training running overnight or in a few hours (speaker judgment, ≈1298–1345 s).

The proposition measures only the rate of loops. It assumes that the value of one loop does not depend on how long its data took to collect. That assumption fails when a model cannot work at all below some amount of data, and the recording names this exception: from previous face systems and from the literature, the lecturer expects some face-recognition systems to need at least 50,000 images before they have any hope of working, and would then invest in collecting them up front (speaker judgment, ≈1403–1466 s).

A published counterpart shows the size of such requirements. Taigman et al. (2014) trained their face representation on 4.4 million labelled faces of 4,030 people (p. 5). When they fine-tuned a Siamese network on pairs generated from roughly 9,000 photographs, they observed "substantial overfitting", since those photographs were "insufficient to reliably estimate more than 120M parameters" (p. 7; empirical result for that network). A data requirement of this kind is a property of the model and the task, which is why the recording limits the exception to builders with experience or literature on the same problem.

Which group of errors to attack first

Partition the inputs the system will meet into disjoint groups, called slices, for example "wearing a hat", "wearing glasses" and "neither". Write \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})\) for the probability that a deployed input falls in a slice and \(\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}\) for the error rate on it. By the law of total probability the overall error rate is

\[\mathrm{Err}=\sum_{\text{slices}}\mathbb P(\text{slice})\,\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}.\]

Proposition 3 (the ceiling of a targeted fix). If a change to the data lowers the error on one slice from \(\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}\) to any value \(\mathrm{Err}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\text{slice}}\ge0\) and leaves the error on every other slice unchanged, the overall error falls by \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})(\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}-\mathrm{Err}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\text{slice}})\), which is at most \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})\,\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}\).

Proof. Only one term of the sum changes, from \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})\mathrm{Err}_{\text{slice}}\) to \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})\mathrm{Err}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\text{slice}}\), and \(\mathrm{Err}^{\mathrm{new}}_{\text{slice}}\ge0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Suppose, as an assumption of this example, that 5% of the people at the door wear hats and the system errs on 20% of them, while 30% wear glasses and it errs on 4% of them. The hat slice has five times the error rate, but a perfect fix for hats lowers the overall error by at most \(0.05\times0.20=0.010\), and a perfect fix for glasses by at most \(0.30\times0.04=0.012\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The slice to attack first is the one with the larger product, and the product needs \(\mathbb P(\text{slice})\) under the deployment distribution, not under the training set. Two limits apply. The partition must be chosen before the numbers are read, or the largest product will partly reflect the choice of groups. And a change aimed at one slice can change the errors on others, since one network serves all of them; the proposition bounds the contribution of the targeted slice only.

For large language models the recording names Claude as ahead in coding for some time, with OpenAI and Gemini 2.5 Pro making progress (product claims as of the recording, ≈1734–1790 s), and states that frontier developers improve coding by acquiring high-quality coding data rather than more text of every kind (speaker judgment, ≈1763–1861 s). For faces it contrasts sharp with blurry images (speaker judgment, ≈1861–1968 s). Taigman et al. give one related observation for faces: frames of YouTube videos have worse image quality than web photographs, "mainly due to motion blur or viewing distance" (p. 7), and their representation reached 91.4% accuracy on the YouTube Faces benchmark against 97.35% on Labeled Faces in the Wild (pp. 1, 7; empirical result). The two benchmarks differ in more than image quality, so the gap is not a controlled measurement of the effect of blur.

Provenance. Lecture: whether the training data must come from the distribution the system will meet; the answer that a large network can absorb loosely relevant data, such as cartoon faces, usually without harm and sometimes with benefit; incorrect data as the exception; the older practice of matching distributions exactly; the Sherlock Holmes "attic" (≈1979–2176 s). Asked whether the collected data must resemble the data the system will meet, the recording answers that it matters less than most people think. A large network can be given many kinds of data, including simulated cartoon characters that look unlike real people, and this usually does no harm and may help a little; how much it helps is an empirical question for each problem (speaker judgment, ≈1979–2065 s). The recording contrasts this with practice ten to fifteen years ago, when small models were thought to be distracted by irrelevant data, and compares a large network to a brain with room to spare and a small one to an attic that must forget to learn (speaker judgment, ≈2065–2176 s). Incorrect data are excluded explicitly: the recording says they would cause a problem (≈2092–2121 s). This section states when the claim is a theorem, when it fails, and why the lecturer's exclusion of incorrect data is necessary.

Two ways a distribution can change

Every joint distribution of inputs and labels factors as \(p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y\mid x)\): the distribution of inputs, times the conditional distribution of the label given the input. Two distributions \(p_{\mathrm{train}}\) and \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}\) can differ in either factor.

  • Covariate shift: \(p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\ne p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)\) while \(p_{\mathrm{train}}(y\mid x)=p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(y\mid x)\). The inputs change; the correct answer for any given input does not. Rabanser, Günnemann and Lipton write the target distribution under this assumption as \(q(x)\,p(y\mid x)\) (2019, p. 2).
  • Concept shift: \(p_{\mathrm{train}}(y\mid x)\ne p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(y\mid x)\) for inputs that both distributions produce. The same input now calls for a different answer.

Adding cartoon faces to a training set, with correct labels, changes only the input factor of the training distribution: it is covariate shift between training and deployment. Adding pairs with wrong labels changes the conditional factor: it is concept shift. The recording's exclusion of incorrect data therefore separates the two cases exactly.

Proposition 4 (when extra inputs cannot change the best predictor). Let \(\mathcal L\) be a loss, and suppose the class of predictors contains a function \(f^*\) that, at every input \(x\) that either distribution can produce, minimises the conditional expected loss \(\mathbb E[\mathcal L(f(x),y)\mid x]\) over all predictions. Then \(f^*\) minimises the risk under every distribution of the form \(p(x)\,p(y\mid x)\) with the same conditional factor, whatever the input distribution \(p(x)\) on those inputs. In particular, mixing the target inputs with any amount of other inputs, correctly labelled, leaves \(f^*\) optimal.

Proof. The risk under \(p(x)\,p(y\mid x)\) is \(\mathbb E_{x\sim p}\bigl[\mathbb E[\mathcal L(f(x),y)\mid x]\bigr]\). For every \(x\), the inner expectation is smallest at \(f=f^*\) by assumption, so the average over any \(p(x)\) is smallest at \(f^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

A class that contains such an \(f^*\) is called correctly specified. The proposition is the population version of a result that Sugiyama, Krauledat and Müller (2007) state for estimators and attribute to Shimodaira (2000). An estimator is consistent when it converges to the risk minimiser as \(m\) grows. Under covariate shift, ordinary empirical risk minimisation "is not generally consistent anymore", but "if the model is correct, ERM is still consistent even under the covariate shift" (p. 3). For a misspecified model they give an importance-weighted variant as the consistent one (p. 4); the drift section derives the weighting.

The lecturer's picture of a large network fits the proposition: a model with enough capacity to represent the best predictor on the target inputs and on the extra inputs at once loses nothing from the extra inputs in the population limit. The picture of the small brain is the misspecified case. A class that cannot fit both regions must trade errors between them, and the trade is weighted by how much input mass each region has; extra inputs elsewhere pull the fit away from the target region. And the proposition says nothing about wrong labels, because they change \(p(y\mid x)\) and hence \(f^*\) itself.

The arithmetic of a two-stage cascade

Index the camera frames by \(t\), one step per frame. Let \(C_1\) be the cost of running the gate on one frame and \(C_2\) the cost of running the encoder and the comparison, in core-seconds, and let \(p_{\mathrm{fire}}\) be the probability that the gate passes a frame on. A frame is genuine when it shows an authorised person who should be admitted.

Proposition 5 (cost and recall of a cascade). (a) The expected cost per frame is \(C_1+p_{\mathrm{fire}}C_2\). (b) For a genuine frame,

\[\mathbb P(\text{accept}\mid\text{genuine})=\mathbb P(\text{gate fires}\mid\text{genuine})\;\mathbb P(\text{encoder accepts}\mid\text{gate fired, genuine}).\]

(c) The last factor equals the encoder's recall on all genuine frames, \(\mathbb P(\text{encoder accepts}\mid\text{genuine})\), if and only if the encoder's decision is independent of the gate's decision on genuine frames.

Proof. (a) The gate runs on every frame; the encoder runs on a frame with probability \(p_{\mathrm{fire}}\); expectation is linear. (b) The cascade accepts exactly when the gate fires and the encoder then accepts, and the probability of an intersection is the probability of the first event times the conditional probability of the second. (c) This is the definition of independence of the two events, conditional on a genuine frame. \(\blacksquare\)

Part (b) is exact; the familiar shortcut "recall of the cascade equals the product of the recalls of the stages" is part (b) with the hypothesis of part (c) added. Viola and Jones describe why the hypothesis fails in their detector: later stages are trained on the examples that pass the earlier ones, so "the second classifier faces a more difficult task than the first" (p. 5). The door has its own reason, which is this book's reasoning and not a measurement: a gate that responds to change fires more readily on frames in which the person is moving, and moving frames are the blurred ones on which the encoder does worst.

Suppose, as assumptions of this example, that half the frames of an approach are blurred, that the gate fires on 95% of blurred and 30% of sharp frames, and that the encoder accepts 80% of blurred and 98% of sharp genuine frames. Then \(p_{\mathrm{fire}}=0.625\) on these frames, the encoder's recall over all of them is 0.89, and the product shortcut gives \(0.625\times0.89=0.556\). The exact value is \(0.5\times0.95\times0.80+0.5\times0.30\times0.98=0.527\), because the encoder's recall on the frames the gate passes is only \(0.527/0.625=0.843\) (engineering check; the code asserts both values). The shortcut overstates what the cascade delivers per frame.

Part (a) with the numbers above: if the gate passes 2% of all frames over a day, an assumption of this example, the encoder's load at the 2014 timing falls from 9.9 to \(0.02\times9.9=0.198\) core-seconds per second, plus the gate's own cost \(C_1\), which for a pixel comparison is a few operations per pixel.

Proposition 6 (several frames per approach). Suppose the system compares \(n_{\mathrm{fr}}\) frames of one approach and unlocks if any comparison accepts. Let the \(j\)-th frame of an authorised person be accepted with probability \(p_{\mathrm{acc},j}\), and let each frame of an impostor be falsely accepted with probability \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\). Then \(\mathbb P(\text{unlock}\mid\text{authorised})\ge\max_j p_{\mathrm{acc},j}\); if the frames are accepted independently, \(\mathbb P(\text{unlock}\mid\text{authorised})=1-\prod_{j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{fr}}}(1-p_{\mathrm{acc},j})\). For an impostor, \(\mathbb P(\text{unlock}\mid\text{impostor})\le n_{\mathrm{fr}}\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\). For a home allow-list of \(N_{\mathrm{id}}\) people, \(\mathbb P(\text{unlock}\mid\text{impostor})\le n_{\mathrm{fr}}N_{\mathrm{id}}\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\).

Proof. The unlock event contains the event that frame \(j\) is accepted, for each \(j\). Under independence the door stays shut only if every frame is rejected. A reference level: human performance and Bayes error 52:20

Provenance. Lecture: what counts as good accuracy; human-level performance as an aspirational reference that makes bias and variance easier to diagnose; the statement that face verification systems now exceed humans; tasks such as recommendation where humans are poor and no reference exists (≈3140–3301 s). Research extension: Taigman et al., Table 3. Original construction: the definition of Bayes error and Proposition 7.

Asked what accuracy is good enough, the recording answers that building a system is easier with a reference level, often the accuracy of a human expert, and that the diagnosis of bias and variance later in the course uses such a level (≈3140–3214 s). A reference level is useful only if its relation to what is achievable is clear, and that relation is a theorem.

For a classification task with classes \(k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\) and inputs distributed as \(p(x)\), the Bayes error is the smallest error rate any decision rule can have,

\[\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}=\mathbb E_{x}\Bigl[1-\max_k\,p(k\mid x)\Bigr],\]

attained by the rule that picks the most probable class for each \(x\). It is positive when some inputs are genuinely ambiguous, for example two different people who look alike in a blurred picture.

Proposition 7 (a human is one decision rule). Let \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\) be the error rate of a human who labels each input using only the input itself, possibly with some randomness of their own that, given \(x\), is independent of the true label. Then \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\ge\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\).

Proof. Fix \(x\), and let the human answer class \(k\) with probability \(p^{\mathrm{hum}}_k(x)\), where \(\sum_kp^{\mathrm{hum}}_k(x)=1\). The probability of a correct answer is \(\sum_kp^{\mathrm{hum}}_k(x)p(k\mid x)\le\max_kp(k\mid x)\), because a weighted average cannot exceed the largest term. So the error at \(x\) is at least \(1-\max_kp(k\mid x)\); average over \(x\). \(\blacksquare\)

Human-level error is therefore an upper bound on the achievable floor, not the floor itself. Two consequences follow. When the training error of a model exceeds \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\), the difference \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\) underestimates the room for improvement \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\). When the model is already better than the human, the human level no longer says how much better it could become, which is the recording's remark that progress gets harder to judge once a system surpasses people (≈3240–3266 s). The hypothesis matters: a human who uses information that is not in \(x\), such as knowing the person, is not a rule on \(x\) and is not bounded this way.

The recording states that under controlled conditions face verification systems are better than humans, perhaps better than all humans, at deciding whether two pictures show the same person (speaker judgment about present systems, ≈3214–3240 s). A published measurement bounds this for one benchmark and one year. On Labeled Faces in the Wild, Taigman et al. report an accuracy of \(0.9735\pm0.0025\) for their best ensemble against 0.9753 for humans on cropped faces (Table 3, p. 7), and describe the result as "closely approaching human-level performance" (p. 1; empirical result, 2014). In error rates that is 2.65% against 2.47%. The human figure is itself an estimate on that benchmark's pairs, so by Proposition 7 it bounds the benchmark's Bayes error only up to its own sampling error; it does not describe systems built since 2014, which were not checked here. For recommendation, the recording's example of a task at which people are poor, there is no human reference to anchor the target (speaker judgment, ≈3240–3301 s).

A test-set number and a working system

The recording describes engineers who consider the job done once the test-set error is low, and product owners who reply that the system does not work; it asks the engineer to find out why good test performance does not carry over, with a change of distribution as the common cause (speaker judgment, ≈3390–3473 s). The distance between the two numbers can be stated exactly. For two distributions \(p\) and \(q\) of \((x,y)\), the total variation distance is the largest difference in the probability they assign to any event,

\[\mathrm{TV}(p,q)=\sup_{E}\,\bigl\lvert p(E)-q(E)\bigr\rvert=\tfrac12\int\sum_y\bigl\lvert p(x,y)-q(x,y)\bigr\rvert\,dx.\]

The two forms agree. For any event \(E\), \(p(E)-q(E)\le\int\sum_y\mathbb 1[(x,y)\in E_{+}]\,(p-q)=(p-q)(E_{+})\), with \(E_{+}\) the event where \(p>q\). Since \(\int\sum_y(p-q)=0\), the positive part \((p-q)(E_{+})\) equals half of \(\int\sum_y\lvert p-q\rvert\). The event \(E_{+}\) itself attains the supremum, and the same argument with \(p\) and \(q\) exchanged bounds \(q(E)-p(E)\) by the same number.

Proposition 8 (what a test-set risk says about deployment). For any system and any loss with values in \([0,1]\), \(\lvert\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}}-\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{test}}}\rvert\le\mathrm{TV}(p_{\mathrm{test}},p_{\mathrm{deploy}})\). Under covariate shift, the right side equals the total variation distance between the two input distributions alone.

Proof. Let \(E_{+}\) be the event \(\{(x,y):p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x,y)>p_{\mathrm{test}}(x,y)\}\). Then \(\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}}-\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{test}}}=\int\sum_y\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)\,(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}-p_{\mathrm{test}})\le\int\sum_y\mathbb 1[(x,y)\in E_{+}]\,(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}-p_{\mathrm{test}})=p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(E_{+})-p_{\mathrm{test}}(E_{+})\le\mathrm{TV}\), since \(\mathcal L\le1\) where the difference is positive and \(\mathcal L\ge0\) where it is negative. Exchanging the two distributions gives the other direction. Under covariate shift both joints have the same factor \(p(y\mid x)\), and \(\sum_y p(y\mid x)\,\lvert p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)-p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)\rvert=\lvert p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)-p_{\mathrm{test}}(x)\rvert\). \(\blacksquare\)

Suppose that in summer 30% of the people at the door wear sunglasses, and that the training and test photographs contained none. Then the summer input distribution puts probability 0.3 on a set the test distribution never produced, the distance is at least 0.3, and the proposition allows the summer error to exceed the test error by up to 30 percentage points. If the remaining 70% of summer inputs are distributed as the test inputs, the distance is exactly 0.3. The bound is attained: a system that fails on every face in sunglasses and is perfect otherwise has test error 0 and summer error 0.3. A test-set error thus certifies deployed behaviour only to within the distance between the two distributions, and that distance is unknown at test time. The door adds a second gap, independent of shift: a test set of face pairs measures the encoder, not the gate's misses or the frames it chose (Proposition 5).

Reweighting old data, and where it stops

Under covariate shift, old labelled data can still estimate the new risk, provided the new inputs are ones the old data could have produced. Define the importance weight \(\omega(x)=p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)/p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\).

Proposition 9 (importance weighting). Assume covariate shift and that \(p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)>0\) wherever \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)>0\). Then \(\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}}=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim p_{\mathrm{train}}}\bigl[\omega(x)\,\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)\bigr]\). If the loss lies in \([0,1]\), the average of this quantity over \(m\) independent training examples is unbiased and has variance at most \(\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{train}}}[\omega^2]/m\).

Proof. \(\mathcal R_{p_{\mathrm{deploy}}}=\int p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)\sum_yp(y\mid x)\,\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)\,dx=\int p_{\mathrm{train}}(x)\,\omega(x)\sum_yp(y\mid x)\,\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)\,dx\); the support assumption makes \(\omega\) finite wherever \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}\) has mass, and the shared \(p(y\mid x)\) is what allows the rewrite. The variance of one term is at most its second moment, \(\mathbb E[\omega^2\,\mathcal L(\hat y(x),y)^2]\le\mathbb E[\omega^2]\), and averaging \(m\) independent terms divides it by \(m\). \(\blacksquare\)

The bound suggests the effective sample size \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}=m/\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{train}}}[\omega^2]\), the number of unweighted examples with the same variance bound. Since \(\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{train}}}[\omega]=\int p_{\mathrm{deploy}}(x)\,dx=1\), Jensen's inequality gives \(\mathbb E[\omega^2]\ge(\mathbb E[\omega])^2=1\), so \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}\le m\). For one input coordinate with \(p_{\mathrm{train}}=\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\), the ratio of the two densities is \(\omega(x)=\exp\bigl(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^2+\tfrac12x^2\bigr)=e^{\mu x-\mu^2/2}\). For a standard normal \(x\), \(\mathbb E[e^{2\mu x}]=e^{2\mu^2}\), because \(-\tfrac12x^2+2\mu x=-\tfrac12(x-2\mu)^2+2\mu^2\) and the shifted Gaussian integrates to 1. So \(\mathbb E[\omega^2]=e^{-\mu^2}\,\mathbb E[e^{2\mu x}]=e^{-\mu^2}e^{2\mu^2}=e^{\mu^2}\), and \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}=m\,e^{-\mu^2}\): with \(m=10{,}000\), 3,679 at \(\mu=1\) and 183 at \(\mu=2\) (exercise 5 tabulates more values).

The laboratory program estimates the deployed error of a rule that errs when \(x>1.5\). At \(\mu=1\) the weighted estimate is 0.311 against the true 0.309; at \(\mu=2\) it is 0.639 against 0.691. The sample version of the effective size, \((\sum\omega)^2/\sum\omega^2\), is 511, well above the population value 183, because the draws that carry the largest weights are rare and mostly absent from one sample (engineering check). Sugiyama et al. report the same weakness of the weighted estimator: it is consistent but "not efficient and can be rather unstable" (p. 4).

The support condition is where the correction stops. If no training face wore sunglasses, \(p_{\mathrm{train}}\) is zero where the summer distribution is not, \(\omega\) is undefined there, and no reweighting of the old data can estimate the summer error. New data from the new region are the only remedy, which is the recording's prescription: update the distribution of the training data to capture what has changed (≈3446–3473 s). Under concept shift reweighting cannot help even with full support, because \(p(y\mid x)\) itself has changed and the old labels are wrong for the new mapping.

Three published practices address the same problem. Taigman et al. held out the most recent 5% of each identity's images, by time stamp, "in order to simulate continuous identification through aging" (p. 5), a test split that anticipates one drift. Rabanser et al. observe that machine-learning systems "tend to fail silently" and, across the shifts they study, find that a two-sample test on representations from a pretrained classifier detects shift best among the methods they compare (p. 1; empirical result for their datasets and shifts). Sculley et al. warn that a manually chosen decision threshold "may be invalid" once a model is retrained on new data (p. 7). For the door, \(c\) must be chosen again on current validation pairs after each update, because \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\) and \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)\) move with the encoder.

Why the simple gate shrugs off sunglasses 59:40

The recording contrasts the two gates under drift. Option 1 has in effect one parameter, the fraction of changed pixels, and is robust because it is simple: sunglasses barely change how many pixels change. A network with thousands of parameters trained when nobody wore sunglasses is more likely to have fitted that condition and to need updating (speaker judgment, ≈3580–3704 s). Two corrections sharpen the claim. The gate as described has two thresholds, \(c_{\mathrm{pix}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{frac}}\), not one. And the robustness comes from what the gate measures, not from how many numbers were tuned.

Proposition 10 (invariance of a statistic-based gate). Suppose the gate's decision on frame \(t\) is a function of \(\mathrm{Chg}_t\) alone, and suppose a change in the world leaves the distribution of \(\mathrm{Chg}_t\) unchanged, both on frames with a person and on frames without one. Then the gate's miss rate and false-fire rate are unchanged.

Proof. Each rate is the probability of the event \(\{\mathrm{Chg}_t>c_{\mathrm{frac}}\}\) or its complement under one of the two conditional distributions of \(\mathrm{Chg}_t\), and those distributions have not changed. \(\blacksquare\)

Sunglasses satisfy the hypothesis in the synthetic scene: a person covers the same pixels with or without them, and the changed fraction is 0.312 in both cases (engineering check). Other changes violate it. Switching on a light changes every pixel, and the changed fraction of an empty doorway jumps to 1.000, so the one-threshold gate fires on every frame until the reference frame catches up. At night, higher sensor noise raises the fraction of pixels whose difference exceeds \(c_{\mathrm{pix}}\) even when nothing moves (exercise 3). A simple gate is robust to the changes its statistic ignores and fragile to the changes its statistic sees. A network gate can be robust or fragile to either kind; whether a given network fits the absence of sunglasses is an empirical question the recording answers from experience, not a consequence of its parameter count.

The false alarms of a band

Let \(p_{\mathrm{re}}\) be the probability that an attempt needs re-authentication while the system works normally, and let the door see \(n_{\mathrm{att}}\) attempts a day. The daily fraction of re-authentications, \(\hat p_{\mathrm{re}}\), is an estimate of \(p_{\mathrm{re}}\); if attempts are independent, the count is binomial and the fraction \(\hat p_{\mathrm{re}}\) has standard deviation \(\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{re}}(1-p_{\mathrm{re}})/n_{\mathrm{att}}}\). A band with edges \(c_{\mathrm{lo}}=p_{\mathrm{re}}-3\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{re}}(1-p_{\mathrm{re}})/n_{\mathrm{att}}}\) and \(c_{\mathrm{hi}}=p_{\mathrm{re}}+3\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{re}}(1-p_{\mathrm{re}})/n_{\mathrm{att}}}\) raises an alarm on a day when \(\hat p_{\mathrm{re}}\) falls outside \([c_{\mathrm{lo}},c_{\mathrm{hi}}]\). Write \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}\) for the probability of an alarm on a normal day.

Proposition 11 (false pages from many dashboards). If \(n_{\mathrm{met}}\) metrics each raise a false alarm on a normal day with probability \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}\), the probability of at least one false page on that day is at most \(n_{\mathrm{met}}\,p_{\mathrm{fa}}\), and equals \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{fa}})^{n_{\mathrm{met}}}\) if the metrics are independent. The expected number of false alarms in \(n_{\mathrm{day}}\) normal days is \(n_{\mathrm{day}}n_{\mathrm{met}}p_{\mathrm{fa}}\), with or without independence.

Proof. The first statement is the union bound and the product formula of Proposition 1 with the metrics in place of the comparisons. The expected count is a sum of \(n_{\mathrm{day}}n_{\mathrm{met}}\) indicator variables, each with expectation \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}\), and expectation is linear whether or not they are independent. \(\blacksquare\)

Take \(p_{\mathrm{re}}=0.05\) and \(n_{\mathrm{att}}=400\), assumptions of this example. The standard deviation is 0.0109 and the band is \([0.0173,0.0827]\), so an alarm fires when fewer than 7 or more than 33 of the 400 attempts need re-authentication. The exact binomial probability of that on a normal day is \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.00227\), somewhat below the normal approximation 0.0027. One such metric pages falsely about once every 440 days. Fifty such metrics give \(1-(1-0.00227)^{50}=0.107\): a false page on about one day in nine. The expected number of false alarms per year is \(365\times50\times0.00227=41.4\), and a simulated year produced 37 (engineering check). Pruning the dashboards to the few that matter, as the recording recommends, lowers the false pages in proportion to \(n_{\mathrm{met}}\).

The band must also catch real changes. If summer sunglasses raise \(p_{\mathrm{re}}\) from 0.05 to 0.08, the daily count exceeds 33 with probability 0.382; over seven independent days the change is caught with probability \(1-0.618^7=0.966\) (engineering check). A wider band pages less often and detects later; averaging over a week narrows the band and delays every detection by up to a week. Breck et al. recommend watching for both "dramatic and slow-leak regressions" in served prediction quality (p. 7), which is the same trade: a slow leak shows only in averages over longer windows.

Maintenance closes the loop. When a metric leaves its band, the response is the one the recording ends with: collect new data from the changed world, update the system, and re-examine the thresholds (≈3969–4019 s). Deployment does not end the project; it adds a data source.

A laboratory for the running case in NumPy

Provenance. Original construction throughout. It implements option 1 of the gate and Proposition 10, Proposition 5, Proposition 4 through least squares, Proposition 9, and Proposition 11.

The program below computes the chapter's numerical examples in one run. Its inputs are fixed constants and seeded random draws: a synthetic \(48\times64\) door scene, the assumed gate and encoder rates of the cascade example, a least-squares problem with a target region and an irrelevant region, a one-dimensional Gaussian covariate shift, and a re-authentication rate of 0.05 over 400 daily attempts. It has no side effects beyond printing. Each check is an assert, so a changed formula or constant stops the program with an AssertionError. It needs Python 3 and NumPy (tested with Python 3.13.5 and NumPy 2.2.6) and ran in a fresh process in under one second.

import math
import numpy as np
H, W = 48, 64                                   # frame size in pixels
yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W]
WALL = 100.0 + 40.0 * xx / W
LEAVES = 120.0 + 60.0 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * xx / 12) * np.cos(
    2 * np.pi * yy / 9)
TREE = (xx >= 48) & (yy < 36)
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)


def scene(shift=0, person=False, glasses=False, car=False, cat=False,
          light=0.0):
    """One grey frame of the door camera; arguments are scene events."""
    f = WALL + 3.0 * rng.standard_normal((H, W))
    f[TREE] = np.roll(LEAVES, shift, axis=1)[TREE]
    if person:
        f[8:, 20:44] = 50.0
        f[8:18, 26:38] = 180.0
        if glasses:
            f[11:14, 27:37] = 20.0
    if car:
        f[10:14, 2:8] = 30.0
    if cat:
        f[H - 20:, 36:60] = 60.0
    return np.clip(f + light, 0.0, 255.0)


def chg(prev, cur, c_pix=25.0):
    """Fraction of pixels whose value changed by more than c_pix."""
    return float(np.mean(np.abs(cur - prev) > c_pix))


def tail(p, n, k_lo, k_hi):
    """P(count < k_lo or count > k_hi) for a Binomial(n, p) count."""
    pmf = [math.comb(n, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (n - k)
           for k in range(n + 1)]
    return sum(pmf[:k_lo]) + sum(pmf[k_hi + 1:])


# 1. Option 1: the pixel-change gate, c_pix = 25 and c_frac = 0.10.
ref = scene()
events = {"empty": {}, "person": {"person": True},
          "person+glasses": {"person": True, "glasses": True},
          "light wind": {"shift": 1}, "strong wind": {"shift": 6},
          "distant car": {"car": True}, "cat": {"cat": True},
          "lights on": {"light": 40.0}}
frac = {k: chg(ref, scene(**v)) for k, v in events.items()}
fires = {k: v > 0.10 for k, v in frac.items()}
assert frac["empty"] == 0.0 and fires["person"] and fires["cat"]
assert abs(frac["person"] - frac["person+glasses"]) < 0.01
assert not fires["light wind"] and fires["strong wind"]
assert not fires["distant car"] and frac["lights on"] == 1.0
# 2. Cascade: exact conditional recall versus the product of marginals.
w = {"blur": 0.5, "sharp": 0.5}           # frame types while approaching
fire = {"blur": 0.95, "sharp": 0.30}      # P(gate fires | frame type)
acc = {"blur": 0.80, "sharp": 0.98}       # P(recogniser accepts | type)
p_fire = sum(w[k] * fire[k] for k in w)
recall_alone = sum(w[k] * acc[k] for k in w)
joint = sum(w[k] * fire[k] * acc[k] for k in w)
assert abs(joint - 0.527) < 1e-12
assert abs(p_fire * recall_alone - 0.55625) < 1e-12
# 3. Irrelevant training data: cubic versus degree-9 least squares.
P = np.polynomial.polynomial
grid = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 1001)
xr = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, 200)                 # relevant inputs
yr = np.sin(3 * xr) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(200)
xi = rng.uniform(2.0, 3.0, 800)                 # irrelevant inputs
yi = np.sin(3 * xi) + 0.1 * rng.standard_normal(800)
xw = rng.uniform(0.0, 1.0, 200)                 # relevant, wrong labels
sets = {"relevant": (xr, yr),
        "+irrelevant": (np.r_[xr, xi], np.r_[yr, yi]),
        "+wrong labels": (np.r_[xr, xw], np.r_[yr, -np.sin(3 * xw)])}
mse = {}
for deg in (3, 9):
    for name, (xs, ys) in sets.items():
        fit = P.polyval(grid, P.polyfit(xs, ys, deg))
        mse[deg, name] = float(np.mean((fit - np.sin(3 * grid)) ** 2))
assert mse[3, "+irrelevant"] > 200 * mse[3, "relevant"]
assert mse[9, "+irrelevant"] < mse[9, "relevant"]
assert min(mse[3, "+wrong labels"], mse[9, "+wrong labels"]) > 0.1
# 4. Covariate shift: importance weights and effective sample size.
m = 10000
x = rng.standard_normal(m)                      # p_train = N(0, 1)
loss = (x > 1.5).astype(float)                  # error region of a rule
iw = {}
for mu in (1.0, 2.0):                           # p_deploy = N(mu, 1)
    om = np.exp(mu * x - 0.5 * mu ** 2)
    truth = 0.5 * math.erfc((1.5 - mu) / math.sqrt(2.0))
    iw[mu] = (round(float(np.mean(om * loss)), 3), round(truth, 3),
              round(float(om.sum() ** 2 / (om ** 2).sum())),
              round(m * math.exp(-mu ** 2)))
assert abs(iw[1.0][0] - iw[1.0][1]) < 0.03
# 5. Monitoring band on the daily re-authentication rate.
p_re, n_att, n_met = 0.05, 400, 50
sd = math.sqrt(p_re * (1 - p_re) / n_att)
lo, hi = p_re - 3 * sd, p_re + 3 * sd
k_lo, k_hi = math.ceil(lo * n_att), math.floor(hi * n_att)
p_fa = tail(p_re, n_att, k_lo, k_hi)
p_day = 1 - (1 - p_fa) ** n_met
detect = tail(0.08, n_att, k_lo, k_hi)
sims = rng.binomial(n_att, p_re, size=(365, n_met))
pages = int(np.sum((sims < k_lo) | (sims > k_hi)))
assert 0.001 < p_fa < 0.006 and abs(pages - 365 * n_met * p_fa) < 40
print("gate fraction changed:", {k: round(v, 3) for k, v in frac.items()})
print("cascade: P(fire) =", p_fire, "| exact recall =", round(joint, 4),
      "| product =", round(p_fire * recall_alone, 4),
      "| recall given fire =", round(joint / p_fire, 4))
print("relevant-region MSE:", {k: round(v, 4) for k, v in mse.items()})
print("importance weighting (estimate, truth, ESS, m*exp(-mu^2)):", iw)
print("band =", (round(lo, 4), round(hi, 4)), "| counts", (k_lo, k_hi))
print("per-metric false alarm =", round(p_fa, 5), "| P(any of 50) =",
      round(p_day, 3), "| pages in 365 days =", pages)
print("daily detection of a drift to 8% =", round(detect, 3))

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction.

Each result of this chapter holds under a stated idealisation, and each idealisation can fail in the running case.

Independence. Propositions 1, 6 and 11 give exact values under independence and bounds without it. Comparisons of one impostor with several registration pictures, frames of one approach, and dashboards driven by one underlying change are all positively dependent, so the independent formulas overstate the recall gained from more frames and misstate the false-page rate; the union bounds remain valid.

Distributions. Propositions 8 and 9 compare two distributions that are never both observed at test time. The total variation distance is unknown when the test is run, and importance weights must be estimated, which the chapter did not treat. Neither result helps where the deployed distribution puts mass on inputs the training data never contained, or under concept shift.

Specification. Proposition 4 is a population statement about a correctly specified class. It does not say that a given network is correctly specified, and it says nothing about finite data or a fixed training budget.

Proxies. The monitored quantities are proxies: re-authentication for false rejects, the accept rate for a shift in predictions. A false accept, the most serious failure at a door, produces no re-authentication and may not change the accept rate measurably, so none of the dashboards named in the recording measures it; it can be sampled only by reviewing logged decisions against ground truth.

Claims not checked. The lecturer's reports of deployed systems, the purchased-dataset anecdote, the 50,000-image requirement, the effect of selecting five frames, the claim that present face systems exceed humans, and the remarks about language-model products are speaker judgments or product claims recorded with their times.

What you can now do. You can state the difference between "same person?" and "authorised?", compute how an allow-list multiplies false accepts, and choose between 1:1 and 1:\(N\) designs. You can express "spend on data about as long as on training" as a bound on the rate of the development loop, and rank error slices by their contribution to the overall error. You can state when extra training data from another distribution cannot change the best predictor and construct the two ways it can. Exercises

1. Card or allow-list for an office

An office has \(N_{\mathrm{id}}=2{,}000\) employees. Security requires that an impostor who approaches once is admitted with probability at most \(10^{-3}\), and the system compares \(n_{\mathrm{fr}}=5\) frames per approach. (a) What per-comparison false-accept rate guarantees this for a 1:\(N\) allow-list check without any independence assumption? (b) For the key-card design? (c) What does meeting the target in (a) cost on the other error rate, and what information would you need to quantify that cost?

Solution

(a) By Proposition 6 the impostor is admitted with probability at most \(n_{\mathrm{fr}}N_{\mathrm{id}}\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)=10{,}000\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\). Requiring this to be at most \(10^{-3}\) gives \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\le10^{-7}\). The union bound needs no independence, so this suffices whatever the dependence between comparisons. (b) With a card, the approach involves one identity and five frames, so \(5\,\mathrm{FAR}(c)\le10^{-3}\) gives \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\le2\times10^{-4}\), a requirement 2,000 times weaker. (c) \(\mathrm{FAR}(c)\) is nondecreasing in \(c\), so meeting \(10^{-7}\) requires a threshold at least as small as the one for \(2\times10^{-4}\), and \(\mathrm{FRR}(c)\) is nonincreasing in \(c\), so the false-reject rate can only rise. How much it rises depends on the distributions of the distance \(d\) for genuine and impostor pairs, which must be measured on a validation set of pairs from the deployed population; with an estimated impostor rate of \(10^{-7}\), such a set needs far more than \(10^{7}\) impostor pairs to measure it at all. The card design is the one that makes the target checkable.

2. A gate that prefers motion

Half of the frames of an approach are blurred. A gate fires on 99% of blurred and 10% of sharp frames; the encoder accepts 80% of blurred and 98% of sharp genuine frames. (a) Compute the probability that the gate fires on a frame of the approach, the exact per-frame recall of the cascade, and the product shortcut. (b) Compute the encoder's recall on the frames the gate passes. (c) The camera runs at 30 frames per second, and over a whole day the gate passes 2% of all frames. At the 2014 timing of 0.33 core-seconds per frame, how many core-hours does the encoder use per day? (d) Which change raises the per-frame recall of the frames sent to the encoder: a gate that fires more often, or selecting sharp frames after the gate fires?

Solution

(a) \(\mathbb P(\text{fire})=0.5\times0.99+0.5\times0.10=0.545\). The exact recall is \(0.5\times0.99\times0.80+0.5\times0.10\times0.98=0.396+0.049=0.445\). The encoder's recall over all frames is \(0.5\times0.80+0.5\times0.98=0.89\), so the shortcut gives \(0.545\times0.89=0.485\), which overstates the exact value by 0.040. (b) \(0.445/0.545=0.817\), below 0.89, because 91% of the passed frames (\(0.495/0.545\)) are blurred. The hypothesis of Proposition 5(c) fails. (c) \(30\times86{,}400=2{,}592{,}000\) frames a day, of which \(0.02\times2{,}592{,}000=51{,}840\) reach the encoder, using \(51{,}840\times0.33=17{,}107\) core-seconds, or 4.75 core-hours, against 237.6 core-hours if every frame were sent. (d) A gate that fires more often adds frames of the same mixture and lowers nothing about blur; selecting sharp frames after the gate fires sends frames on which the encoder accepts 98% of genuine faces. The gate decides whether to look; the selector decides what to look at.

3. The simple gate at night

In the scene of the laboratory, sensor noise has standard deviation 3 grey levels by day, independent across pixels and frames, and \(c_{\mathrm{pix}}=25\), \(c_{\mathrm{frac}}=0.10\). At night the noise standard deviation rises to 20. (a) For an empty doorway, compute the expected changed fraction by day and by night. (b) Does the night-time change satisfy the hypothesis of Proposition 10? (c) Evaluate two repairs: raising \(c_{\mathrm{pix}}\), and averaging each frame over non-overlapping \(4\times4\) blocks of pixels before comparing.

Solution

(a) Let \(\Phi\) be the standard normal distribution function. The difference of two independent noise values has standard deviation \(\sqrt2\) times that of one. By day it is \(3\sqrt2=4.24\), and \(\mathbb P(\lvert\text{difference}\rvert>25)=2(1-\Phi(25/4.24))=2(1-\Phi(5.89))\approx4\times10^{-9}\), so the expected changed fraction is essentially 0. By night it is \(20\sqrt2=28.3\), and \(2(1-\Phi(25/28.3))=2(1-\Phi(0.884))=0.377\). The expected changed fraction of an empty doorway is 0.377, above \(c_{\mathrm{frac}}=0.10\), so the gate fires on almost every frame. (b) No. The distribution of \(\mathrm{Chg}_t\) on frames without a person has changed from near 0 to near 0.377, so the false-fire rate changes from near 0 to near 1. The gate that ignored sunglasses does not ignore the dark. (c) Raising \(c_{\mathrm{pix}}\) to six standard deviations of the night difference, about 170, removes the noise but also most of the person: in the scene a person differs from the wall by about 40 to 90 grey levels, so the gate would miss people at night. Averaging over \(4\times4\) blocks divides the noise standard deviation by \(\sqrt{16}=4\), giving a night difference standard deviation of 7.07 and \(2(1-\Phi(25/7.07))=2(1-\Phi(3.54))\approx4\times10^{-4}\), while a person, who covers many blocks with a contrast of 50 grey levels or more, still changes them. The block average is the better repair here; the lesson is that "robust because simple" held for one shift and failed for another.

4. Which kind of change?

For each change, say whether \(p(x)\), \(p(y\mid x)\) or both change for the model named, whether importance weighting of old labelled data can estimate the new risk, and what new data are needed. (a) Winter scarves at the door, for the face encoder deciding "same person?". (b) An employee leaves and the card is revoked, for the door deciding "authorised?". (c) Queries after a new album is released, for a search engine that ranks results for a query. (d) A new machine on an inspection line makes a new kind of scratch, for a defect classifier. (e) Traffic lights mounted differently in Texas, for a light-state classifier trained in California.

Solution

(a) Covariate shift: scarves change the images, not whether two images show the same person. Weighting helps only if the training data contained scarved faces; if not, the support condition of Proposition 9 fails and new scarved pairs are needed. (b) Concept shift for the "authorised?" decision: the same face now has label 0. No reweighting helps; the fix is not data at all but an update of the registration list, which is why the allow-list is stored outside the network. (c) Both. New queries change \(p(x)\); for queries about the new album, the relevant results did not exist before, so the correct ranking for such a query is new. New relevance judgments for the new queries are needed. (d) Covariate shift if a person would label a new scratch as a defect by the same standard as old scratches: the images change, the rule does not. The new scratch type lies outside the training support, so weighting cannot help, and labelled images of the new scratch are needed. If the factory also changes what counts as a defect, the conditional changes too. (e) Covariate shift: a red light means the same thing in Texas; the images differ. Unless some California images showed such installations, the new inputs are outside the training support, and the recording reports that new data were collected (speaker judgment, ≈3550–3580 s).

5. Weights for a shifted Gaussian

Let \(p_{\mathrm{train}}=\mathcal N(0,1)\) and \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}=\mathcal N(\mu,1)\) on the real line. (a) Derive \(\omega(x)\), \(\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{train}}}[\omega]\) and \(\mathbb E_{p_{\mathrm{train}}}[\omega^2]\). (b) Tabulate \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}\) for \(m=10{,}000\) at \(\mu=0.5,1,2,3\). (c) Now let \(p_{\mathrm{train}}\) be uniform on \([0,1]\) and \(p_{\mathrm{deploy}}\) uniform on \([0.5,1.5]\). What goes wrong, and what does Proposition 8 say?

Solution

(a) \(\omega(x)=\exp(-\tfrac12(x-\mu)^2+\tfrac12x^2)=\exp(\mu x-\tfrac12\mu^2)\). For a standard normal \(x\), completing the square gives \(\mathbb E[e^{\mu x}]=e^{\mu^2/2}\) and \(\mathbb E[e^{2\mu x}]=e^{2\mu^2}\). Hence \(\mathbb E[\omega]=e^{-\mu^2/2}e^{\mu^2/2}=1\), as for any importance weight, and \(\mathbb E[\omega^2]=e^{-\mu^2}e^{2\mu^2}=e^{\mu^2}\). (b) \(m_{\mathrm{eff}}=10{,}000\,e^{-\mu^2}\): 7,788 at \(\mu=0.5\), 3,679 at \(\mu=1\), 183 at \(\mu=2\), and 1.2 at \(\mu=3\). A shift of three standard deviations leaves 10,000 examples worth about one. (c) On \((1,1.5]\) the deployed density is 1 and the training density 0, so \(\omega\) is undefined there and Proposition 9 does not apply: half the deployed inputs lie where the training data say nothing. On \([0.5,1]\), \(\omega=1\). The total variation distance is \(\tfrac12(0.5\times1+0.5\times1)=0.5\), so by Proposition 8 the deployed error may differ from the test error by up to 0.5, and a system that fails on every input above 1 attains that difference. Only data from \((1,1.5]\) can settle it.

6. A monitoring plan for the door through the year

You will monitor \(n_{\mathrm{met}}=20\) daily metrics, including the re-authentication rate (normally 0.05 of 400 daily attempts), and the team accepts at most one false page per 30 days on average. (a) What per-metric false-alarm probability does this allow, and what band width in standard deviations does that imply under the normal approximation? (b) With that band on daily values, is a rise of the re-authentication rate to 0.08 caught on a given day with a probability different from the one computed in the chapter? (c) Design the rest of the plan: what to log, how to handle privacy, which failure no dashboard catches, and what should trigger retraining.

Solution

(a) By Proposition 11 the expected number of false alarms in 30 days is \(30\times20\,p_{\mathrm{fa}}\); setting it to at most 1 gives \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}\le1/600=0.00167\). Under the normal approximation a two-sided band with that tail probability has a half-width, in standard deviations, that satisfies \(2(1-\Phi(\text{half-width}))=1/600\); the half-width is 3.14 standard deviations, and the edges are \(0.05\pm3.14\times0.0109=[0.0157,0.0843]\), that is 6.3 and 33.7 of 400 attempts. (b) At 400 attempts the count is an integer, so the band of (a) alarms at the same counts as the chapter's band, fewer than 7 or more than 33, and its exact false-alarm probability is the chapter's \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.00227\), above the 0.00167 that (a) requires: the expected false pages in 30 days would be \(600\times0.00227=1.36\). The normal approximation, which gave 0.0027 for the chapter's band, is too coarse at these counts to choose between neighbouring bands, so the choice must use the binomial itself. Moving the upper edge out by one count, to alarm at fewer than 7 or more than 34, gives \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.00130\) (lower tail 0.00020, upper tail 0.00110), which meets the target with 0.78 expected false pages in 30 days. With that band the daily detection probability of a rise to 0.08 is \(\mathbb P(\text{count}\ge35)=0.315\), below the chapter's 0.382: the false-page budget is paid for in detection. A weekly band on 2,800 attempts has edges \(0.05\pm3.14\times0.00412=[0.0371,0.0629]\), or counts 104 to 176; its exact false-alarm probability is 0.00159, within the target, and a true rate of 0.08 exceeds 176 with probability 0.9997, at the cost of up to a week's delay. A sensible plan runs both: daily bands for abrupt failures, weekly bands for slow leaks. (c) Log, with user consent and a retention limit, the decision, the distance \(d\), the gate's changed fraction, latency, and whether a second attempt followed; store face images only for users who opted in, since the recording asks for transparent privacy practice (≈3706–3744 s). No dashboard of the recording catches false accepts, so schedule a periodic review of a random sample of accepted entries against the card holder's registration picture. Trigger retraining, and a new choice of \(c\) on current validation pairs, when a weekly band is breached on re-authentication or on the distribution of \(d\) for accepted users, and before each season whose changes (scarves, sunglasses) were listed in the brainstorm but are absent from the training data, because Proposition 9 cannot repair missing support.
