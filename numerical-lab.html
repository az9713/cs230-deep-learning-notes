Numerical laboratory: assumptions change the answer

These two original finite models connect the detector decisions in chapter 6 with the prototype portfolio in chapter 8. Change one assumption while keeping the other inputs fixed. Neither model estimates a deployed system's performance or a person's career prospects.

Rare events and positive predictive value

A detector can recognize most real events and still produce mostly false alarms. Let \(p_{+}\) be the event prevalence, \(p_{\mathrm{det}}\) the sensitivity (probability of a positive call given an event), and \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}\) the false-positive rate (probability of a positive call without an event). All three are dimensionless probabilities. For a randomly sampled window from a fixed population, true-positive mass is \(p_{+}p_{\mathrm{det}}\) and false-positive mass is \((1-p_{+})p_{\mathrm{fa}}\). Conditioning on a positive call gives

\[\Pr(\text{event}\mid\text{positive})=\frac{p_{+}p_{\mathrm{det}}}{p_{+}p_{\mathrm{det}}+(1-p_{+})p_{\mathrm{fa}}}.\]

This is the fraction of positive calls that correspond to real events, often called precision. The expression is undefined when the denominator is zero, because the conditioning event never occurs. It presumes the stipulated conditional rates apply in this population; changing the sampling population can change them. Correlation between nearby windows affects uncertainty in estimated rates even when the population identity holds.

For \(p_{+}=0.01\), \(p_{\mathrm{det}}=0.9\) and \(p_{\mathrm{fa}}=0.05\), an illustrative population of 10,000 windows has expected counts 90 true-positive calls and 495 false-positive calls. Precision is 90/585 ≈ 15.38%. These are expected counts under the model, not observations.

Chapter 6: project strategy and detector assumptions develops the training and decision context. A threshold change may alter both sensitivity and the false-positive rate; this lab holds them as separately stipulated inputs to expose the base-rate mechanism.

Repeated attempts with a shared cause

Let \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\) be a nonnegative integer count of attempts and \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\) each attempt's marginal success probability against one fixed criterion. With mutually independent outcomes, the probability of at least one success is \(1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\). The proof multiplies the failure probabilities and takes the complement. Independence is the consequential assumption: twenty perfectly correlated outcomes with marginal \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=0.1\) give only a 10% chance of any success, whereas independence gives about 87.84%.

To interpolate between those cases, introduce \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}\in[0,1]\). With probability \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}\), all attempts share a single Bernoulli outcome with success probability \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\). Otherwise the outcomes are mutually independent with that same probability. Each individual attempt still has marginal \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}\), because \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{shared}})p_{\mathrm{succ}}=p_{\mathrm{succ}}\). For \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\ge1\) the law of total probability gives

\[\Pr(\text{at least one success})=(1-p_{\mathrm{shared}})\bigl[1-(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})^{n_{\mathrm{try}}}\bigr]+p_{\mathrm{shared}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}.\]

When \(n_{\mathrm{try}}=0\) the probability is zero in both branches. Here \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}\) is a mixture weight; it is not an empirical estimate of correlation. If \(n_{\mathrm{try}}\ge2\) and \(0<p_{\mathrm{succ}}<1\), pairwise correlation in this particular model equals \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}\): the joint success probability is \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}+(1-p_{\mathrm{shared}})p_{\mathrm{succ}}^2\), so covariance is \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})\), and dividing by the Bernoulli variance \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}(1-p_{\mathrm{succ}})\) gives \(p_{\mathrm{shared}}\). At \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=0\) or \(p_{\mathrm{succ}}=1\) that correlation is undefined, although the success probability remains well defined. Chapter 1's shared-premise failure model is a different construction: here the shared branch may succeed or fail.

Chapter 8: career advice and finite portfolio examples gives the independent calculation and the dependent counterexample. The present mixture is an original extension. In real projects, success probabilities, selection, feedback and shared constraints may all change between attempts; the fixed-marginal model does not predict those changes.
