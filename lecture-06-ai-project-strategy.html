Deciding what to try next when a learned system fails

A team trains a network, measures it, and finds that it does not work. The next week of work depends on one decision: what to change. This chapter asks how that decision can rest on computation instead of habit. When the training set is skewed, which of the standard remedies changes what, and what does each one do to the decision threshold? When a model does well on its training data and badly elsewhere, how does a team tell overfitting from a mismatch between the data it trained on and the data it will meet? When a system is a pipeline of stages, how many failing examples must be read before the stage to improve is known? And how does the length of one training run limit how many such decisions a team can make in a year?

The recording answers these questions with advice drawn from the lecturer's projects. Most of that advice has no formal content and is reported here as advice. Four parts of it do have a model that can be derived and checked: the arithmetic of a skewed training set and of loss weighting; the synthesis of audio by adding waveforms; the decomposition of an error gap with a held-out part of the training distribution; and the attribution of failures to pipeline stages, with its sampling error, together with the arithmetic of iteration time. The chapter develops those four models on two running cases, a wake-word detector for a desk lamp and a pipeline that writes research reports. It has six exercises, all set on the four models; none is set on the advice that has no model.

The task as a sequence of binary decisions

A microphone records air pressure as a sequence of numbers. Cut the recording into overlapping windows of fixed length, three seconds in the recording's example. A window is the input \(x\in\mathbb R^{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\), where \(n_{\mathrm{samp}}\) is the number of pressure samples in three seconds. Its label is \(y=1\) if the phrase "Robert, turn on" ended within the last short interval before the window's end, and \(y=0\) otherwise; the length of that interval is a design choice, and the remedies section changes it. A network \(f_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\) computes a logit \(u=f_\theta(x)\) and a probability \(\hat y=\sigma(u)=1/(1+e^{-u})\). The lamp fires on a window when \(\hat y\ge\tfrac12\), that is, when \(u\ge0\), unless a section states another threshold. Training minimises the binary cross-entropy \(\mathcal L(\hat y,y)=-y\log\hat y-(1-y)\log(1-\hat y)\), averaged over the \(m\) training windows; chapter 2 derives its gradient at the logit, \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u=\hat y-y\).

Three quantities describe the detector on a set of windows. The accuracy \(\mathrm{Acc}\) is the fraction of windows on which the decision agrees with the label. The recall is the fraction of positive windows on which the lamp fires. The precision is the fraction of firings that fall on positive windows. For the lamp, recall measures how often a spoken command works, and one minus precision measures how often a firing is a false activation. The prevalence \(p_{+}\) is the fraction of windows labelled 1. Accuracy mixes the two kinds of error in proportion to their frequency; when \(p_{+}\) is small, accuracy is dominated by the negative windows. The next sections show this failing in practice.

Three sets of data

A project divides its labelled data into up to three sets with different jobs. The training set is the data on which \(\theta\) is fitted. The development set, or dev set, is used to choose among configurations: architectures, hyperparameters, stopping points and data recipes. The test set is used once, after every choice has been made, to estimate the performance of the chosen system; chapter 1 shows that it gives an unbiased estimate when nothing was chosen with it, and the clips section below computes what it costs to choose with the dev set and report the dev score. The error rates of one classifier on these sets are written \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}}\), \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{dev}}\) and \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{test}}\), each the fraction of examples misclassified. Chapter 3 defines the human-level error \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\) and proves, for a human who labels each input from the input alone, that it is at least the Bayes error \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\), the lowest error any rule can reach (its Proposition 7).

The numerical running case

The recording's network is not specified, so this chapter needs a stand-in whose optimal behaviour is known in closed form. Suppose the network's last hidden layer has reduced each window to two numbers \(h\in\mathbb R^2\), the activation of that layer in the notation of chapter 2, and that given the label they are Gaussian: \(h\mid y=1\sim\mathcal N(\mu,I)\) and \(h\mid y=0\sim\mathcal N(-\mu,I)\), with \(\mu=(0.5,0.5)\) and \(I\) the identity. The prevalence is \(p_{+}=1/30\), the value the recording's data will produce. By Bayes' rule the log-odds of a positive are

\[\log\frac{p(y=1\mid h)}{p(y=0\mid h)}=\log\frac{\mathcal N(h;\mu,I)}{\mathcal N(h;-\mu,I)}+\log\frac{p_{+}}{1-p_{+}}=2\mu^\top h-\log29,\]

because the quadratic terms \(-\tfrac12\lVert h\mp\mu\rVert_2^2\) differ by \(2\mu^\top h\) and \(p_{+}/(1-p_{+})=1/29\). The score \(2\mu^\top h\) is Gaussian with mean \(2\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2=1\) and variance \(4\lVert\mu\rVert_2^2=2\) for positive windows, and mean \(-1\) with the same variance for negative ones. The features are weak on purpose: a single window carries about as much evidence as a network that has not yet learned the phrase well, which is the situation the recording describes. A logistic model \(u=w^\top h+b\), with weight vector \(w\in\mathbb R^2\) and bias \(b\), contains the exact log-odds (at \(w=2\mu\), \(b=-\log29\)), so every optimum computed below is the optimum over all functions, not an accident of a small model class.

What tuning to the dev set does to the dev score

Let each dev clip be scored as handled correctly or not, and let \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}}\) be the fraction of the \(25\) dev clips handled correctly. Suppose a fixed system handles each dev clip correctly with probability \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}\), independently across clips. Each clip's outcome is then a 0–1 variable with mean \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}\) and variance \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}(1-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}})\); \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}}\) is the average of 25 such independent variables, so its mean is \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}\) and, because variances of independent variables add, its variance is \(25\,\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}(1-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}})/25^2\). Its standard deviation is therefore \(\mathrm{sd}(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}})=\sqrt{\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}(1-\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}})/25}\); at \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}=0.9\) that is \(\sqrt{0.09/25}=0.06\). A dev score of 0.90 is therefore consistent with true clip accuracies from about 0.78 to 1.00 at two standard deviations. Now suppose the team compares \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\) configurations on the same dev set and keeps the one with the highest score.

Proposition 1 (optimism of the best dev score). Let \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\) configurations have the same true accuracy \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}\), and let their dev scores be \(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{true}}+\mathrm{sd}(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}})\,\chi_j\), \(j=1,\dots,n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\), where each \(\chi_j\) is a standard Gaussian variable; the \(\chi_j\) may be dependent. Then the expected dev score of the selected configuration exceeds its true accuracy by

\[\mathbb E\Bigl[\max_j\,\mathrm{sd}(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}})\,\chi_j\Bigr]=\mathrm{sd}(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}})\;\mathbb E\bigl[\max_j\chi_j\bigr]\le\mathrm{sd}(\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{dev}})\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}.\]

Proof. The equality holds because the standard deviation is a positive constant. For the bound, write \(\chi^{\max}=\max_j\chi_j\) and let \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\ge2\), so that \(\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\) is positive. The exponential function is convex, so by Jensen's inequality \(\exp\bigl(\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\,\mathbb E[\chi^{\max}]\bigr)\le\mathbb E\bigl[\exp\bigl(\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\,\chi^{\max}\bigr)\bigr]\). The maximum of positive numbers is at most their sum, and a standard Gaussian \(\chi\) satisfies \(\mathbb E[e^{\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\,\chi}]=e^{\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}=n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\) (its moment-generating function at a point is the exponential of half the square of that point). Hence the right-hand side is at most \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\cdot n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\). Taking logarithms, \(\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\,\mathbb E[\chi^{\max}]\le2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}\); divide by \(\sqrt{2\log n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}\). No step used independence. \(\blacksquare\)

With \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}=10\) and a standard deviation of 0.06 the bound is \(0.06\sqrt{2\log10}=0.129\). If the ten scores were independent Gaussians, the expected maximum of ten standard Gaussians, 1.539, would give an optimism of 0.092. The dev score is in fact a binomial count out of 25, capped at 1, and the code below simulates it exactly: the best of ten equally good configurations scores on average 0.079 above their common true accuracy of 0.9 (engineering check). Configurations scored on one dev set can make correlated errors. As an additional modelling assumption, suppose the vector of score noises is jointly Gaussian and every distinct pair has correlation \(\mathrm{cor}\in[0,1)\). It then has the same joint distribution as \(\chi_j=\sqrt{\mathrm{cor}}\,\chi_0+\sqrt{1-\mathrm{cor}}\,\chi_j^{\mathrm{ind}}\), with independent standard Gaussians \(\chi_0,\chi_1^{\mathrm{ind}},\dots,\chi_{n_{\mathrm{cfg}}}^{\mathrm{ind}}\): this construction has the required mean vector and covariance matrix, which determine a joint Gaussian distribution. Its common term has mean zero, so \(\mathbb E[\max_j\chi_j]=\sqrt{1-\mathrm{cor}}\,\mathbb E[\max_j\chi_j^{\mathrm{ind}}]\). In this jointly Gaussian model, correlation scales the independent optimism by \(\sqrt{1-\mathrm{cor}}\). Equal correlations alone do not imply the identity. Proposition 1's upper bound still holds for every dependence structure allowed by its marginal assumptions. Every configuration in the proposition is equally good, so the excess is selection, not improvement.

This is the cost of a missing test set. After the team has chosen among configurations on the dev set, the dev score of the winner is an optimistic estimate of how the lamp will do on new clips, and the optimism grows with the number of configurations tried and shrinks with the size of the dev set. Cawley and Talbot (2010) make the same point for model selection: a selection criterion with "a non-negligible variance introduces the potential for over-fitting in model selection", and some common evaluation practices are therefore subject to "a form of selection bias" (p. 2079). They report the effects of this over-fitting to be often of comparable magnitude to differences in performance between learning algorithms (p. 2079; empirical result for their benchmarks), and they state that the finding applies to any selection that optimises a criterion evaluated on a finite sample (p. 2079). The recording's practice is sound when the product is judged by users and not by a reported number. What it gives up is a number that can be quoted as the deployed performance.

The same book that states the recording's framework, Ng's Machine Learning Yearning (draft, 2018), recommends choosing dev and test sets "from a distribution that reflects what data you expect to get in the future and want to do well on", which "may not be the same as your training data's distribution", and choosing dev and test sets from the same distribution if possible (p. 27). The book is the lecturer's own; its recommendations carry the same grade as the recording's, speaker judgment, and are cited here for their precise wording.

Windows from clips

The whiteboard draws a waveform with the phrase near its end, a three-second window, and a row of labels over the windows: zeros, then ones just after the end of the phrase, marked as taking about one second to say (≈1606–1792 s). Each three-second window whose end falls just after the phrase is labelled 1, and every other window 0. Taking 30 windows from each of the 100 training clips gives \(m=3{,}000\) labelled windows (≈1754–1792 s).

The recording's description of the geometry is loose. It speaks of three-second windows cut from a clip of about ten seconds, 30 windows per clip, and a positive label when the phrase ended within the last 100 ms; the remedies section shows that these figures cannot all hold with one positive window per clip unless the windows are spaced 100 ms apart, and then 30 windows cover only about three seconds of window ends. The chapter keeps the figures the recording uses in arithmetic, 100 clips, 30 windows each and one positive window per clip, and treats the spacing as a free parameter where it matters.

With one positive window per clip, the training set has \(m_{+}=100\) positive and \(m_{-}=2{,}900\) negative windows, so

\[p_{+}=\frac{100}{3{,}000}=\frac1{30}=0.0333,\qquad m_{+}:m_{-}=1:29.\]

The recording calls the ratio "one to thirty" (≈2026–2051 s); the exact ratio of positives to negatives is 1:29, and 1:30 is the ratio of positives to all windows.

One rule follows from the construction, although the recording does not state it. The 25 dev clips must be separated from the 100 training clips before windows are cut. Windows of one clip overlap and share most of their audio, and the same speaker and room appear in all of them. A split made at the level of windows would put near-copies of training windows into the dev set, and the dev score would then measure memory of those recordings, not performance on a new speaker. The recording's split, by clips, avoids this (this book's reasoning).

Ninety-seven percent accurate and never firing 30:26

Provenance. Lecture: the trained system was 97 % accurate on the dev set with no detections, the same as a program that prints 0; the audience's diagnosis of an unbalanced training set (≈1826–1974 s). Research extension: none. Original construction: the application of chapter 2's constant-predictor result, the best constant probability, and the running-case operating point.

The team trained on the 3,000 windows and evaluated on the dev set. The whiteboard records "97% accurate" beside a one-line program that prints "0" (≈1826–1888 s): the network had learned to output 0 on every window, so it was accurate and useless. The recording reports this as what happened on the team's project (speaker judgment, ≈1792–1859 s).

The number is what the construction of the data forces. Chapter 2, Proposition 4, proves two facts about a set with prevalence \(p_{+}\): the predictor that outputs 0 everywhere has accuracy \(1-p_{+}\), and among predictors that output one constant probability the binary cross-entropy is smallest at the constant \(p_{+}\). Applied to the dev set, which by construction has the same prevalence (25 clips, 30 windows each, one positive per clip: \(725/750\)), or to the 3,000 training windows, the first fact gives an accuracy of \(1-1/30=96.67\,\%\), which the recording rounds to 97 %. The second gives a best constant probability of \(1/30=0.033\), with a loss of \(-\tfrac1{30}\log\tfrac1{30}-\tfrac{29}{30}\log\tfrac{29}{30}=0.146\) nats (engineering check by shown arithmetic). A network that learns nothing about the audio beyond the base rate therefore outputs about 0.033 on every window, which is below the threshold \(\tfrac12\): it never fires. Its accuracy is 96.67 % and its recall is 0.

The quantity that exposed the failure is recall, not accuracy. The best achievable accuracy on a skewed set is at least the accuracy of the constant majority prediction, \(1-p_{+}\), so a claim of progress in accuracy must beat 96.67 %, and even a model that beats it can still miss most commands. The recording's summary is that a large network had been trained to do what one line of code does (≈1826–1888 s).

The running case shows the same thing with a model that is optimal. Its exact log-odds \(2\mu^\top h-\log29\) exceed zero only when the score \(2\mu^\top h\) exceeds \(\log29=3.37\). A positive window's score is Gaussian with mean 1 and standard deviation \(\sqrt2\), so the Bayes-optimal detector at threshold \(\tfrac12\) fires on a positive window with probability \(1-\Phi\bigl((3.37-1)/\sqrt2\bigr)=1-\Phi(1.674)=0.047\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function; it fires on a negative window with probability \(1-\Phi(3.088)=0.001\). Its accuracy is 0.967 and its recall 0.047 (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The detector is calibrated and accuracy-optimal, and it misses 95 % of the commands. Nothing is wrong with the model. The threshold \(\tfrac12\) on a calibrated probability is the rule that maximises accuracy, and accuracy is the wrong objective for a lamp.

Four remedies for a skewed training set 32:54

Provenance. Lecture: duplicate the positive windows, or give them more weight in the cost; a rule of thumb that networks tolerate imbalance up to about 1:10; penalise false negatives; remove negative windows, at the price of less diverse negatives; the team's choice, which widened the positive label from about 100 ms to half a second or a second (≈1974–2270 s). Research extension: Elkan's rebalancing theorem; King and Zeng's prior correction; Buda, Maki and Mazurowski on imbalance in convolutional networks. Original construction: Propositions 2–4, the running-case numbers and Figure 1.

The audience proposes more positive examples, and the recording lists four ways to get the effect (≈1974–2155 s): duplicate the positive windows; give them more weight in the cost function, which is the same as penalising false negatives more; delete some negative windows; and, what the team actually did, widen the positive label. It adds a rule of thumb: many networks train well at a ratio of 1:2, begin to need attention near 1:10, and may or may not cope at 1:30 (speaker judgment, ≈2009–2083 s). The four remedies are four different operations on the objective, and they are not interchangeable.

Duplication and weighting

Let \(\omega_{+}\) be a weight on the loss of each positive window, with weight 1 on each negative, and write \(\omega^{(i)}=\omega_{+}\) if \(y^{(i)}=1\) and \(\omega^{(i)}=1\) otherwise. The weighted cost is

\[J_{\omega}(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\omega^{(i)}\,\mathcal L\bigl(\hat y^{(i)},y^{(i)}\bigr).\]

Duplication by an integer factor \(\omega_{+}\) replaces each positive window by \(\omega_{+}\) identical copies, giving a training set of \(m_{\mathrm{dup}}=m+(\omega_{+}-1)m_{+}\) windows and the ordinary average cost \(J_{\mathrm{dup}}\) on it.

Proposition 2 (duplication and weighting). (a) \(J_{\mathrm{dup}}(\theta)=(m/m_{\mathrm{dup}})\,J_\omega(\theta)\) for every \(\theta\); the two costs have the same minimisers. (b) Let a stochastic gradient step use one example drawn uniformly: from the original set with its weight for \(J_\omega\), or from the duplicated set, rescaled by \(m_{\mathrm{dup}}/m\), for \(J_{\mathrm{dup}}\). Both estimates have expectation \(\nabla J_\omega(\theta)\). (c) Their second moments differ. With \(\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\hat y^{(i)},y^{(i)})\), the weighted estimate has second moment \(\frac1m\sum_i(\omega^{(i)})^2\lVert\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\rVert_2^2\) and the duplicated estimate \(\frac{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}{m^2}\sum_i\omega^{(i)}\lVert\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\rVert_2^2\).

Proof. Write \(\mathcal L^{(i)}\) for the loss of example \(i\). (a) In the duplicated set example \(i\) appears \(\omega^{(i)}\) times, so \(J_{\mathrm{dup}}=\frac1{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}\sum_i\omega^{(i)}\mathcal L^{(i)}\), which is \(m/m_{\mathrm{dup}}\) times \(J_\omega\). (b) The weighted estimate is \(\omega^{(i)}\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\) with \(i\) drawn uniformly from \(\{1,\dots,m\}\); its expectation is \(\frac1m\sum_i\omega^{(i)}\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}=\nabla J_\omega\). In the duplicated set example \(i\) is drawn with probability \(\omega^{(i)}/m_{\mathrm{dup}}\), so the rescaled estimate \(\frac{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}{m}\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\) has expectation \(\frac{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}{m}\sum_i\frac{\omega^{(i)}}{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}=\nabla J_\omega\). (c) The same two distributions give \(\frac1m\sum_i\lVert\omega^{(i)}\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\rVert_2^2\) and \(\sum_i\frac{\omega^{(i)}}{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}\bigl(\frac{m_{\mathrm{dup}}}{m}\bigr)^2\lVert\nabla\mathcal L^{(i)}\rVert_2^2\), which simplify to the stated forms. \(\blacksquare\)

The recording's "mathematically, this is equivalent" (≈1999–2010 s) is part (a): the two remedies define the same objective up to a constant, which only rescales the step size. They are not the same training procedure. With the lamp's numbers, \(\omega_{+}=29\), \(m=3{,}000\) and \(m_{\mathrm{dup}}=3{,}000+28\times100=5{,}800\), a positive example contributes \(29^2/3{,}000=0.280\) times its squared gradient norm to the weighted estimate's second moment and \(5{,}800\times29/3{,}000^2=0.019\) times to the duplicated one, fifteen times less; a negative example contributes \(0.00033\) and \(0.00064\) times respectively (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Weighting draws each positive with probability \(1/3{,}000\) per step and multiplies its gradient by 29 when it does; duplication draws each positive with probability \(29/5{,}800\), fifteen times as often, and multiplies its gradient by \(m_{\mathrm{dup}}/m=1.93\). The weighted estimate is therefore noisier on the positives. A minibatch of \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\) independent draws leaves the mean \(\nabla J_\omega\) unchanged and divides the variance, the second moment minus \(\lVert\nabla J_\omega\rVert_2^2\), by \(n_{\mathrm{batch}}\); because both estimates have the same mean, comparing their variances is the same as comparing their second moments.

What weighting does to the optimal logit

The objective changed, so its optimum changed too. The question is how.

Proposition 3 (the weighted optimum). Fix an input \(x\), let \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}=p(y=1\mid x)\in(0,1)\) be the conditional probability of a positive at that input, and weight the loss of positives by \(\omega_{+}\gt0\). Among all values of the logit \(u\), the expected weighted loss \(\omega_{+}\,p_{\mathrm{cond}}\log(1+e^{-u})+(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\log(1+e^{u})\) is minimised at

\[u^*(x)=\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{cond}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}}+\log\omega_{+}.\]

Consequently the weighted model's probability \(\sigma(u^*)=\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}/(\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}+1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\) is not the conditional probability; the conditional probability is recovered as \(\sigma(u^*-\log\omega_{+})\); and firing when \(u^*\ge0\) is the same as firing when \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge c\) with threshold \(c=1/(1+\omega_{+})\).

Proof. The binary cross-entropy of a logit \(u\) is \(\log(1+e^{-u})\) for a positive and \(\log(1+e^{u})\) for a negative. The derivative of the expected weighted loss with respect to \(u\) is \(-\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}(1-\sigma(u))+(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\,\sigma(u)\). It is increasing in \(u\), since \(\sigma\) is increasing, so the loss is convex and its minimum is at the zero of the derivative: \(\sigma(u)/(1-\sigma(u))=\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}/(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\). Since \(\sigma(u)/(1-\sigma(u))=e^{u}\), taking logarithms gives \(u^*\). Then \(u^*\ge0\) exactly when \(\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}\), that is, \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge1/(1+\omega_{+})\). \(\blacksquare\)

Weighting does not teach the network anything about the audio that the unweighted objective did not reward. It adds the constant \(\log\omega_{+}\) to the optimal logit at every input. With \(\omega_{+}=29\), the shift is \(\log29=3.37\), and the rule "fire when the weighted model says at least \(\tfrac12\)" becomes "fire when the conditional probability is at least \(1/30\)". Choosing \(\omega_{+}\) is choosing a threshold, and a threshold is a statement about costs. If a missed command costs \(\omega_{+}\) times as much as a false activation, the decision that minimises expected cost fires when \(\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}\), which is the same condition. The weight 29 thus says that a missed command is 29 times as bad as a false activation, a product decision the recording does not discuss.

Two primary sources state the same mechanism from different directions. Elkan (2001) proves that to make a learner that applies a probability threshold \(c_{\mathrm{lrn}}\) behave as if it applied a target threshold \(c_{\mathrm{tgt}}\), the number of negative training examples should be multiplied by \(\frac{c_{\mathrm{tgt}}}{1-c_{\mathrm{tgt}}}\cdot\frac{1-c_{\mathrm{lrn}}}{c_{\mathrm{lrn}}}\) (Theorem 1, p. 3; the paper writes \(p_0\) and \(p^*\) for the two thresholds). Weighting positives by \(\omega_{+}\) has the same effect as multiplying the negatives by \(1/\omega_{+}\), so with \(c_{\mathrm{lrn}}=\tfrac12\) the theorem gives \(c_{\mathrm{tgt}}/(1-c_{\mathrm{tgt}})=1/\omega_{+}\), that is, \(c_{\mathrm{tgt}}=1/(1+\omega_{+})\), as Proposition 3 does. Elkan also defines oversampling as duplicating examples and undersampling as deleting them (p. 3), and recommends learning a classifier from the training set as given and then computing the decisions explicitly from its probability estimates (p. 1). King and Zeng (2001) give the correction in logistic regression when the fraction of ones in the sample, \(p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}}\), differs from the fraction in the population, \(p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}}\) (the paper writes \(\bar y\) and \(\tau\)): the slope estimates are consistent and the intercept is corrected by subtracting \(\log\bigl[\frac{1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}}}{p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}}}\cdot\frac{p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}}}{1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}}}\bigr]\) (p. 144, eq. 7; the same paper gives the alternative of weighting ones by \(p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}}/p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}}\) and zeros by \((1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}})/(1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}})\), p. 145, eqs. 8–9). Duplicating positives by \(\omega_{+}\) makes the sample odds \(p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}}/(1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{smp}})\) equal to \(\omega_{+}\) times the population odds \(p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}}/(1-p_{+}^{\mathrm{pop}})\), so the correction is \(-\log\omega_{+}\), the same subtraction.

The running case makes the trade explicit (Figure 1). At \(\omega_{+}=1\) the optimal detector fires when \(2\mu^\top h\ge3.37\): recall 0.047, a false-activation probability of 0.001 per negative window, precision 0.617, accuracy 0.967. At \(\omega_{+}=29\) it fires when \(2\mu^\top h\ge0\): recall 0.760, false activation 0.240 per negative window, precision 0.099, accuracy 0.760 (engineering check: the code below evaluates the two Gaussian scores with \(\Phi\)). Weighting raised recall sixteenfold and lowered accuracy below the constant predictor's 96.67 %; nine of every ten firings are now false. The code also fits logistic regression to 3,000 simulated windows with and without the weight and finds the same pattern on 600,000 fresh windows, recall 0.054 against 0.733 and precision 0.607 against 0.103, with an intercept shift of 3.38 against the population value \(\log29=3.367\) (engineering check).

Figure 1 shows why the weight cannot do more. The three points lie on one curve, the recall and false-activation rate of the rule "fire when \(2\mu^\top h\) exceeds a threshold" as the threshold varies. That curve is fixed by the features: by how far apart the two Gaussian scores lie. A weight chooses a point on the curve; it does not move the curve. Which operating point the lamp should use depends on how often a false activation annoys a user compared with how often a missed command does, and on how many negative windows the lamp hears per hour. Neither weight settles it by itself; with the features this weak, no threshold gives high recall and high precision at once, and the remedy for that is better features, not a different weight.

Buda, Maki and Mazurowski (2018) compared these remedies in convolutional networks on MNIST, CIFAR-10 and ImageNet with imbalance created artificially. They found oversampling dominant "in almost all analyzed scenarios", found that oversampling "does not cause overfitting of CNNs", and recommend thresholding "to compensate for prior class probabilities when overall number of properly classified cases is of interest" (p. 1; empirical result for those image benchmarks, with the area under the ROC curve as the main metric). The last recommendation is Proposition 3 read backwards. Their study does not test the recording's 1:10 rule of thumb, which remains speaker judgment.

Deleting negatives

Keep each negative window independently with probability \(1/\omega_{+}\). The expected cost over the random deletion is

\[\mathbb E\Bigl[\sum_{i\ \text{kept}}\mathcal L^{(i)}\Bigr]=\sum_{y^{(i)}=1}\mathcal L^{(i)}+\frac1{\omega_{+}}\sum_{y^{(i)}=0}\mathcal L^{(i)}=\frac1{\omega_{+}}\sum_i\omega^{(i)}\mathcal L^{(i)},\]

which is proportional to the weighted cost, so in expectation undersampling has the optimum of Proposition 3. The realised training set is different: with \(\omega_{+}=29\) about 100 of the 2,900 negative windows survive. The recording's objection is exactly this, that the network then has less variety of negative examples to learn from, and it judges the loss small (speaker judgment, ≈2083–2126 s). For a detector whose deployed input is mostly negative, the variety of the negatives is what determines the false-activation rate, so the loss can matter more than the recording suggests; the chapter records that as this book's reasoning, not a measurement.

Widening the positive label

The team's own remedy changed the labels (≈2155–2235 s). Instead of a positive label only for the window that ends within about 100 ms after the phrase, every window ending within half a second or a second after it is labelled 1; the whiteboard shows several ones after the end of the phrase, drawn as a run of red rectangles. The recording presents this as close to duplication but better, because the extra positives are different windows of audio, and expects the difference to be small (speaker judgment, ≈2210–2270 s).

How many positives the widening creates depends on how far apart consecutive windows start, which the recording does not state. Let \(t_{\mathrm{hop}}\) be the time between the ends of consecutive windows and \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\) the tolerance: a window is positive when its end falls in the interval \((t_{\mathrm{end}},t_{\mathrm{end}}+t_{\mathrm{tol}}]\), where \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\) is the moment the phrase ends. Write \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) for the number of positive windows in one clip, as in chapter 2.

Proposition 4 (positive windows per clip). Assume the phrase ends far enough from both ends of the clip that the grid of window ends extends at least \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\) past \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\). Then \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) equals \(\lfloor t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\rfloor\) or \(\lceil t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\rceil\), depending on where the phrase ends relative to the grid of window ends. If that position is uniformly distributed over one hop, the expected value of \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) is exactly \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\).

Proof. Let \(t_{\mathrm{off}}\in[0,t_{\mathrm{hop}})\) be the time from the last window end at or before \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\) to \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\). The window ends after \(t_{\mathrm{end}}\) lie at \(t_{\mathrm{end}}-t_{\mathrm{off}}+k\,t_{\mathrm{hop}}\) for \(k=1,2,\dots\), and such a window is positive when \(k\,t_{\mathrm{hop}}-t_{\mathrm{off}}\le t_{\mathrm{tol}}\), that is, \(k\le t_{\mathrm{off}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}+t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\). So \(n_{\mathrm{on}}=\lfloor t_{\mathrm{off}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}+t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\rfloor\). Split \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\) into its integer part \(\lfloor t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\rfloor\) and its fractional part \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}-\lfloor t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\rfloor\in[0,1)\). Because \(t_{\mathrm{off}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\lt1\), \(n_{\mathrm{on}}\) is the integer part plus 1 when \(t_{\mathrm{off}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\) plus the fractional part reaches 1, and the integer part otherwise; this gives the floor or the ceiling. If \(t_{\mathrm{off}}\) is uniform on \([0,t_{\mathrm{hop}})\), the extra 1 occurs with probability equal to the fractional part, so the expected count is the integer part plus the fractional part, \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The proposition resolves the geometry. With a 100 ms tolerance, exactly one positive window per clip requires \(t_{\mathrm{hop}}=0.1\) s. If the 30 windows were instead spread over a ten-second clip, the hop would be \((10-3)/29=0.24\) s, and a 100 ms tolerance would produce one positive window in a fraction \(0.1/0.24=0.41\) of the clips and none in the rest. Taking the recording's one positive per clip as the fixed point, widening the tolerance to 0.5 s gives five positives per clip and to 1 s ten. Keeping 30 windows per clip, the 3,000 windows then contain 500 or 1,000 positives: \(p_{+}=1/6\) (ratio 1:5) or \(1/3\) (ratio 1:2) (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Either is inside the range in which the recording's rule of thumb says networks train without special treatment.

Widening is not a form of duplication. Proposition 2 changes how often each labelled example counts; widening changes the labels, and so the function being learned. The label now means "the phrase ended within the last \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\) seconds", and a detector that learns it fires on several consecutive windows after each command. The deployed lamp must therefore act once per run of firings, as chapter 2 notes for its own run of ones, and must not act again within \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\). The positives are also no longer identical copies: they are windows shifted by up to \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\), which is the variety the recording prefers to duplication. The widening also lowers the precision of the timing, by up to \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}\), which is harmless for a lamp and would matter for a system that must mark where a word ends.

After the remedy the team trains again. The whiteboard records "95% on train" and "50% on dev", and the recording says the same (≈2275–2290 s). The recording does not say which quantity the 50 % measures. The audience offers two explanations (≈2290–2315 s): the network has overfitted the training set, or the training and dev sets come from different distributions. The recording rules out the second for this project. The 125 clips were collected in one way and assigned to training or dev at random, so the two sets are samples from one distribution (≈2315–2343 s). The diagnosis is overfitting, and the recording's first remedy is regularisation, followed, for speech in its experience, by more data (speaker judgment, ≈2429–2481 s).

The recording then states the general case. Earlier machine learning assumed that training, dev and test data share one distribution, an assumption the lecturer attributes to its convenience for proving theorems (speaker judgment about motive, ≈2315–2372 s). In practice the training distribution often differs from the one the system meets, for example when training data are synthesised, and then the dev and test sets should contain real data, because they must measure what users will experience (≈2372–2429 s). Two requirements must be kept apart. Dev and test sets from the same distribution is a requirement: without it, a system tuned on the dev set can fail on the test set for reasons the dev set cannot show. Training and test sets from the same distribution is an assumption of the older theory; practice can drop it, at the cost of a new kind of error that must then be measured.

A held-out part of the training distribution

When the training and dev distributions differ, a single gap \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{dev}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}}\) mixes two causes: failure to generalise to new data of the kind trained on, and failure to transfer to data of another kind. Machine Learning Yearning separates them with a fourth set. The training-dev set is drawn from the same distribution as the training set and is not trained on; it "only needs to be large enough to evaluate and track the progress of our learning algorithm" (pp. 77–78). Let \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tdev}}\) be the error on it. The book names three problems (p. 77): high avoidable bias (poor performance on the training set itself), high variance (poor generalisation to unseen data from the training distribution), and data mismatch (good generalisation within the training distribution but not to the dev distribution). The recording names the same three at ≈3074–3101 s, without the training-dev set.

Proposition 5 (the error-gap identity). For any four numbers,

\[\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{dev}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}=\underbrace{(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}})}_{\text{avoidable bias}}+\underbrace{(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tdev}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{train}})}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{dev}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{tdev}})}_{\text{data mismatch}}.\]

Proof. The right-hand side telescopes. \(\blacksquare\)

The identity is trivial; the content is in the hypotheses that let each term carry its name. The variance term measures generalisation only if the training-dev set is drawn from the training distribution and never used to fit or choose anything. To interpret the mismatch term as a population-risk difference, fix the same predictor independently of both evaluation sets, draw the dev set from the deployment distribution and the training-dev set from the training distribution, and use the same loss. Conditional on that predictor, the expected difference measures distribution change; a finite observed difference also includes sampling variation. A dev set reused to choose the predictor needs a fresh held-out evaluation for this interpretation. The bias term uses the human-level error as a stand-in for the lowest achievable error; for a human who labels from the input alone, chapter 3 proves \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}\ge\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\), so this term understates the room for improvement by \(\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{hum}}-\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}\). For a predictor fixed independently of an evaluation sample, an error rate on independent examples has binomial standard deviation. That guarantee does not apply to the fitted training error, to a dev set reused to choose the predictor, or to correlated windows; use fresh held-out clips and account for shared speakers and sessions before assigning uncertainty to a gap. A gap smaller than its sampling uncertainty is not reliable evidence of a difference. Terms can be negative: a dev set that is easier than the training distribution gives a negative mismatch term.

Machine Learning Yearning works three cases with human error near 0 (p. 79): training, training-dev and dev errors of 1 %, 5 %, 5 % indicate high variance; 10 %, 11 %, 12 % indicate high avoidable bias; 10 %, 11 %, 20 % indicate high avoidable bias and data mismatch. Its remedy for mismatch is to find out by error analysis how the dev data differ, for example car noise in the dev audio and quiet backgrounds in the training audio, and then to collect or synthesise training data that match (p. 81). In the recording's example the training and dev sets came from one shuffled pool, so the mismatch term is zero in expectation and the whole gap is variance plus bias; with 95 % on training the bias term is small, and the recording's diagnosis of overfitting follows. Once the team trains on synthetic mixtures, as the next section describes, and evaluates on real clips, the mismatch term can no longer be assumed away, and a training-dev set of held-out synthetic mixtures is what measures it.

What adding two waveforms does

The recording justifies the addition by the superposition of sound: pressure waves from two sources add (≈2507–2533 s). In the linear model of sound that the statement invokes, the pressure a microphone records from two sources is the sum of the pressures each would produce alone. Let \(x_{\mathrm{bg}}\) and \(x_{\mathrm{fg}}\) be the background and the clean phrase, each a vector of \(n_{\mathrm{samp}}\) samples, and let the synthesised input be

\[x=x_{\mathrm{bg}}+A\,x_{\mathrm{fg}},\]

where the amplitude scale \(A\gt0\) sets how loud the phrase is. The mean power of a waveform is the average of its squared samples: \(P_{\mathrm{bg}}=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_jx_{\mathrm{bg},j}^2\) and \(P_{\mathrm{fg}}=\frac1{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_jx_{\mathrm{fg},j}^2\), where \(j\) runs over the \(n_{\mathrm{samp}}\) samples. Expanding the square,

\[\frac1{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_jx_j^2=P_{\mathrm{bg}}+A^2P_{\mathrm{fg}}+\frac{2A}{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_jx_{\mathrm{bg},j}\,x_{\mathrm{fg},j}.\]

The last term is the cross term. For a background recorded independently of the phrase it is a sum of products of unrelated signals and is small compared with the others (the code measures it); treating it as zero is the assumption under which powers add. The signal-to-noise ratio of the mixture is then \(\mathrm{SNR}=A^2P_{\mathrm{fg}}/P_{\mathrm{bg}}\), usually quoted in decibels,

\[\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}=10\log_{10}\frac{A^2P_{\mathrm{fg}}}{P_{\mathrm{bg}}}=20\log_{10}A+10\log_{10}\frac{P_{\mathrm{fg}}}{P_{\mathrm{bg}}}.\]

Doubling \(A\) adds \(20\log_{10}2=6.02\) dB, whatever the two waveforms. A script that must produce mixtures at a target ratio solves for the scale: \(A=\sqrt{10^{\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}/10}\,P_{\mathrm{bg}}/P_{\mathrm{fg}}}\). In the code's fixture, a one-second tone of power 0.3 against Gaussian white noise of standard deviation 0.3 (power 0.09 in expectation), \(A=1\) gives 5.27 dB and \(A=0.5\) gives \(-0.75\) dB, a difference of 6.02 dB, and the cross term is 0.5 % of \(P_{\mathrm{fg}}+P_{\mathrm{bg}}\) (engineering check; 5.27 dB rather than \(10\log_{10}(0.3/0.09)=5.23\) dB because the sampled noise has power slightly below 0.09). A training set that spans a range of \(A\) spans a range of loudness of the command relative to the room, which is the variable a real lamp faces.

The superposition statement is physics; that the resulting data made a good product is the lecturer's experience (speaker judgment, ≈2591–2711 s). Two properties of real recordings are missing from the sum. A room adds reflections, so the command reaches the microphone as a sum of delayed and attenuated copies of itself, and a microphone and its amplifier have a frequency response and can clip loud input. A clean utterance recorded close to a good microphone and added to noise has neither. A detector trained only on such mixtures can therefore fail in a reverberant room, and that failure would appear as data mismatch in Proposition 5, which is why the dev set must be real recordings. Warden (2018) made the same judgment in building a keyword-spotting dataset: studio-captured samples "would lack background noise, would be captured with high-quality microphones", so utterances were captured through phone or laptop microphones "wherever users happened to be" (p. 3). He also added several minute-long files of background noise, some recorded in noisy places and some generated, "to help train and test" a model's ability to distinguish audio that contains speech from audio that contains none (p. 6).

A detector of speech instead of a detector of the phrase

The recording warns that the procedure as first described fails (≈2612–2641 s). If every synthesised positive contains speech and every negative is background alone, the easiest rule that separates them is "is someone speaking?", and the network becomes a voice activity detection (VAD) system, a detector of speech and not of "Robert, turn on". The fix is to mix in recordings of other words as negatives, so that speech appears on both sides of the label (≈2641–2671 s).

Proposition 6 (the speech shortcut). Let \(\mathrm{sp}(x)\in\{0,1\}\) indicate that a window contains speech. Suppose every positive training window contains speech and no negative training window does. Then the rule "fire when \(\mathrm{sp}(x)=1\)" has zero training error, and on deployed windows that contain speech other than the phrase it fires on every one.

Proof. On the training set, \(\mathrm{sp}(x)=y\) for every window by assumption, so the rule makes no error. On a deployed window with speech other than the phrase, \(\mathrm{sp}(x)=1\) and \(y=0\), so the rule fires and is wrong. \(\blacksquare\)

A learner that minimises training error has no reason to prefer the phrase detector to the speech detector, since both have zero training error, and the speech detector is typically the simpler function of the audio. Adding negatives that contain speech removes the tie: on those windows \(\mathrm{sp}(x)=1\) and \(y=0\), so the speech rule now makes training errors and only a rule that distinguishes the phrase from other speech can reach zero. Geirhos et al. (2020) call such rules shortcuts: "decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging testing conditions, such as real-world scenarios" (p. 1). Warden lists among the requirements of keyword spotting that "most of the input that is speech will be unrelated to the voice interface, so the model should be unlikely to trigger on arbitrary speech" (p. 2), and the evaluation standard he proposes scores, besides the ten target words, an "Unknown Word" category and a "Silence" category of one-second clips cut from the background-noise files (p. 7). Deployed keyword spotters often place a voice-activity detector in front of the keyword detector: Chen, Parada and Heigold use one "to reduce computation" and run the keyword network only in voice regions (p. 2). Behind such a front end the keyword detector sees speech almost all the time, so its ability to reject speech other than the phrase is exactly what determines its false-activation rate.

Why a factor of two in speed matters 55:57

The whiteboard plots error against months for "you" and for a "competitor" that needs twice as long to reach each level, and the recording concludes that the factor of two becomes a large difference in the performance customers see at any moment (≈3357–3452 s). The curves are drawn, not measured. The model behind them can be stated. Let \(\mathrm{Err}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})\) be the error of the team's system after \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}\) months of development, a decreasing function; its argument, calendar time, distinguishes it from the learning curve \(\mathrm{Err}(m)\) of chapter 1, whose argument is the number of training examples. A competitor that needs twice as long for everything has error

\[\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{slow}}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})=\mathrm{Err}(t_{\mathrm{cal}}/2).\]

In time, the factor is exactly two by construction: the competitor reaches any error level at twice the date. In error, the size of the difference depends on the shape of the curve. If \(\mathrm{Err}\) is differentiable, with derivative \(d\mathrm{Err}/dt_{\mathrm{cal}}\), the mean value theorem gives, for some \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}'\) between \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}/2\) and \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}\),

\[\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{slow}}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})-\mathrm{Err}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})=\frac{t_{\mathrm{cal}}}{2}\,\Bigl\lvert\frac{d\mathrm{Err}}{dt_{\mathrm{cal}}}(t_{\mathrm{cal}}')\Bigr\rvert:\]

half the elapsed time, multiplied by the rate of improvement somewhere in the second half of it. The gap is large while the curve is steep and small once it has flattened. Take, as an assumption of this example only, \(\mathrm{Err}(t_{\mathrm{cal}})=0.05+0.30\,e^{-t_{\mathrm{cal}}/3}\) with \(t_{\mathrm{cal}}\) in months (Figure 2). At three months the team's error is 0.160 and the competitor's 0.232, a gap of 0.072; at twelve months they are 0.056 and 0.091, a gap of 0.035 (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Measured instead as excess error above the floor 0.05, the competitor's is \(e^{t_{\mathrm{cal}}/6}\) times the team's, 1.65 at three months and 7.39 at twelve, and that ratio grows without limit. For a curve whose excess error falls in proportion to a fixed power of time, the same ratio is constant. Whether a factor of two in speed is "massive" therefore depends on which curve holds and on what the customer compares; the recording's conclusion is speaker judgment about markets (≈3423–3486 s), and the model above says what it would take to test it.

What a share measures

A share is a statement about failures, not about stages. If a fraction \(p_{\mathrm{fail}}\) of all queries fail and the rows are a random sample of failures, the fraction of all queries on which stage \(j\) is marked is \(p_{\mathrm{fail}}\,\mathbb E[\hat p_j]\). With \(p_{\mathrm{fail}}=0.3\), a fetch share of 0.70 corresponds to a bad page selection on 21 % of all queries. The spreadsheet by design contains no successful queries, so it cannot estimate \(p_{\mathrm{fail}}\), and a stage's share can be high because the stage is bad or because the other stages are good. The share also depends on how rows are chosen. The recording selects queries on which the performance is clearly below what a person would do (≈4277–4307 s), which is a sample of the failures that a reader notices, not a random sample of all failures.

Proposition 7 (what a local defect repair can gain). Model each failing row's marks as an exhaustive set of defects: a row with no remaining defects succeeds. Suppose a perfect repair of stage \(j\) removes its defect on every row marked at \(j\), preserves the presence or absence of every other defect, and leaves every row unmarked at \(j\) unchanged. If each remaining defect is sufficient by itself to spoil the report, the repair fixes exactly the rows marked only at \(j\). If this sufficiency assumption is dropped while the exhaustive-defect and local-repair assumptions are retained, the fraction of failing rows repaired lies between the fraction marked only at \(j\) and \(\hat p_j\).

Proof. A row unmarked at \(j\) is unchanged, so the repaired fraction is at most \(\hat p_j\); this is the targeted-slice argument of chapter 3, Proposition 3. A row marked only at \(j\) has no defects after the repair and therefore succeeds. If every remaining defect suffices to cause failure, every jointly marked row still fails, giving equality with the singleton fraction. Without that sufficiency assumption, jointly marked rows may succeed or fail, which gives the stated bounds. \(\blacksquare\)

These are conditional bounds for the defect model. Under its assumptions, a fetch share of 70 % is a ceiling on the fraction of the original failures repaired. For an actual pipeline, a better upstream output changes what later stages receive, so their correctness need not stay fixed: the marks alone establish neither the singleton lower bound nor a causal repair ceiling without the stated assumptions. Machine Learning Yearning gives an operational procedure: replace the first stage's output on a failing example with a hand-made "perfect" output and run the rest of the pipeline; if the result is correct, attribute the error to that stage, otherwise try the next stage, attributing it to the last stage if none of the earlier substitutions repair it (pp. 110–111). This assigns each failure one category, so its shares sum to 100 %. It tests specified interventions on the chosen examples; it does not guarantee the effect of a newly trained stage on all queries.

How many failing queries to read

Suppose the \(n_{\mathrm{ex}}\) rows are a random sample of failures, and stage \(j\) is marked on a fraction \(\mathbb E[\hat p_j]\) of the whole population of failures. The number of rows marked at \(j\) is then binomial, and

\[\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_j)=\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb E[\hat p_j]\,\bigl(1-\mathbb E[\hat p_j]\bigr)}{n_{\mathrm{ex}}}}.\]

With \(\mathbb E[\hat p_j]=0.70\) and \(n_{\mathrm{ex}}=50\) this is \(\sqrt{0.21/50}=0.065\); with \(\mathbb E[\hat p_j]=0.20\), it is 0.057 (engineering check by shown arithmetic; the code's simulation of 20,000 spreadsheets gives 0.065 for the first).

Proposition 8 (interval separation at the expected shares). Call the shares of two stages, numbered 1 and 2, separated when their intervals \(\hat p_j\pm1.96\,\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_j)\) do not overlap. For this planning calculation, place each estimate at its expectation. If \(\mathbb E[\hat p_1]\gt\mathbb E[\hat p_2]\), the intervals are separated exactly when

\[n_{\mathrm{ex}}\gt\Biggl(\frac{1.96\Bigl(\sqrt{\mathbb E[\hat p_1](1-\mathbb E[\hat p_1])}+\sqrt{\mathbb E[\hat p_2](1-\mathbb E[\hat p_2])}\Bigr)}{\mathbb E[\hat p_1]-\mathbb E[\hat p_2]}\Biggr)^2.\]

Proof. The intervals fail to overlap when \(\mathbb E[\hat p_1]-1.96\,\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_1)\gt\mathbb E[\hat p_2]+1.96\,\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_2)\). Substitute the formula for \(\mathrm{sd}\), multiply both sides by \(\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{ex}}}\), and solve for \(n_{\mathrm{ex}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

For the board's 70 % against 20 %, the right-hand side is \(\bigl(1.96\times(0.458+0.400)/0.50\bigr)^2=11.3\), so the expected-centre calculation first gives separation at 12 rows. For 20 % against 5 %, it is \(\bigl(1.96\times(0.400+0.218)/0.15\bigr)^2=65.2\), giving 66 rows (engineering check by shown arithmetic). These calculations illustrate the scale of the recording's suggested 10 to 100 rows; they do not prescribe a probability of obtaining separated intervals or a correct ranking in a random sample. For independent stage marks on each row, write \(\mathrm{sd}_j=\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_j)\). The standard deviation of \(\hat p_1-\hat p_2\) is then \(\sqrt{\mathrm{sd}_1^2+\mathrm{sd}_2^2}\), less than the sum \(\mathrm{sd}_1+\mathrm{sd}_2\) used by the non-overlap criterion; the code's simulation draws marks independently. With paired marks on the same rows, \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat p_1-\hat p_2)=\mathrm{sd}_1^2+\mathrm{sd}_2^2-2\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat p_1,\hat p_2)\), where \(\mathrm{Cov}\) denotes covariance. Positive covariance lowers this variance relative to the independent case; negative covariance raises it. In either case, the covariance inequality \(\lvert\mathrm{Cov}(\hat p_1,\hat p_2)\rvert\le\mathrm{sd}_1\mathrm{sd}_2\) implies \(\mathrm{sd}(\hat p_1-\hat p_2)\le\mathrm{sd}_1+\mathrm{sd}_2\). The sum remains an upper bound, although the amount of conservatism depends on the joint marks. Rows with exactly one of the two marks give negative covariance; merely allowing several marks does not establish positive covariance. Proposition 8 is an arithmetic separation criterion evaluated at the expected shares, not a guarantee that a realised sample ranks the stages correctly. The criterion also assumes random rows, which a reader's selection of "clearly bad" queries does not provide. In the code's simulation of 20,000 spreadsheets of 50 rows each, with marks drawn independently at the board's four rates, the fetch column had the largest share in every one (engineering check).

The recording's cost argument is that three or four hours of such analysis can save weeks spent in the wrong direction, and it recalls teams that spent six months on work that an early analysis would have shown to be misdirected, such as trying one web-search service after another when the pages chosen were the problem (speaker judgment, ≈4413–4494 s). Machine Learning Yearning makes the same estimate for a classifier: at one minute per example, reading 100 misclassified dev examples takes under two hours, and "these two hours could save you a month of wasted effort" (p. 31). It is the same author's judgment in a second place, not independent evidence. What the propositions add is a conditional repair bound and a way to compare the expected gap between two shares with their sampling variation under an explicit random-row model.

The program below checks the chapter's computed numbers in one run. Its inputs are fixed: the running case with \(\mu=(0.5,0.5)\) and prevalence 1/30, a synthetic tone and noise at 16 kHz, 25 dev clips with true clip accuracy 0.9, and the board's four attribution rates, with seeded random draws. It has no side effects beyond printing. Each check is an assert, so a wrong formula or a changed constant stops the program with an AssertionError; the fitting function raises ValueError for a nonpositive example weight. It needs Python 3 and NumPy, and it ran in a fresh process (python -I) with Python 3.13.5 and NumPy 2.2.6 in about 2 seconds.

The program has eight numbered parts. (1) It checks the constant-0 accuracy and the best constant loss of the 3,000 windows. (2) It evaluates the optimal detector's operating points for \(\omega_{+}=1\) and \(\omega_{+}=29\) from the two Gaussian scores, and the second-moment coefficients of Proposition 2(c). (3) It fits logistic regression by Newton's method to 3,000 simulated windows with weight 29 on the positives and on the set with each positive copied 29 times, and asserts that the two fits give the same parameters (Proposition 2a); it also fits without a weight and measures recall and precision on 600,000 fresh windows. (4) It fits both models on 300,000 windows and asserts that the intercepts differ by \(\log29\) to within 0.05, that the slopes are within 0.05 of the exact value \(2\mu\) (Proposition 3), and that the prior-corrected probabilities average to the prevalence. (5) It simulates the offset of the phrase end and checks the mean count of Proposition 4 for three pairs of hop and tolerance. (6) It mixes the tone into the noise at two amplitudes and asserts the power expansion and the 6.02 dB step. (7) It simulates the best of ten equally good configurations on 25 dev clips and asserts the bound of Proposition 1. (8) It simulates 20,000 spreadsheets of 50 failing queries.

import math
import numpy as np

MU = np.array([0.5, 0.5])          # running-case class mean, ||mu||^2 = 0.5


def Phi(z):
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2.0)))


def operating_point(c_s, p_pos):
    """Recall, false activations, precision, accuracy when s >= c_s."""
    rec = 1.0 - Phi((c_s - 1.0) / math.sqrt(2.0))    # s ~ N(1, 2) if y = 1
    fa = 1.0 - Phi((c_s + 1.0) / math.sqrt(2.0))     # s ~ N(-1, 2) if y = 0
    prec = p_pos * rec / (p_pos * rec + (1.0 - p_pos) * fa)
    acc = p_pos * rec + (1.0 - p_pos) * (1.0 - fa)
    return rec, fa, prec, acc


def windows(n_pos, n_neg, rng):
    """Running-case features: N(mu, I) if y = 1, N(-mu, I) if y = 0."""
    h = np.vstack([MU + rng.standard_normal((n_pos, 2)),
                   -MU + rng.standard_normal((n_neg, 2))])
    y = np.concatenate([np.ones(n_pos), np.zeros(n_neg)])
    return h, y


def fit_logistic(h, y, weight, steps=30):
    """Weighted logistic regression u = w.h + b by Newton's method."""
    if np.any(weight <= 0):
        raise ValueError("example weights must be positive")
    a = np.column_stack([h, np.ones(len(y))])
    theta = np.zeros(a.shape[1])
    for _ in range(steps):
        p = 1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-a @ theta))
        grad = a.T @ (weight * (p - y))
        hess = (a * (weight * p * (1.0 - p))[:, None]).T @ a
        theta -= np.linalg.solve(hess, grad)
    return theta[:-1], theta[-1]


def rates(w, b, h, y):
    fire = h @ w + b >= 0.0
    rec = fire[y == 1].mean()
    prec = y[fire].mean()
    return round(float(rec), 3), round(float(prec), 3)

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction.

The models hold under the assumptions stated beside their proofs: population optimality and calibration in Proposition 3; a regular window grid and a uniform phrase offset in Proposition 4; comparable sampling in Proposition 5; additive mixing and the insufficiency of speech negatives alone in Proposition 6; one decision per loop and an assumed development curve in the iteration model; and the joint-cause hypotheses and random-row sampling in Propositions 7 and 8. The examples check those models on stated fixtures; they do not establish a deployed detector's performance or a real team's development curve.

Advice without a model. The recommendations on literature search, writing to experts, speed of building, natural data before synthetic data, discipline of the daily cycle and the value of experience are the lecturer's judgment. This chapter reports them with their times and does not test them.

What you can now do. You can compute the accuracy and the best constant loss of a detector that has learned nothing, and explain why accuracy hides the failure. You can show that duplication and weighting define the same objective but different gradient noise, derive the logit shift that weighting causes, convert a weight into a decision threshold and a cost ratio, and undo the shift. You can count the positive windows that a label tolerance creates. You can synthesise mixtures at a target signal-to-noise ratio and explain why negatives must contain speech. You can decompose an error gap with a training-dev set and say when each term means what its name says. You can bound the optimism of a dev score after tuning. And you can read an error-analysis spreadsheet: state what a share measures, identify the assumptions needed to turn it into a repair bound, and calculate expected-centre interval separation without treating it as a guarantee of a sample ranking.

Exercises

The exercises test derivation (1), computation with an assumption check (2, 3), diagnosis of an error table (4), reading an error-analysis spreadsheet (5) and design (6). 1. Weights on both classes, and a cost ratio

Weight the loss of positives by \(\omega_{+}\) and of negatives by \(\omega_{-}\), both positive. (a) Find the logit that minimises the expected weighted loss at an input with \(p(y=1\mid x)=p_{\mathrm{cond}}\). (b) Show that only the ratio \(\omega_{+}/\omega_{-}\) matters for the decision at logit 0, and give the threshold on \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\). (c) Keeping each negative with probability \(\tfrac14\) and no weights: what logit shift does this produce at the optimum? (d) The lamp's designers judge a false activation five times as costly as a missed command. What ratio \(\omega_{+}/\omega_{-}\) makes firing at logit 0 minimise expected cost, and is the lecture's duplication by 29 consistent with that judgment?

Solution

(a) The expected loss is \(\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}\log(1+e^{-u})+\omega_{-}(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\log(1+e^{u})\). Its derivative in \(u\) is \(-\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}(1-\sigma(u))+\omega_{-}(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\sigma(u)\), increasing in \(u\), and zero when \(e^{u}=\sigma(u)/(1-\sigma(u))=\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}/(\omega_{-}(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}))\). So \(u^*=\log\frac{p_{\mathrm{cond}}}{1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}}+\log\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{-}}\). (b) The weights enter only through \(\log(\omega_{+}/\omega_{-})\), so multiplying both by the same constant changes nothing but the scale of the loss. The optimum is nonnegative when \(\omega_{+}p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge\omega_{-}(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\), that is, \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge\omega_{-}/(\omega_{+}+\omega_{-})\). (c) By the undersampling identity of the remedies section, keeping each negative with probability \(\tfrac14\) gives in expectation the weights \(\omega_{+}=1\), \(\omega_{-}=\tfrac14\), so the shift is \(\log4=1.39\), and firing at logit 0 corresponds to \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge0.2\). (d) At an input with conditional probability \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\), firing costs \(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}}\) false activations in expectation and not firing costs \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\) missed commands. With a false activation five times as costly, fire when \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge5(1-p_{\mathrm{cond}})\), that is, \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge5/6\). Part (b) gives this threshold when \(\omega_{-}/(\omega_{+}+\omega_{-})=5/6\), so \(\omega_{+}/\omega_{-}=1/5\): the negatives, not the positives, should get the larger weight. Duplication by 29 sets the threshold at \(p_{\mathrm{cond}}\ge1/30\), which corresponds to a missed command 29 times as costly as a false activation, the opposite judgment. The exercise shows that the weight is a decision about costs; the recording chose it to make training work, and the deployed threshold can then be set separately by prior correction.

2. Counting positives when the hop is known

Suppose the lamp's clips are 10 s long, windows are 3 s long, and window ends are spaced \(t_{\mathrm{hop}}=0.05\) s apart. Assume each phrase ends after the first window end and at least 1 s before the end of the clip. (a) How many windows does one clip give? (b) For tolerances of 0.1, 0.5 and 1 s, find the positives per clip, the prevalence \(p_{+}\), and the ratio of positives to negatives. Which tolerance meets the recording's rule of thumb of 1:10 or better? (c) With \(t_{\mathrm{hop}}=0.25\) s and a tolerance of 0.1 s, what fraction of clips contain no positive window, assuming the phrase ends at a uniformly random point of the grid?

Solution

(a) Window ends run from 3 s to 10 s in steps of 0.05 s: \((10-3)/0.05+1=141\) windows. (b) By Proposition 4, the tolerance divided by the hop is an integer in all three cases (2, 10, 20), so the count is exact: 2, 10 and 20 positives per clip. The prevalences are \(2/141=0.014\), \(10/141=0.071\) and \(20/141=0.142\), and the ratios of positives to negatives are \(2:139=1:69.5\), \(10:131=1:13.1\) and \(20:121=1:6.1\). Only the 1 s tolerance reaches 1:10 or better; the 0.5 s tolerance stays just outside it. The comparison with the chapter's figures (1:5 and 1:2) shows how strongly the prevalence depends on the hop, which the recording did not state. (c) The ratio \(t_{\mathrm{tol}}/t_{\mathrm{hop}}=0.4\), so by the proof of Proposition 4 a clip has one positive window with probability 0.4 and none with probability 0.6. Sixty percent of clips would carry no positive label at all, and their commands would be taught to the network as negatives, which is a labelling error, not an imbalance. A tolerance shorter than the hop is therefore not a usable design.

3. Mixing at a target ratio, and when powers do not add

A clean utterance has mean power \(P_{\mathrm{fg}}=0.04\) and a background \(P_{\mathrm{bg}}=0.01\), in squared amplitude units. (a) Find the scale \(A\) for mixtures at 0 dB and at 10 dB. (b) What \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}\) results from \(A=2\)? (c) Suppose the "background" is the room's echo of the same utterance, arriving in phase: \(x_{\mathrm{bg}}=0.5\,x_{\mathrm{fg}}\). Compute the mean power of \(x_{\mathrm{bg}}+x_{\mathrm{fg}}\) exactly and with the formula that drops the cross term, and explain the difference.

Solution

(a) \(\mathrm{SNR}=A^2P_{\mathrm{fg}}/P_{\mathrm{bg}}=4A^2\). At 0 dB, \(4A^2=1\), so \(A=0.5\). At 10 dB, \(4A^2=10\), so \(A=\sqrt{2.5}=1.581\). (b) \(4\times2^2=16\), and \(10\log_{10}16=12.04\) dB; equivalently, 0 dB at \(A=0.5\) plus two doublings of 6.02 dB each. (c) Exactly, \(x=1.5\,x_{\mathrm{fg}}\), with power \(2.25\times0.04=0.09\). Dropping the cross term gives \(P_{\mathrm{bg}}+P_{\mathrm{fg}}=0.25\times0.04+0.04=0.05\). The cross term is \(2\times\frac1{n_{\mathrm{samp}}}\sum_j0.5\,x_{\mathrm{fg},j}\,x_{\mathrm{fg},j}=P_{\mathrm{fg}}=0.04\), the whole difference. Powers add only for signals whose samples are uncorrelated; an echo is the same signal delayed, and at zero delay it is perfectly correlated. A real echo arrives with a delay, which makes the cross term smaller but not zero, and filters the sound. This is one of the ways the additive synthesis differs from a real room.

4. Reading three error tables

For the lamp, take the human-level error on real clips to be 2 %. The team trains on synthetic mixtures, keeps a training-dev set of held-out synthetic mixtures, and uses real clips as the dev set. Diagnose each case with Proposition 5 and name the next action: (a) training 3 %, training-dev 4 %, dev 15 %; (b) training 10 %, training-dev 11 %, dev 12 %; (c) training 3 %, training-dev 12 %, dev 10 %, with 25 dev clips and 200 training-dev clips.

Solution

(a) Avoidable bias \(3-2=1\) point, variance \(4-3=1\) point, data mismatch \(15-4=11\) points. The synthetic mixtures are learned and generalise within their own distribution, but they do not resemble real clips. Next action: find out, by listening to failed dev clips, how they differ, for example reverberation or a microphone the synthesis did not model, and change the synthesis or collect real training clips with that property (Machine Learning Yearning, p. 81). (b) Bias 8 points, variance 1, mismatch 1. The model underfits even its training data: a larger network, longer training or better features, not more data. On an edge chip the network size is bounded, which makes this case the hard one. (c) Bias 1, variance 9, mismatch \(10-12=-2\) points. The negative mismatch term says the real clips are, if anything, easier than the synthetic ones, which is plausible if the synthesis includes harsher noise than real rooms. But with 25 dev clips the standard deviation of a 10 % error estimate is \(\sqrt{0.1\times0.9/25}=0.06\), six points, and with 200 training-dev clips that of 12 % is \(\sqrt{0.12\times0.88/200}=0.023\); a difference of \(-2\) points is well inside the noise, so the sign of the mismatch term is not established. The clear finding is variance: regularise or add more synthetic data, and enlarge the dev set before interpreting small terms.

5. A spreadsheet of 40 failures

An analyst reads 40 failing queries of the deep researcher. Page selection is marked on 26, search terms on 9, writing on 8 and web search on 4. Of the 26 rows marked at page selection, 5 are also marked at writing and 2 at search terms; no other row has two marks, no row has three, and every row has at least one. (a) Compute the four shares and their sum. (b) Under all the hypotheses of Proposition 7, what fraction of these failures does a perfect local page-selection repair fix? What bounds remain if only the assumption that each defect alone forces failure is dropped? (c) Suppose 30 % of all queries fail, the spreadsheet's joint mark frequencies equal those of the failure population, and the repair leaves originally successful queries successful. What range of overall success rates follows under the weaker assumptions in part (b)? (d) Do the page-selection and search-terms intervals fail to overlap when the observed shares are substituted into Proposition 8's standard-deviation formula? What does this calculation alone establish?

Solution

(a) The shares are 0.650 for page selection, 0.225 for search terms, 0.200 for writing and 0.100 for web search; their sum is 1.175. There are \(26+9+8+4=47\) marks, comprising one per row plus 7 extra marks. The singleton counts are \(26-7=19\), \(9-2=7\), \(8-5=3\), and 4; these sum with the 7 doubly marked rows to 40. (b) There are \(26-5-2=19\) rows marked only at page selection. All the hypotheses give exactly \(19/40=0.475\) repaired. Dropping only the individual-defect sufficiency assumption gives a range from 0.475 to \(26/40=0.650\), while retaining exhaustive defects and local repair. (c) Under the population-frequency and no-harm assumptions in the question, success rises from 0.70 to between \(0.70+0.30\times0.475=0.8425\) and \(0.70+0.30\times0.650=0.895\). Empirical frequencies estimated from 40 failures would add sampling uncertainty; successful-query performance must be checked separately for an actual replacement selector. (d) The plug-in standard deviations are \(\sqrt{0.65\times0.35/40}=0.075\) and \(\sqrt{0.225\times0.775/40}=0.066\). The corresponding intervals are \(0.650\pm0.148\), whose lower end is 0.502, and \(0.225\pm0.129\), whose upper end is 0.354. They do not overlap. Substitution into the sample-size expression gives \(\bigl(1.96\times(0.477+0.418)/0.425\bigr)^2=17.0\), below 40. This is descriptive interval separation for these shares; without a sampling model it is not a confidence statement about the population ordering.

6. Shipping without a test set, honestly

The lamp team tunes 20 configurations on its 25 dev clips and ships the best, whose dev score is 0.92. (a) Bound the optimism of that score, assuming a common true accuracy near 0.9. (b) Design an evaluation that can report the deployed clip accuracy with a standard deviation of at most 0.02. State what must be kept separate and how the clips must be split. (c) What does the design cost, and when is the recording's practice of skipping it reasonable?

Solution

(a) With \(\mathrm{sd}=\sqrt{0.9\times0.1/25}=0.06\) and \(n_{\mathrm{cfg}}=20\), Proposition 1 bounds the expected optimism by \(0.06\sqrt{2\log20}=0.06\times2.448=0.147\); independent Gaussian scores would give \(0.06\times1.867=0.112\). A dev score of 0.92 after this search is consistent with a true accuracy well below 0.9, and it cannot be quoted as the lamp's accuracy. (b) Collect a fresh set of real clips that plays no part in any choice: not in training, not in choosing configurations, not in choosing the threshold. At accuracy near 0.9, a standard deviation of at most 0.02 requires at least \(0.09/0.02^2=225\) independent clips. Split by speaker and recording session, not by window or even by clip alone, so that no voice or room in the test set was heard in training or tuning; windows from one clip overlap, and clips from one speaker share a voice. Report clip-level recall and false activations per hour of negative audio, the two quantities a user experiences. (c) The cost is 225 more real clips, collected with consent, and the discipline not to look at them until the end. Skipping the test set is reasonable, as the recording says, when the goal is a working product whose quality users will judge directly and no performance number will be claimed; it is not reasonable when a number will be reported, to a customer, a regulator or a paper.
