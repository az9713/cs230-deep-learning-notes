Learning which actions are good from a delayed reward

A program that plays Go must choose a move now, but it learns whether the move was good only when the game ends, dozens of moves later, and nobody can say which move deserves the credit. This chapter asks how an agent can learn from such a delayed scalar reward, how a neural network can replace the table of action values when the number of situations is astronomically large, and how the same machinery aligns a language model with the preferences of the people who use it.

The answer has three layers. A Markov decision process states the problem; the Bellman optimality equation characterises its solution and, because the equation is a contraction, determines it uniquely. Q-learning turns the equation into a regression target that the agent can compute from its own experience, and deep Q-learning fits that target with a network. The tabular convergence guarantee no longer applies; experience replay reduces temporal correlation in the training data, and exploration collects experience with actions that a greedy policy can miss. Reinforcement learning from human feedback then replaces the game score by a reward model trained on human comparisons and adds a penalty that keeps the model close to where it started. One five-state problem, the recycling example of the recording, carries every derivation and is solved exactly by the program near the end of the chapter.

Why a game of Go is not a supervised-learning problem 0:05

Provenance. Lecture: the plan of the lecture, the four results on slide 4, the question of how to solve Go with supervised learning and the three issues of slide 5, the definition of reinforcement learning as learning sequences of good decisions, teaching by experience against teaching by example, and applications (≈5–839 s). Research extension: the supervised and reinforcement stages of AlphaGo (Silver et al. 2016), AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al. 2017), the DQN results of Mnih et al. (2015). Original construction: the state count and the statement of what the supervised target can reach.

Chapter 2 trained networks on pairs \((x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\) of an input and its label. The obvious way to teach a network Go follows that pattern. Collect positions from games of strong players; let the input \(x\) be the board and the label \(y\) be the move the player chose; train a classifier whose softmax output assigns a probability to each of the legal moves. Slide 5 draws exactly this and lists three objections, which the recording develops with the audience (≈312–640 s).

The first objection concerns the label. A classifier trained with cross-entropy is pushed toward the conditional distribution of the labels given the input (chapter 2, Proposition 9, "what next-token cross-entropy rewards", whose argument applies to any categorical label, a move as well as a token). Its best possible behaviour is therefore to reproduce the chosen move, including every move that a strong player would, on reflection, not repeat. The target is the player's choice, not the best move, and the recording adds that even a panel of the best players does not define the best move (≈549–609 s). The second objection concerns coverage. The recording describes the board on screen as 13 by 13 (≈434–458 s); both boards drawn on slide 5 have 19 lines each way, the full size on which AlphaGo played. A \(19\times19\) board has 361 intersections, each empty, black or white, so there are at most \(3^{361}\) configurations; since \(361\log_{10}3=172.2\), this is about \(1.7\times10^{172}\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic; an upper bound that includes illegal configurations). The 30 million positions on which AlphaGo's supervised network was trained (next paragraph) are a fraction \(30\times10^{6}/(1.7\times10^{172})\approx2\times10^{-165}\) of that bound (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The third objection concerns strategy: a classifier makes one decision per position and is never told which earlier decision led to a win or a loss.

AlphaGo began with exactly the supervised model the slide describes and then went beyond it, which turns the three objections into measurements. Silver et al. (2016) trained a 13-layer policy network on 30 million positions from the KGS Go server; it predicted the expert's move on held-out positions with accuracy 57.0% (p. 2; empirical result). They then improved this network by reinforcement learning from games against earlier versions of itself, and the resulting policy "won more than 80% of games" against the supervised one (p. 2; empirical result). The full system, which adds tree search, defeated the European champion by 5 games to 0 on the full 19×19 board (p. 1; empirical result). The later AlphaGo Zero started "tabula rasa", with no human games at all, and won 100–0 against the published champion-defeating AlphaGo (Silver et al. 2017, author manuscript p. 1; empirical result). The recording summarises this history as Go having been solved in 2017 (≈211–237 s). In the sense used for games, a solved game is one whose outcome under perfect play by both sides is known; the papers report wins against the strongest human players and programs, not a solution, and do not claim one.

The recording defines reinforcement learning (RL) as learning, automatically, to make good sequences of decisions when the feedback is delayed (≈640–672 s); supervised learning teaches by example, reinforcement learning by experience (slide 5). The same move can be good or bad depending on what follows it, so the learner must be credited with the consequences of whole sequences. The recording lists games, robotics and advertising as applications, and calls advertising, where a sequence of ads precedes a purchase, the largest one (speaker judgment, ≈778–839 s). The result the recording opens with is the deep Q-network of Mnih et al. (2015), which the recording describes as one algorithm that learned "40, 50" Atari games (≈157–186 s). The paper evaluated it on 49 games with the same network, algorithm and hyperparameters, found it comparable to a professional human games tester across the set, and reports more than 75% of the tester's score on 29 of them (p. 2; empirical result). The rest of the first half of this chapter builds that algorithm.

The agent–environment loop, and when a state is Markov 13:59

Provenance. Lecture: agent, environment, state, action, observation, reward and transition (slide 6); observation equal to the state in chess and Go but not under fog of war; restricting observations for computational reasons (≈839–1072 s). Research extension: the Markov property and the finite Markov decision process as stated by Sutton and Barto. Original construction: the formal definitions as used in this chapter.

Slide 6 draws the loop that every later section instantiates. At each time step \(t=0,1,2,\dots\) an agent (the learner) sees the environment (everything else) in a state \(s_t\), chooses an action \(a_t\), and receives a scalar reward \(r_t\) and an observation \(o_t\) while the environment moves to \(s_{t+1}\). The recording calls one step of this loop, \((s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})\), a transition (≈984–1030 s). The word "agent" here means the decision-maker of this loop; chapters 7 and 8 use "agentic workflow" for a multi-step program built around a language model, which is a different object.

Why distinguish the observation from the state? In chess and Go the observation is the whole board, so \(o_t=s_t\). In StarCraft or League of Legends a "fog" hides the parts of the map that no friendly unit is near, so the observation carries less information than the state (≈931–984 s). The distinction matters because the mathematics of this chapter needs a state with a specific property. Sutton and Barto state it: the state "must include information about all aspects of the past agent–environment interaction that make a difference for the future", and a state that does is said to have the Markov property (p. 49).

A finite Markov decision process (MDP) collects the objects of the loop. It has a finite set of states \(\mathcal S\), a finite set of actions \(\mathcal A\), transition probabilities \(p(s'\mid s,a)\), the probability that action \(a\) in state \(s\) leads to the next state \(s'\), an expected immediate reward \(r(s,a)\), and a discount factor \(\gamma\in[0,1]\) defined in the next section. In this chapter a prime marks the next state or the next action. The Markov property is what makes \(p\) and \(r\) functions of \((s,a)\) alone: the history before \(s_t\) adds nothing to the prediction of \(s_{t+1}\) and \(r_t\). Some states are terminal: entering one ends an episode, and a new episode restarts from an initial state (≈1196–1223 s). A policy \(\pi\) is the agent's rule for choosing actions, written \(\pi(a\mid s)\) for the probability of action \(a\) in state \(s\); a deterministic policy is written \(\pi(s)\).

Asked whether an agent might deliberately observe less than it could, the recording answers that in an open-world game one may restrict attention to a region that holds nearly all the information needed, for computational reasons (speaker judgment, ≈1030–1072 s). Whatever the agent is given as input must still be Markov for the equations below to hold; the Breakout section returns to this condition, and the next section shows how a clock breaks it.

The recycling problem: rewards, return and a deadline 17:52

Provenance. Lecture: the five-state recycling example, its rewards, initial and terminal states, the actions left and right, the three-minute rule and why it is needed, the discounted return, the meaning of discounting, and the best strategy and its return for \(\gamma=1\) (slide 8, ≈1072–1436 s). Research extension: the return and its bounded form as in Sutton and Barto. Original construction: the precise reward rule, the bound \(r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)\), the unboundedness argument and the enumeration of three-move plans.

The running case of this chapter is the recording's first example (slide 8). Five cells lie in a row. The agent starts in cell 2 holding a piece of rubbish. Cell 1 holds a garbage can and cell 5 a recycling bin; both are terminal. Cell 3 is empty, and cell 4 holds a chocolate wrapper that the agent can pick up. The actions are \(\mathcal A=\{\leftarrow,\rightarrow\}\), and each moves the agent one cell. The rewards on slide 8 are attached to cells: \(+2\) for the garbage can, \(+1\) for the chocolate wrapper, \(+10\) for the recycling bin, and 0 for cells 2 and 3. This chapter reads the slide as follows: the reward of a move is the reward of the cell it enters, paid on every entry. So \(r(3,\rightarrow)=+1\), \(r(4,\rightarrow)=+10\), \(r(2,\leftarrow)=+2\), and every other move pays 0. The recording confirms the "every entry" reading when it explains why a rule is needed (below). The transitions are deterministic: \(p(s'\mid s,a)=1\) for the neighbouring cell in the chosen direction. Figure 1 draws the problem.

The return, and why it is discounted

The agent's goal is to maximise not the next reward but the rewards of the whole episode. The discounted return from step \(t\) is

\[R_t=\sum_{j\ge0}\gamma^j\,r_{t+j}=r_t+\gamma\,r_{t+1}+\gamma^2r_{t+2}+\cdots,\]

with the sum ending at the terminal step of the episode (slide 8 writes it from \(t=0\) as \(R=r_0+\gamma r_1+\gamma^2r_2+\cdots\)). Sutton and Barto write the return as \(G_t\) and index the reward that follows action \(a_t\) as \(R_{t+1}\) (p. 55); this book writes \(R_t\), because \(G\) is the generator of chapter 4, and indexes that reward \(r_t\), as slide 6 does. The recording motivates the discount by the value of time: a dollar now is worth more than a dollar in ten years, and a robot may lose energy during a long plan (≈1275–1337 s). The mathematics gives a second reason. If every reward satisfies \(\lvert r_t\rvert\le r_{\max}\), then for \(\gamma<1\)

\[\lvert R_t\rvert\le\sum_{j\ge0}\gamma^j r_{\max}=\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma},\]

by the geometric series. In the recycling problem \(r_{\max}=10\), so every return at \(\gamma=0.9\) lies within \(\pm100\), however long the episode. The one-line identity \(R_t=r_t+\gamma R_{t+1}\), which follows from the definition by factoring \(\gamma\) out of every term after the first, is the source of every equation in the next section.

Why the problem needs a deadline at \(\gamma=1\)

Slide 8 asks for the best strategy when \(\gamma=1\), when a reward is worth the same whenever it arrives. Without further rules the question has no answer. The policy that moves \(2\to3\to4\to3\to4\to\cdots\) enters cell 4 once every two moves and collects \(+1\) each time, so its undiscounted return grows by 1 every two moves; no finite number bounds it, and any plan that goes to the bin can be beaten by bouncing longer first. A student gives this reason for the rule the recording adds (≈1246–1275 s): the garbage collector arrives in three minutes and each move takes one minute, so an episode ends after at most three moves. With that rule the eight three-move plans can be enumerated. Moving left first ends the episode at once with return 2. Of the four plans that start \(\rightarrow\) and do not stop early, \(\rightarrow\rightarrow\rightarrow\) earns \(0+1+10=11\), \(\rightarrow\rightarrow\leftarrow\) earns 1, \(\rightarrow\leftarrow\leftarrow\) earns 2 and \(\rightarrow\leftarrow\rightarrow\) earns 0. The best return is 11, the value the recording obtains (≈1399–1436 s; engineering check by enumeration, confirmed by the code below). The rule does its job, but it has a cost that the next section makes precise: once time matters, the cell number alone no longer determines the future.

The Q-table, and the equation it satisfies 23:56

Provenance. Lecture: the Q-table of size states × actions, each entry as a rating of action \(a\) in state \(s\), the backtracking computation for \(\gamma=0.9\) (slides 9–15), the admission that the table ignores the time remaining, the Bellman optimality equation, the greedy policy, and the memory cost of the table (slide 16, ≈1436–2116 s). Research extension: the Bellman equations for \(Q^\pi\) and \(Q^*\) and the greedy-policy statement of Sutton and Barto. Original construction: the check of slide 16, Propositions 1 and 2 and their proofs, the sweep counts, the deadline state \((s,n_{\mathrm{left}})\), Figure 2 and Table 2.

The recording's second question is what the agent should learn (slide 9). It proposes a table with one row per state and one column per action, whose entry rates that action in that state. The definition that makes the rating precise is the action value of a policy \(\pi\),

\[Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E\bigl[R_t\bigm| s_t=s,\ a_t=a,\ \text{later actions drawn from }\pi\bigr],\]

the expected return of taking \(a\) in \(s\) and following \(\pi\) afterwards; the expectation is over the random transitions and the policy's random choices. The state value is \(V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,Q^\pi(s,a)\). The optimal action value \(Q^*(s,a)\) is the least upper bound of the action values that policies achieve, \(\sup_\pi Q^\pi(s,a)\), and \(V^*(s)=\max_aQ^*(s,a)\). In this chapter "policy" means a stationary policy, one whose choice depends only on the current state. Proposition 2 below shows that one policy attains the bound in every state at once, so the supremum is a maximum.

The Bellman optimality equation 30:47

Start from \(R_t=r_t+\gamma R_{t+1}\) and condition on \(s_t=s\), \(a_t=a\). The first term has expectation \(r(s,a)\). The second is \(\gamma\) times the return from the next state, which by the Markov property depends on the past only through \(s_{t+1}=s'\). If the agent follows \(\pi\) from there, taking \(a'\) with probability \(\pi(a'\mid s')\), then

\[Q^\pi(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\sum_{a'}\pi(a'\mid s')\,Q^\pi(s',a').\]

This is the Bellman expectation equation: it averages the next action over the policy. If instead the agent acts optimally from \(s'\) on, the best it can do there is \(\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\), and

\[Q^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a'),\]

the Bellman optimality equation (Sutton and Barto, eq. 3.20, p. 64). In both, the term for a terminal \(s'\) is zero, because the return ends there. For deterministic transitions the sum has one term, and the equation becomes the form on slide 16, \(Q^*(s,a)=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\), with \(r=r(s,a)\) and \(s'\) the next state. The difference between the two equations is the operator applied at the next state: an average over the policy's choices, or a maximum over all choices. The optimality equation is only a heuristic argument so far, because it assumes that an optimal continuation exists; Propositions 1 and 2 remove that assumption.

Table 1 satisfies the deterministic form entry by entry: \(Q^*(4,\rightarrow)=10+0=10\) (the bin is terminal); \(Q^*(4,\leftarrow)=0+0.9\max(8.1,10)=9\); \(Q^*(3,\rightarrow)=1+0.9\max(9,10)=10\); \(Q^*(3,\leftarrow)=0+0.9\max(2,9)=8.1\); \(Q^*(2,\rightarrow)=0+0.9\max(8.1,10)=9\); \(Q^*(2,\leftarrow)=2+0=2\) (engineering check by shown arithmetic). The recording states the intuition: at the optimum, the value of an action is the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the best action available next (≈1905–1965 s). Why does satisfying the equation make the table the optimal table? Two properties of the right-hand side, viewed as a map on tables, answer the question.

Write \(\mathcal T\) for the Bellman optimality operator, which maps any table \(Q\) with an entry for every non-terminal state and every action to the table

\[(\mathcal TQ)(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\max_{a'}Q(s',a'),\]

with the maximum replaced by zero at terminal \(s'\), and measure the distance between two tables \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) by their largest entrywise difference, \(\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty=\max_{s,a}\lvert Q_1(s,a)-Q_2(s,a)\rvert\).

Proposition 1 (the optimality operator is a contraction). For \(0\le\gamma<1\) and any two tables \(Q_1,Q_2\), \(\lVert\mathcal TQ_1-\mathcal TQ_2\rVert_\infty\le\gamma\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty\). Consequently \(\mathcal T\) has exactly one fixed point \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\), and for every starting table \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\) the iterates satisfy \(\lVert\mathcal T^{n_{\mathrm{it}}}Q_{\mathrm{init}}-Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\rVert_\infty\le\gamma^{n_{\mathrm{it}}}\lVert Q_{\mathrm{init}}-Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\rVert_\infty\), where \(\mathcal T^{n_{\mathrm{it}}}\) means \(n_{\mathrm{it}}\) applications of \(\mathcal T\).

Proof. Fix a next state \(s'\) and compare the two rows \(Q_1(s',\cdot)\) and \(Q_2(s',\cdot)\). Evaluating both at an action \(a^\circ\) that maximises the first row gives \(\max_{a'}Q_1(s',a')-\max_{a'}Q_2(s',a')\le Q_1(s',a^\circ)-Q_2(s',a^\circ)\le\max_{a'}\bigl(Q_1(s',a')-Q_2(s',a')\bigr)\). Exchanging the two tables gives \(\bigl\lvert\max_{a'}Q_1(s',a')-\max_{a'}Q_2(s',a')\bigr\rvert\le\max_{a'}\lvert Q_1(s',a')-Q_2(s',a')\rvert\). Hence, for every \((s,a)\),

\[\bigl\lvert(\mathcal TQ_1)(s,a)-(\mathcal TQ_2)(s,a)\bigr\rvert\le\gamma\sum_{s'}p(s'\mid s,a)\,\max_{a'}\lvert Q_1(s',a')-Q_2(s',a')\rvert\le\gamma\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty,\]

because the probabilities sum to at most 1 (terminal next states contribute zero). If \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) were both fixed points, \(\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty=\lVert\mathcal TQ_1-\mathcal TQ_2\rVert_\infty\le\gamma\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty\), which forces \(\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty=0\) since \(\gamma<1\). Existence follows from the Banach fixed-point theorem, because the space of real tables with this norm is complete. For the rate, \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}=\mathcal TQ_{\mathrm{fix}}\), so each application of \(\mathcal T\) to both \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\) multiplies their distance by at most \(\gamma\); \(n_{\mathrm{it}}\) applications multiply it by at most \(\gamma^{n_{\mathrm{it}}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

The same argument, with the average over \(\pi(a'\mid s')\) in place of the maximum, shows that the Bellman expectation operator \(\mathcal T^\pi\), the right-hand side of the expectation equation, is also a \(\gamma\)-contraction; its unique fixed point is \(Q^\pi\), which satisfies that equation. Both operators are also monotone: if \(Q_1\le Q_2\) entry by entry, then \(\mathcal TQ_1\le\mathcal TQ_2\) and \(\mathcal T^\pi Q_1\le\mathcal T^\pi Q_2\), because the rewards are unchanged and maxima and averages of larger numbers are larger.

Proposition 2 (the fixed point is \(Q^*\), and acting greedily on it is optimal). Let \(\gamma<1\), let \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\) be the fixed point of \(\mathcal T\), and let \(\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}\) be a greedy policy for it, \(\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}(s)\in\arg\max_aQ_{\mathrm{fix}}(s,a)\). Then \(Q^\pi\le Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\) entry by entry for every stationary policy \(\pi\), and \(Q^{\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}}=Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\). Hence \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}=Q^*\), the supremum in the definition of \(Q^*\) is attained by \(\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}\) in every state, and \(\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}\) is an optimal policy.

Proof. For any \(\pi\), an average never exceeds a maximum, so \(\mathcal T^\pi Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\le\mathcal TQ_{\mathrm{fix}}=Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\). Applying the monotone operator \(\mathcal T^\pi\) repeatedly gives \((\mathcal T^\pi)^{n_{\mathrm{it}}}Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\le Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\) for every \(n_{\mathrm{it}}\), and the left side converges to \(Q^\pi\) by the contraction property of \(\mathcal T^\pi\); so \(Q^\pi\le Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\). For the greedy policy the average puts all weight on a maximiser, so \(\mathcal T^{\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}}Q_{\mathrm{fix}}=\mathcal TQ_{\mathrm{fix}}=Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\): \(Q_{\mathrm{fix}}\) is a fixed point of \(\mathcal T^{\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}}\), and by uniqueness it equals \(Q^{\pi_{\mathrm{fix}}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Slide 16 defines the policy as \(\pi(s)=\arg\max_aQ^*(s,a)\), the function that tells the agent its best strategy (≈1965–2055 s). Proposition 2 is the statement behind that definition; Sutton and Barto put it as "any policy that is greedy with respect to the optimal evaluation function \(v_*\) is an optimal policy" (p. 64; their \(v_*\) is this book's \(V^*\)), and add that the action-value form lets the agent choose without knowing the environment's dynamics (p. 65). Since Table 1 satisfies the optimality equation, Proposition 1 says it is the only table that does, and Proposition 2 says it is \(Q^*\) of the recycling problem without the deadline.

Proposition 1 also gives an algorithm, value iteration: apply \(\mathcal T\) repeatedly from any table. In the recycling problem, starting from the zero table, the fourth sweep already returns Table 1 exactly, because each sweep propagates the bin's reward one cell further back. Starting from the optimistic table in which every non-terminal entry equals the bound 100, the error after 10 sweeps is 29.2 and after 20 sweeps 7.68, against the guarantees \(0.9^{10}\times98=34.2\) and \(0.9^{20}\times98=11.9\) (engineering check; the initial error is \(100-2=98\)). The slow case is the cycle between cells 3 and 4, which passes an over-estimate back and forth and shrinks it by a factor 0.9 per sweep.

The deadline changes the state

Asked about the three-minute rule, the recording concedes that the table ignores the time remaining (≈1782–1847 s). The concession has a precise form. Under the rule, cell 2 with three moves left and cell 2 with one move left have different futures: from the first the bin is reachable, from the second it is not. The cell number is therefore not a Markov state. The state that is Markov is the pair \((s,n_{\mathrm{left}})\), where \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\in\{0,1,2,3\}\) is the number of moves remaining; each move lowers \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\) by 1, and at \(n_{\mathrm{left}}=0\) the episode ends with the rewards already collected. Because \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\) strictly decreases, the augmented problem has no cycles, and backward induction is exact: compute the values with one move left from those with none, then two from one, then three from two. Replacing the table by a network: deep Q-learning 35:16

Provenance. Lecture: a Q-function computed by a network instead of a table, one-hot state input and one output per action (slide 19), the absence of labels, the Bellman target computed by a second forward pass, the squared loss, holding the target fixed for backpropagation (slides 20–21), the training loop (slide 22), and answers to questions about the discount, look-ahead and learned rewards (≈2116–3209 s). Research extension: semi-gradient methods, the convergence theorem of Watkins and Dayan, the deadly triad, the analysis of Tsitsiklis and Van Roy, the held-fixed targets of Mnih et al. (2013) and the target network of Mnih et al. (2015). Original construction: Proposition 3 and the one-hot linear Q-network of the code.

A Q-network is a network \(Q_\theta\) with parameters \(\theta\) that takes a state and returns one number per action, \(Q_\theta(s,\cdot)\in\mathbb R^{\lvert\mathcal A\rvert}\), where \(\lvert\mathcal A\rvert\) is the number of actions. One forward pass evaluates every action, and the agent acts greedily by taking the largest output. Slide 19 draws the recycling version: the state is encoded as a one-hot vector (cell 2 is \((0,1,0,0,0)\)), two hidden layers of four and three units follow (the slide writes their activations \(a^{[\ell]}\); this book writes \(h^{[\ell]}\)), and two outputs give \(Q_\theta(s,\leftarrow)\) and \(Q_\theta(s,\rightarrow)\). For more actions one adds output units (≈2174–2236 s). The recording presents the network as a universal function approximator that replaces a lookup by a forward pass (≈2116–2171 s).

A target from the Bellman equation

A randomly initialised network outputs arbitrary values, and there is no label to correct them: nobody knows \(Q^*(s,a)\). The recording's answer uses the two things the agent does know, the rewards it observes and the fact that \(Q^*\) satisfies the optimality equation (≈2413–2464 s). After taking action \(a\) in state \(s\), observing \(r\) and arriving in \(s'\), the agent forms the target

\[y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a'),\]

by a second forward pass on \(s'\), and regresses the output for the action actually taken toward it with the squared loss \(\mathcal L=(y-Q_\theta(s,a))^2\) (slide 20). If \(s'\) is terminal, \(y=r\). The target is not correct at the start of training; it is the right-hand side of the optimality equation evaluated with the current estimate, so the network is asked to move one step of value iteration toward a fixed point it does not yet know. The recording calls this "catching up" with a better estimate, and says that as the network improves the targets improve with it (≈2672–2833 s). Estimates that are updated toward targets built from other estimates are said to bootstrap.

Slide 20 marks the term \(\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\) "hold fixed for backprop". The target depends on \(\theta\), but the gradient step treats it as a constant:

\[\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\tfrac12\bigl(y-Q_\theta(s,a)\bigr)^2\Big|_{y\ \text{fixed}}=\theta+\eta\,\bigl(y-Q_\theta(s,a)\bigr)\,\nabla_\theta Q_\theta(s,a),\]

with learning rate \(\eta>0\); the factor \(\tfrac12\), absent from the slide's loss, only rescales \(\eta\). Sutton and Barto call such updates semi-gradient methods: they "take into account the effect of changing the weight vector … on the estimate, but ignore its effect on the target" (p. 202). Differentiating through \(y\) as well would add the term \(-\gamma\,\nabla_\theta\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\) inside the bracket and give a different algorithm, a true gradient method on the squared temporal-difference error \((y-Q_\theta(s,a))^2\) of each sampled transition. Sutton and Barto analyse the prediction version of that alternative, the "naive residual-gradient algorithm", in their Section 11.5 (pp. 269–271) and show on a three-state example that it converges to values that minimise this error but differ from the true values (p. 271). It is not what deep Q-learning does. Mnih et al. (2013) state the same convention for their targets: "the parameters from the previous iteration \(\theta_{i-1}\) are held fixed when optimising the loss function", and they note that, unlike supervised targets, these targets depend on the network weights (p. 3; their subscript counts iterations).

The training loop of slide 22 follows. Initialise the parameters. For each episode, start from the initial state; at each time step, forward-propagate \(s\), take the action with the largest output, observe \(r\) and \(s'\), forward-propagate \(s'\) to form \(y\), and take one gradient step on the loss; stop when a terminal state is reached. Asked how far ahead the target looks, the recording answers one step: the target uses one forward pass on the next state, not a search of the tree, which would cost more (≈2755–2804 s).

When the network is the table

The recycling network of slide 19 has hidden layers, but the simplest network that fits the same interface has none. Let \(x=\mathrm{onehot}(s)\in\{0,1\}^5\) be the one-hot encoding of the state and \(W\in\mathbb R^{2\times5}\) a weight matrix, and set \(Q_\theta(s,\cdot)=Wx\), so \(\theta=W\).

Proposition 3 (a one-hot linear Q-network is a Q-table). For this network, \(Q_\theta(s,a)=W_{a s}\), and one semi-gradient step on the transition \((s,a,r,s')\) changes exactly one entry: \(W_{as}\leftarrow W_{as}+\eta\bigl(y-W_{as}\bigr)\), with \(y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}W_{a's'}\) (and \(y=r\) at a terminal \(s'\)).

Proof. \((Wx)_a=\sum_jW_{aj}x_j=W_{as}\), since \(x\) has a single 1, in position \(s\). The gradient of \(W_{as}\) with respect to \(W\) is the matrix with a 1 in position \((a,s)\) and zeros elsewhere, so the semi-gradient step of the previous display adds \(\eta(y-W_{as})\) to that entry and changes nothing else. \(\blacksquare\)

The update of Proposition 3 is Watkins's Q-learning rule, \(Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\eta\,[\,r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a')-Q(s,a)\,]\) (Sutton and Barto, eq. 6.8, p. 131), where \(y-Q(s,a)\) is the temporal-difference error. For this rule a convergence theorem exists. Watkins and Dayan (1992) prove that with bounded rewards, learning rates in \([0,1)\) whose sum over the updates of each pair \((s,a)\) is infinite while the sum of their squares is finite, the estimates converge to \(Q^*\) "with probability 1" (p. 282). The theorem assumes "a look-up table representation", and the paper notes that Q-learning "may not converge correctly for other representations" (p. 281); the step-size condition also requires every pair to be updated infinitely often. The code below uses the one-hot network with a constant rate \(\eta=0.1\) on the deterministic recycling problem and reaches Table 1 to within \(2.7\times10^{-14}\) (engineering check for this fixture; a constant rate is outside the theorem's hypotheses, and the check covers only this deterministic problem).

When the network is not the table

A network with hidden layers shares its parameters across states: a step that corrects \(Q_\theta(s,a)\) also moves \(Q_\theta\) at other states, including the next state that defines the next target. Nothing in Watkins and Dayan's proof covers that. The recording's statement that estimate and network improve together until they satisfy the optimality equation (≈3054–3079 s) is therefore speaker judgment; no general theorem guarantees it. Sutton and Barto name the combination that creates the danger, the deadly triad: "the danger of instability and divergence arises whenever we combine" function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training (p. 264), where off-policy training means learning about one policy (here the greedy one) from data generated by another. They add that if any two of the three are present without the third, instability can be avoided (p. 264), and credit the thorough analysis to Tsitsiklis and Van Roy (1997) (p. 285). That analysis concerns the simpler problem of estimating the values of a fixed policy with a linear approximator. It proves convergence with probability one when the updated states are sampled along a trajectory of the Markov chain (p. 674), and uses this result to explain earlier counterexamples: convergence is guaranteed when states are sampled according to the chain's steady-state probabilities, while divergence is possible when they are sampled independently of the chain's dynamics (pp. 674–675). The paper also gives an example of divergence with a nonlinear approximator, which the authors call "somewhat contrived" and "a warning rather than a ban" (p. 675). Deep Q-learning with the experience replay of a later section has all three elements of the triad.

Mnih et al. (2015) say the same in their first page: reinforcement learning "is known to be unstable or even to diverge when a nonlinear function approximator such as a neural network is used to represent the action-value" function, and they list three causes: correlations in the sequence of observations, small updates to \(Q\) that change the policy and hence the data, and correlations between the action values and the targets (p. 1). Their second remedy, after experience replay, is a separate target network: every fixed number of updates they copy the parameters into a second set \(\theta^-\) and compute the targets as

\[y=r+\gamma\,\bigl(1-\mathbb 1[s'\ \text{terminal}]\bigr)\max_{a'}Q_{\theta^-}(s',a'),\]

which adds a delay between an update of \(Q\) and its effect on the targets, "making divergence or oscillations much more unlikely" (p. 7); the copy is made every 10,000 updates (Extended Data Table 1, p. 10). The recording does not cover the target network. The two ideas differ in scope: holding \(y\) fixed removes the target from one gradient step, while a target network keeps the targets unchanged across many steps.

Two answers to questions complete the section. The discount is a hyperparameter that shapes the strategy, for example to prefer winning in ten moves over winning in a hundred (speaker judgment, ≈2923–2953 s). And rewards need not be set by whoever designed the game: a separate learned function can produce them, which the recording calls a critic (≈2993–3023 s); the reward-model section builds one.

Terminal states and experience replay 1:01:05

Provenance. Lecture: the preprocessing inserted into the training loop, the terminal flag and the target \(y=r\) at a terminal state (slides 28–30), the correlation of successive screens, the single use of each experience, the replay memory and uniform sampling (slides 31–32), and weighting experiences by the size of their gradients (≈3665–4228 s). Research extension: the absorbing-state convention of Sutton and Barto, experience replay in Lin (1992) and Mnih et al. (2013, 2015), prioritized experience replay (Schaul et al.) and prioritized sweeping (Moore and Atkeson). Original construction: the terminal-state error in the recycling network and the gradient–TD-error identity.

Two corrections come first (slides 28–30). Wherever the loop used \(s\), it now uses \(\mathrm{pre}(s)\). And the loop tracks whether \(s'\) is terminal, because the target differs there: \(y=r\), with no discounted future (≈3752–3815 s). The reason is the convention that the return ends at a terminal state, which Sutton and Barto formalise by an "absorbing state that transitions only to itself and that generates only rewards of zero" (p. 57). A network has no such convention built in. In the one-hot recycling network the output for the bin, \(\max_{a'}W_{a'5}\), is never trained, because no transition starts in the bin; without the terminal flag, every target for the move into the bin would add \(0.9\) times whatever those untrained weights happen to hold.

Why replay experiences

The loop so far trains on each transition once, in the order it happens. The recording names two defects (≈3815–3891 s). Successive transitions are nearly identical, as when the ball crosses the screen for many frames, so consecutive gradient steps pull in the same direction; the recording compares this with training a cat–dog classifier on a minibatch of cats, then one of dogs, and so on. And an experience that is not repeated by chance is never used again. Experience replay stores each transition \((\mathrm{pre}(s),a,r,\mathrm{pre}(s'))\) in a replay memory \(\mathcal B\) (the slides write \(D\)) and trains on minibatches sampled from it uniformly at random (slides 31–32). The agent still acts in the order the game unfolds; only the learning is decoupled from the acting, so a new transition goes into \(\mathcal B\), and the gradient step of the same iteration may or may not sample it (slide 32).

The idea is older than deep Q-learning. Lin (1992) introduced it for connectionist Q-learning: "the learning agent simply remembers its past experiences and repeatedly presents the experiences to its learning algorithm as if the agent experienced again and again what it had experienced before" (p. 299). Mnih et al. list three advantages (2015, p. 7). Each transition can be used in many updates, which improves data efficiency. Random sampling breaks the correlations between consecutive samples, which reduces the variance of the updates. And when learning from its own current behaviour, the agent's training data follow its current greedy action; if that action is "move left", the data are dominated by the left side, and the resulting feedback loop can leave the parameters "stuck in a poor local minimum, or even diverge catastrophically"; replay averages the data over many past behaviours. They add that replay forces off-policy learning, because the stored transitions were generated by older parameters, "which motivates the choice of Q-learning" (p. 7). Their memory held the most recent one million frames (p. 6), and Schaul et al. estimate that DQN revisited each transition eight times on average (p. 1; empirical result as reported by Schaul et al. for the DQN of Mnih et al., not recomputed here).

Which experiences to replay

The recording adds that a refined memory weights experiences by how large their gradients are, so that an insightful experience is replayed more often, and names this "prioritized sweeping" (≈4015–4041 and ≈4174–4228 s). The method that does this for replay is prioritized experience replay: Schaul et al. replay transitions "with high expected learning progress, as measured by the magnitude of their temporal-difference (TD) error", and correct the resulting bias with importance sampling (p. 1). The recording's gradient criterion and theirs agree at the output: for the loss \(\tfrac12(y-Q_\theta(s,a))^2\) the derivative with respect to the output is \(-(y-Q_\theta(s,a))\), whose magnitude is the TD error. Prioritized sweeping is a different method. Moore and Atkeson use all previous experiences "to prioritize important dynamic programming sweeps" (preprint, p. 1), that is, to order planning updates rather than replayed gradient steps, and Schaul et al. describe their own method as similar "but for model-free RL rather than model-based planning" (p. 2). Mnih et al. also mention prioritized sweeping as the inspiration for a possible improvement of uniform replay (p. 7). The recording's examples, a chess beginner who sees few endgames and a blunder worth revisiting, are illustrations (speaker judgment).

Exploration: why a greedy learner gets stuck 1:10:28

Provenance. Lecture: the three-action example of slides 33–36, exploration against exploitation, ε-greedy action selection with probability 5% (slide 37), a question about initialising the values to infinity, the full algorithm (slide 38), how to tell that training has finished, and the Breakout and other Atari results (slides 39–40) (≈4228–4828 s). Research extension: ε-greedy and optimistic initial values in Sutton and Barto; the exploration schedule, evaluation and Breakout result of Mnih et al. (2015). Original construction: Proposition 4, the first-visit probability and its simulation, and the Bellman-residual counterexample.

Slide 33 isolates the failure. From an initial state, three actions lead to three terminal states with rewards \(0\), \(+1\) and \(+1000\); call the actions \(a_1,a_2,a_3\), and write \(Q(a)\) for the value of action \(a\) in the initial state, which slide 33 writes \(Q(S1,a)\). Just after initialisation the network happens to output \(Q(a_1)=0.5\), \(Q(a_2)=0.4\), \(Q(a_3)=0.3\). The greedy agent takes \(a_1\), sees reward 0, and since the next state is terminal its target is \(y=0\). The recording's update sets \(Q(a_1)\) to 0; the agent then takes \(a_2\), sees 1, and sets \(Q(a_2)\) to 1; from then on \(a_2\) is always greedy and its target is always met, so training stops changing anything. The action worth 1000 is never tried (slides 34–36; ≈4285–4372 s).

Proposition 4 (greedy tabular learning never tries \(a_3\)). In the example of slide 33, suppose the three values are stored separately (a table), the agent always takes a greedy action, and each update moves the chosen value toward its target by \(Q(a)\leftarrow Q(a)+\eta\,(r-Q(a))\) with any fixed \(\eta\in(0,1]\). Then \(a_3\) is never taken.

Proof. \(Q(a_3)\) stays at 0.3 as long as \(a_3\) is not taken. While \(a_1\) is greedy, each update moves \(Q(a_1)\) from above 0.4 toward 0, so after finitely many episodes it falls below 0.4 and \(a_2\), still at 0.4, becomes greedy before \(a_3\) can. Each update of \(a_2\) moves \(Q(a_2)\) toward 1 from below, so \(Q(a_2)\ge0.4>0.3\) from then on, and \(a_3\) is never the maximiser. \(\blacksquare\)

Both hypotheses bind. With a shared-parameter network, an update of \(a_1\)'s output also moves \(a_3\)'s, up or down, so the proposition can fail in either direction. And the argument uses nothing about the rewards of unvisited actions, which is the point: the greedy learner is confident about an action it has never tried. The recording's analogy is a cyclist who always takes the same route across campus and gets better at it, never learning whether another route is faster; using what one knows is exploitation, and trying something else to learn is exploration (≈4372–4439 s).

ε-greedy exploration

The remedy on slide 37 is to take, with probability \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}\), a random action instead of the greedy one; the recording uses \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}=5\%\), "one time every 20" (≈4439–4467 s). Sutton and Barto define the rule as selecting, with small probability, "from among all the actions with equal probability, independently of the action-value estimates" (p. 28). The random draw can land on the greedy action, so the probability of a non-greedy action is \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}(1-1/\lvert\mathcal A\rvert)\), which is \(0.05\times\tfrac23=0.033\), about one step in 30, in the three-action example. Until \(a_3\) is first tried it is never greedy, so each episode tries it with probability \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}/3=1/60\), independently of the others, and

\[\mathbb P\bigl(a_3\ \text{tried within}\ n_{\mathrm{ep}}\ \text{episodes}\bigr)=1-\Bigl(1-\frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}}{3}\Bigr)^{n_{\mathrm{ep}}},\]

which is \(1-(59/60)^{100}=0.814\) for \(n_{\mathrm{ep}}=100\); the expected waiting time is 60 episodes. The code below simulates the learner of Proposition 4 with \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}=0.05\) in 4,000 independent runs of 100 episodes and finds \(a_3\) tried in a fraction 0.817 of them (engineering check). With \(\eta=1\), the value the code uses, one trial sets \(Q(a_3)\) to 1000 and \(a_3\) becomes greedy; with a smaller \(\eta\) one trial raises \(Q(a_3)\) to \(0.3+\eta\times999.7\), which is certain to exceed the other two values when \(\eta>0.7/999.7\approx0.0007\), because those values never exceed 1. Mnih et al. used a schedule rather than a constant: \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}\) "annealed linearly from 1.0 to 0.1 over the first million frames, and fixed at 0.1 thereafter" during training, and 0.05 when evaluating the trained agents (p. 6). The recording's 5% matches their evaluation value, not their training value.

A student asks whether initialising every value to infinity would force exploration (≈4485–4508 s). The recording answers that infinite values give no basis for a choice and huge losses, and that random initialisation adapts better to unknown reward structures (speaker judgment, ≈4508–4568 s). The finite version of the student's idea is a known technique. Sutton and Barto call it optimistic initial values: if the initial estimates exceed every achievable reward, then "whichever actions are initially selected, the reward is less than the starting estimates; the learner switches to other actions, being 'disappointed'", so all actions are tried even by a purely greedy learner (p. 34). They regard it as "a simple trick that can be quite effective on stationary problems, but … far from being a generally useful approach to encouraging exploration" (p. 34). Exercise 4 computes which initial values make the learner of Proposition 4 try \(a_3\).

Sparse rewards

In Montezuma's Revenge the player must climb down ladders, swing on a rope, avoid a creature and collect a key before the first reward arrives (≈4828–4923 s). An agent that starts from random values and explores by ε-greedy moves has almost no chance of producing that sequence. As an illustration (not a model of the game), if the first reward required one particular sequence of 20 actions, each chosen uniformly from 8, a random policy would produce it with probability \(8^{-20}=8.7\times10^{-19}\) per attempt (engineering check by shown arithmetic). Until the first reward is seen, every target is \(\gamma\max_{a'}Q_\theta(s',a')\) with no reward term, so the values the learner propagates carry no information about the reward. Mnih et al. list the game among those "demanding more temporally extended planning strategies", which "still constitute a major challenge" for DQN (p. 4). Bellemare et al. (2016) describe the situation: because of the sparse reward, "most published agents achieve an average score close to zero and completely fail to explore most of the 24 rooms that constitute the first level", and escaping the first room already requires climbing ladders, dodging a creature, picking up a key and backtracking to a door (pp. 7, 16).

The recording names this problem "imitation learning" and asks whether the network could start from something better than a random initialisation, such as knowing what a ladder is; people solve the game quickly because of prior knowledge (speaker judgment, ≈4923–4980 s). The problem is exploration under a sparse reward. Imitation learning is one remedy for it. Slide 41 places imitation learning beside RLHF as two ways of bringing human knowledge into training; its screenshot of Montezuma's Revenge comes from Bellemare et al., whose remedy is a different one, an exploration bonus. Imitation learning learns a policy "from example expert behavior, without interaction with the expert or access to reinforcement signal" (Ho and Ermon 2016, p. 1). An exploration bonus adds to the reward a term that is large in rarely visited states: Bellemare et al. derive pseudo-counts of visits from a density model over screens and turn them into bonuses, and their agent "consistently navigates through 15 rooms" of the first level within 50 million frames (p. 7; empirical result for their agent). The recording's other use of human knowledge, preferences, is the subject of the rest of the chapter.

Learning the policy directly

DQN learns values and derives the policy as their argmax. The recording contrasts it with PPO, which learns the policy directly as a probability distribution over actions and therefore handles continuous actions, such as the angle of a steering wheel, which a network with one output per action cannot enumerate (≈5040–5098 s; slide 43). PPO is the algorithm of the language-model section below. Its central quantity is the advantage of an action,

\[\mathrm{Adv}^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s),\]

which the recording describes as how much better an action is than what the agent would otherwise have done (≈5152–5182 s). The description is exact: since \(V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)Q^\pi(s,a)\), the advantage averages to zero under the policy's own choices, \(\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\,\mathrm{Adv}^\pi(s,a)=0\), so a positive advantage marks an action better than the policy's average in that state. Schulman et al. (2017) define PPO by the clipped surrogate objective (p. 3, eq. 7). Let \(\theta_{\mathrm{old}}\) be the parameters that collected the data, let the probability ratio of step \(t\) be \(\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\), and let \(\widehat{\mathrm{Adv}}_t\) be an estimate of the advantage; then PPO maximises the batch average of

\[\min\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\,\widehat{\mathrm{Adv}}_t,\ \ \mathrm{clip}\Bigl(\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)},\,1-c_{\mathrm{clip}},\,1+c_{\mathrm{clip}}\Bigr)\widehat{\mathrm{Adv}}_t\Bigr),\]

where \(\mathrm{clip}\) keeps the ratio when it lies in the interval \([1-c_{\mathrm{clip}},1+c_{\mathrm{clip}}]\) and otherwise replaces it by the nearer end of that interval, and the paper suggests \(c_{\mathrm{clip}}=0.2\). Raising the probability of an action with positive advantage increases the first term without limit, but the clipped term stops rewarding the change once the ratio passes \(1+c_{\mathrm{clip}}\), which "removes the incentive for moving" the ratio outside the interval (p. 3). The minimum keeps the smaller of the two, so the objective is a pessimistic estimate of improvement (p. 1). The predecessor, TRPO, maximises a similar surrogate subject to a hard constraint on the size of the policy change (PPO paper, p. 2). Slide 43 dates TRPO to 2017; the paper appeared at ICML 2015, and 2017 is the date of its fifth arXiv revision (TRPO paper, p. 1). The slide's comparative entries, such as PPO being "generally more stable", summarise practice and are graded speaker judgment; the PPO paper itself reports better results than the methods it compares against on continuous-control tasks and better sample complexity than A2C on Atari (p. 1; empirical result for its benchmarks), and notes that DQN had not been shown to work well on continuous-control benchmarks (p. 1).

What the reward says

Asked how rewards are chosen, the recording answers that Go can be rewarded 1 for a win and 0 otherwise, that chess can add rewards for capturing pieces, and that a reward only at the end, though harder to learn from, may lead to a better strategy, since one can win without capturing (speaker judgment, ≈5101–5151 s). The first case has an exact reading. If the only reward is \(\mathbb 1[\text{win}]\) at the end of the game and \(\gamma=1\), then the return is the indicator of winning, and \(Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb P(\text{win}\mid s_t=s,a_t=a,\pi)\): the agent maximises its probability of winning and is indifferent to the margin. The recording offers this as the reason for the surprising move of AlphaGo shown in the documentary, which put the program in position to win by a small margin (speaker judgment, ≈5347–5426 s); the objective permits that interpretation, but whether it explains that move is not established here. Any reward the designer adds is part of what the agent maximises. Mnih et al. clipped every positive reward to 1 and every negative reward to \(-1\) during training so that one learning rate would serve all games, and note that the agent then "cannot differentiate between rewards of different magnitude" (p. 6). Exercise 3 shows the same effect in the recycling problem: a larger reward for the wrapper makes an endless loop optimal.

Self-play and many agents

The recording shows two simulated humanoids in a sumo ring that learn to push each other out, lowering their centre of gravity and attacking the opponent's legs (≈5204–5266 s). Research extension: the SFT stage and dataset sizes of Ouyang et al. (2022). A language model is trained first to predict the next token of text (slide 49). Chapter 2 wrote this objective as a sum, over the positions \(t\) of a text, of the log-probability of the token \(v_t\) given the tokens before it, for tokens from a vocabulary \(\mathcal V\), and proved that, for each context, the expected cross-entropy of the next token is minimised exactly by the probability with which each token follows that context in the training data (chapter 2, Proposition 9, "what next-token cross-entropy rewards"). In this chapter's language the model is a policy. Write \(\pi_\theta(v\mid x,v_{1:t})\) for the probability it assigns to token \(v\) after a prompt \(x\) and the first \(t\) tokens of a response; the probability of a whole response \(y=v_{1:n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\) is the product of the conditionals, \(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\pi_\theta(v_t\mid x,v_{1:t-1})\).

Slide 50 states two problems with the pretrained model. Text on the web continues sentences ("deep learning is so cool") more often than it answers questions ("what is deep learning?"), and the model has no notion of a good, polite or helpful answer: asked what to do about a laptop that will not turn on, it may reply that laptops sometimes fail because of power issues (≈5539–5628 s). Proposition 9 of chapter 2 turns the first problem into a statement about the objective: a pretrained model that reached the minimum of its loss would continue a question the way questions are continued in its training text. Helpfulness appears in the objective only to the extent that it appears in the data; the claim about how often web text contains helpful answers is speaker judgment.

Supervised fine-tuning 1:34:15

Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) continues training the pretrained model on a dataset of prompts and responses written by people (slide 51). The loss is unchanged; only the data change. For a prompt \(x\) and a human response \(y=v_{1:n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\), the loss is \(-\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=-\sum_{t=1}^{n_{\mathrm{seq}}}\log\pi_\theta(v_t\mid x,v_{1:t-1})\), the next-token cross-entropy of the response given the prompt; this is the book's formalisation of slide 51's description of the loss as the distance between the model's response and the human one. By the same proposition, its minimiser reproduces the conditional distribution of the human responses. SFT is imitation, as the slide says: it learns what demonstrators wrote, including their limits, exactly as the Go classifier of the first section learned the experts' moves.

Ouyang et al. (2022), the InstructGPT paper that slides 41 and 51 cite, describe three datasets built from prompts written by labelers and prompts submitted to their API; "the SFT dataset contains about 13k training prompts" (p. 7). Their Table 6 gives 11,295 labeler-written and 1,430 customer prompts in the SFT training split, 12,725 in total (p. 33; engineering check of the sum). The recording's figure, "only 13,000" pairs (≈5738–5769 s; slide 51), matches the paper's rounded count of prompts. The recording names two shortcomings: demonstrations are expensive to write, and nothing guarantees that 13,000 examples cover the prompts that users will send (speaker judgment, ≈5769–5801 s). The third follows from the objective: an imitator is bounded by its demonstrations. Ranking two answers is easier for a person than writing one, and a ranking can say that one answer is better than another even when neither is good. The next section turns rankings into a training signal.

The second stage trains a reward model \(r_\psi(x,y)\), a network with parameters \(\psi\) that returns a real-valued score for a response \(y\) to a prompt \(x\) (slide 52). From a comparison to a loss

A comparison says that response \(y_w\) was preferred to \(y_l\) for prompt \(x\), written \(y_w\succ y_l\). The Bradley–Terry model assumes that the probability of this outcome depends only on the difference of the two scores,

\[\mathbb P(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\frac{e^{r_\psi(x,y_w)}}{e^{r_\psi(x,y_w)}+e^{r_\psi(x,y_l)}}=\sigma\bigl(r_\psi(x,y_w)-r_\psi(x,y_l)\bigr),\]

where \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid; the second form follows by dividing numerator and denominator by \(e^{r_\psi(x,y_w)}\). Christiano et al. (2017) fit rewards to human comparisons of pairs of short trajectory clips with the first form, noting that it "follows the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry, 1952)" (p. 5), and Rafailov et al. (2023) state the same model for language-model responses (p. 3); the 1952 paper itself is not among this chapter's sources. Maximising the likelihood of the observed comparisons gives the loss that slide 52 draws and the recording does not derive (≈5977–6004 s):

\[\mathcal L(\psi)=-\log\sigma\bigl(r_\psi(x,y_w)-r_\psi(x,y_l)\bigr),\]

averaged over comparisons; Ouyang et al. write it with a factor \(1/\binom{n_{\mathrm{resp}}}{2}\) for the pairs of one prompt (p. 8, eq. 1). Slide 52 cites Christiano et al. as 2023, the date of a later arXiv revision; the paper is from 2017.

The loss is familiar. With the score difference \(u_{\mathrm{pref}}=r_\psi(x,y_w)-r_\psi(x,y_l)\) playing the part of a logit and the label "\(y_w\) preferred" equal to 1, it is the binary cross-entropy of chapter 2, and its derivative is the prediction minus the label: \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_{\mathrm{pref}}=\sigma(u_{\mathrm{pref}})-1\). Treating the two scores as independent coordinates gives \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial r_\psi(x,y_w)=\sigma(u_{\mathrm{pref}})-1\) and \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial r_\psi(x,y_l)=1-\sigma(u_{\mathrm{pref}})\). Gradient descent on those two coordinates would raise the preferred score and lower the other equally. A reward network instead updates their shared parameters, with \(\nabla_\psi\mathcal L=(\sigma(u_{\mathrm{pref}})-1)(\nabla_\psi r_\psi(x,y_w)-\nabla_\psi r_\psi(x,y_l))\); this does not guarantee opposite or equally sized changes in the two scores. The scalar factor \(\sigma(u_{\mathrm{pref}})-1\) tends to zero as the preference probability tends to 1. If the model scores the preferred answer 1.2 and the other 0.4, then \(u_{\mathrm{pref}}=0.8\), the predicted preference probability is \(\sigma(0.8)=0.690\), the loss is \(-\log0.690=0.371\) nats, and the gradient with respect to the preferred score is \(-0.310\) (engineering check; printed by the code below). Ouyang et al., describing the reward model of Stiennon et al. on which theirs builds, identify the reward difference with the log odds of preferring one response to the other (p. 8).

The loss depends on the scores only through their difference. Adding any amount that depends on the prompt alone, \(r_\psi(x,y)+\mathrm{shift}(x)\) for a real function \(\mathrm{shift}\) of the prompt, changes no comparison probability and no loss. The data therefore determine the reward only up to a per-prompt shift, and any use of the reward must not depend on its level. Ouyang et al. act on this: "since the RM loss is invariant to shifts in reward, we normalize the reward model using a bias so that the labeler demonstrations achieve a mean score of 0 before doing RL" (p. 9). The recording's reason for training a model at all is scale: once trained, it scores any number of responses without a person, and comparing two answers takes a person less time than writing one (speaker judgment, ≈6004–6063 s). Christiano et al. report solving their control and Atari tasks with human feedback on "less than 1% of our agent's interactions with the environment" (p. 1; empirical result for their tasks).

The recording calls the reward model a critic (≈6004–6033 s). In the PPO algorithm of the next section a separate network, also called a critic, estimates a state value \(V^\pi(s)\) of the current policy for computing advantages (slide 43 writes it \(V(s)\)); the two are different objects, although Ouyang et al. initialise the value function from the reward model (p. 9).

The third stage lets the SFT model generate, scores its responses with the reward model, and updates it toward higher scores (≈6063–6119 s). Slide 53 maps the problem onto the loop of the loop section. The agent is the language model under fine-tuning. The state at step \(t\) is the prompt with the tokens generated so far, \(s_t=(x,v_{1:t})\), starting from \(s_0=x\). The action is the next token, \(a_t=v_{t+1}\), chosen by the policy \(\pi_\theta\). The transition is deterministic: it appends the token. An episode is the generation of one complete response. The reward is the reward model's score of the finished response, given once at the end; every earlier reward is zero, which makes the task a sparse-reward problem, like chess scored only at the end (≈6149–6260 s). With \(\gamma=1\), the return of an episode is \(r_\psi(x,y)\), and the objective is \(\mathbb E_{x}\,\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\,r_\psi(x,y)\).

The objective of InstructGPT

Ouyang et al. describe the same stage differently. Their environment "is a bandit environment which presents a random customer prompt and expects a response to the prompt. Given the prompt and response, it produces a reward determined by the reward model and ends the episode"; in addition, "we add a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model at each token to mitigate over-optimization of the reward model" (p. 9). Their objective (p. 9, eq. 2), in this book's letters, is

\[\mathbb E_{x}\,\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Bigl[r_\psi(x,y)-\lambda\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr]\]

plus, for the variant they call PPO-ptx, a term that keeps the log-likelihood of pretraining text high, weighted by a coefficient that the paper writes \(\gamma\) and that is unrelated to the discount (for the "PPO" models it is zero). Here \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is the reference policy, the SFT model, and \(\lambda\ge0\) is the KL coefficient that the paper writes \(\beta\). They optimise it with PPO, the algorithm of the previous section.

The bandit description and the token-level description of slide 53 are the same problem once the penalty is split over tokens. Since \(\pi(y\mid x)\) is a product of per-token probabilities, its logarithm is a sum:

\[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}=\sum_{t=0}^{n_{\mathrm{seq}}-1}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(v_{t+1}\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(v_{t+1}\mid s_t)}.\]

So give the token-level agent the reward \(-\lambda\log\bigl(\pi_\theta(v_{t+1}\mid s_t)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(v_{t+1}\mid s_t)\bigr)\) at every step and \(r_\psi(x,y)\) in addition at the last; with \(\gamma=1\) its return is exactly the bracket above. The intermediate rewards are then not zero. Slide 53's note that all intermediate rewards are typically zero describes the reward-model term alone, and omits the penalty that Ouyang et al. apply at every token. Under \(\pi_\theta\), the expected value of the summed log-ratios is the Kullback–Leibler divergence \(\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)=\sum_y\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\log\bigl(\pi_\theta(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)\), in nats, so the objective is expected reward minus \(\lambda\) times a divergence from the SFT model.

Why penalise the divergence? The reward model was trained on responses sampled from the SFT model, so its scores are constrained by data only near that distribution. A policy free to move anywhere can find responses that the reward model scores highly for reasons no labeler would endorse, the same reasoning by which chapter 4's forged inputs exploited a classifier where no data constrained it; this is the "over-optimization" the quotation names. The penalty prices every departure from the reference model. What does the penalised objective select?

Proposition 5 (optimum of the KL-regularised objective). Fix a prompt \(x\), a finite set of responses, a reward \(r(x,y)\), a reference distribution \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\) that is positive on every response, and \(\lambda>0\). Among all distributions \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) on the responses, \(\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)\,r(x,y)-\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\bigr)\) is maximised by exactly one distribution,

\[\pi^*(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\lambda\bigr),\qquad Z(x)=\sum_y\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\bigl(r(x,y)/\lambda\bigr),\]

and the maximum value is \(\lambda\log Z(x)\).

Proof. By the definition of \(\pi^*\), \(r(x,y)=\lambda\log\bigl(\pi^*(y\mid x)/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)\bigr)+\lambda\log Z(x)\) for every \(y\). Substitute this into the objective and combine the two logarithms; the ratio to \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) cancels:

\[\sum_y\pi(y\mid x)\Bigl[\lambda\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}+\lambda\log Z(x)-\lambda\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y\mid x)}\Bigr]=\lambda\log Z(x)-\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\Vert\pi^*(\cdot\mid x)\bigr).\]

The divergence is nonnegative and zero only when \(\pi=\pi^*\); the proof of chapter 2's Proposition 9 ("what next-token cross-entropy rewards") establishes exactly this for two distributions on a finite set, and \(\pi^*\) is positive on every response because \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is. \(\blacksquare\)

Rafailov et al. state the same optimum (p. 4, eq. 4). The formula has two limits that explain the role of \(\lambda\). As \(\lambda\to\infty\) the factor \(\exp(r/\lambda)\to1\) and \(\pi^*\to\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\): the SFT model is kept. As \(\lambda\to0\) the factor concentrates all probability on the responses of highest reward: the reward model is trusted completely. A worked case shows the range between. Take three responses with reference probabilities \((0.5,0.3,0.2)\) and rewards \((0,1,2)\). The recording mentions DPO as an algorithm more popular than PPO, from Stanford "a year ago", and does not study it (speaker judgment, ≈5010–5040 s). import math
import numpy as np

GAMMA = 0.9
ENTER = {1: 2.0, 2: 0.0, 3: 0.0, 4: 1.0, 5: 10.0}  # reward on entering
TERMINAL = {1, 5}
LEFT, RIGHT = 0, 1


def step(s, a, r_choc=1.0):
    """Recycling MDP: deterministic next state and reward of (s, a)."""
    s_next = s - 1 if a == LEFT else s + 1
    return s_next, (r_choc if s_next == 4 else ENTER[s_next])


def bellman(Q, gamma, r_choc=1.0):
    """Bellman optimality operator on the 5 x 2 table; terminal rows 0."""
    new = np.zeros_like(Q)
    for s in (2, 3, 4):
        for a in (LEFT, RIGHT):
            s_next, r = step(s, a, r_choc)
            future = 0.0 if s_next in TERMINAL else Q[s_next - 1].max()
            new[s - 1, a] = r + gamma * future
    return new


def deadline_q(gamma):
    """Backward induction on the Markov state (position, moves left)."""
    V = {(s, 0): 0.0 for s in range(1, 6)}
    Q = {}
    for n_left in (1, 2, 3):
        for s in range(1, 6):
            V[(s, n_left)] = 0.0
            if s in TERMINAL:
                continue
            for a in (LEFT, RIGHT):
                s_next, r = step(s, a)
                Q[(s, n_left, a)] = r + gamma * V[(s_next, n_left - 1)]
            V[(s, n_left)] = max(Q[(s, n_left, LEFT)], Q[(s, n_left, RIGHT)])
    return Q, V

What the models capture and what they miss

Provenance. Original construction, with statements from Sutton and Barto, Watkins and Dayan, and Ouyang et al. already cited above.

Each result of this chapter holds under an idealisation, and each idealisation fails in practice in a specific way.

The Markov state. Propositions 1 and 2 assume a state that makes the future independent of the past. The recycling deadline and the single Breakout frame are two ways that assumption fails, and in both the repair was to enlarge the state. Under partial observation, as in StarCraft, no finite stack of observations need be Markov; the equations then describe an approximation.

Tables and networks. The contraction and the Watkins–Dayan theorem are statements about tables: exact updates for Propositions 1 and 2, and look-up tables updated with decreasing step sizes and visited infinitely often for Q-learning. Deep Q-learning combines function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy training, the deadly triad, and has no general convergence guarantee; its success on Atari is an empirical result for the games, architecture and hyperparameters of Mnih et al.

Exploration. ε-greedy exploration is undirected: it spends random actions equally on well-known and unknown actions, and the probability of reaching a distant reward by chance falls geometrically with the length of the required sequence. The Montezuma's Revenge results show where that matters, and the exploration-bonus result is one method on one benchmark.

Rewards. An agent maximises the reward it is given. The wrapper threshold of exercise 3, the reward clipping of Mnih et al. and the win-probability reading of a terminal reward all show that the designer's choice of reward, not the designer's intention, defines optimal behaviour.

Preferences. The reward model assumes that one scalar score per response explains the comparisons through the Bradley–Terry form. Exercise 5 shows that a cycle of preferences cannot be fitted, and the model says nothing about disagreement between labelers, whose comparisons it averages. The score is identified only up to a per-prompt shift. The KL penalty limits how far the policy exploits the reward model's errors; it does not remove them, and Proposition 5 describes the optimum of the objective, not what a finite run of PPO reaches.

Claims not checked. The Seaquest anecdote, the claims that advertising is the largest application of RL and that DPO is more popular than PPO, the interpretation of AlphaGo's surprising move, and the closing interview are speaker judgments recorded with their times.

What you can now do. You can state a problem as a Markov decision process and test whether a proposed state is Markov. You can define the discounted return, bound it, and explain why an undiscounted problem needs a horizon. You can derive the Bellman expectation and optimality equations, prove that the optimality operator is a contraction, and show that acting greedily on its fixed point is optimal. You can compute a Q-table by value iteration or, on an acyclic state, by backward induction. You can derive the Q-learning target and its semi-gradient update, show that a one-hot linear Q-network is a table, and name the three ingredients that remove the guarantee. You can explain frame stacking, terminal targets, experience replay, target networks and ε-greedy exploration by the failure each one addresses. You can derive the Bradley–Terry reward-model loss and its gradient, decompose the RLHF objective into per-token rewards, and derive the KL-regularised optimum and the DPO loss from it.

1. The value of wandering

In the recycling problem without the deadline and with \(\gamma=0.9\), let \(\pi\) choose \(\leftarrow\) and \(\rightarrow\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each in every non-terminal cell. (a) Write the Bellman expectation equations for \(V^\pi(2),V^\pi(3),V^\pi(4)\) and solve them. (b) Compute \(Q^\pi(s,a)\) for every non-terminal pair and the policy that is greedy with respect to \(Q^\pi\). (c) Compare with Table 1 and explain what one step of "evaluate, then act greedily" achieved here.

Solution

(a) Write \(V(s)\) for \(V^\pi(s)\) throughout. From the expectation equation with \(V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)Q^\pi(s,a)\) and the terminal values 0: \(V(2)=\tfrac12\cdot2+\tfrac12(0+0.9V(3))\), \(V(3)=\tfrac12(0+0.9V(2))+\tfrac12(1+0.9V(4))\), \(V(4)=\tfrac12(0+0.9V(3))+\tfrac12\cdot10\). That is, \(V(2)=1+0.45V(3)\), \(V(3)=0.5+0.45V(2)+0.45V(4)\), \(V(4)=5+0.45V(3)\). Substituting the first and third into the second gives \(V(3)=0.5+0.45(1+0.45V(3))+0.45(5+0.45V(3))=3.2+0.405V(3)\), so \(V(3)=3.2/0.595=5.378\), \(V(2)=1+0.45\times5.378=3.420\) and \(V(4)=5+0.45\times5.378=7.420\). (b) \(Q^\pi(2,\leftarrow)=2\), \(Q^\pi(2,\rightarrow)=0.9\times5.378=4.840\); \(Q^\pi(3,\leftarrow)=0.9\times3.420=3.078\), \(Q^\pi(3,\rightarrow)=1+0.9\times7.420=7.678\); \(Q^\pi(4,\leftarrow)=0.9\times5.378=4.840\), \(Q^\pi(4,\rightarrow)=10\). The greedy policy moves right in all three cells. (c) Every value of the random policy is below Table 1 (3.42 against 9 at the start), as Proposition 2 requires, yet the greedy policy with respect to its values is already the optimal policy of Table 1. One step of evaluation followed by greedy improvement reached the optimum here because the right move is better than the left move in every cell even under random continuation; in general the step improves the policy without reaching the optimum, and the step is repeated.

2. No solution at \(\gamma=1\)

(a) Show that, without the deadline and with \(\gamma=1\), the Bellman optimality equation of the recycling problem has no solution in real numbers. (b) Which hypothesis of Proposition 1 fails? (c) Show that the deadline state \((s,n_{\mathrm{left}})\) restores a unique solution, and compute its value at the start.

Solution

(a) Suppose a finite table \(Q\) solved it, and let \(V(s)=\max_aQ(s,a)\). The equation for \((4,\leftarrow)\) gives \(Q(4,\leftarrow)=0+V(3)\), so \(V(4)\ge V(3)\). The equation for \((3,\rightarrow)\) gives \(Q(3,\rightarrow)=1+V(4)\), so \(V(3)\ge1+V(4)\ge1+V(3)\), which is impossible. The contradiction is the bounce loop: every round trip adds 1. (b) Proposition 1 requires \(\gamma<1\). At \(\gamma=1\) the bound reads \(\lVert\mathcal TQ_1-\mathcal TQ_2\rVert_\infty\le\lVert Q_1-Q_2\rVert_\infty\), which forces neither uniqueness nor existence; the code shows value iteration growing past 100. (c) With \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\) in the state, every move lowers \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\) by one and states with \(n_{\mathrm{left}}=0\) have value 0, so the values with a given number of moves left are determined uniquely by those with one move fewer; induction on \(n_{\mathrm{left}}\) gives a unique solution, with no condition on \(\gamma\). At \(\gamma=1\): \(V(4,1)=\max(0,10)=10\), \(V(2,1)=\max(2,0)=2\), \(V(3,2)=\max(0+V(2,1),1+V(4,1))=11\), \(V(2,3)=\max(2,0+V(3,2))=11\), the return of 11 found by enumeration.

3. How much chocolate makes the agent loop?

Keep \(\gamma=0.9\) and no deadline, but let entering cell 4 pay \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}>0\) instead of 1. (a) Compute the discounted return from cell 3 of the policy that loops \(3\to4\to3\to\cdots\) forever. (b) Find the value \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}\) above which the optimal action in cell 4 is \(\leftarrow\), for general \(\gamma\in(0,1)\), and evaluate it at \(\gamma=0.9\). (c) What happens to the threshold as \(\gamma\to1\), and what does the result say about designing rewards?

Solution

(a) The loop earns \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}\) on every entry into cell 4, at steps 0, 2, 4, …, so its return from cell 3 is \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}(1+\gamma^2+\gamma^4+\cdots)=r_{\mathrm{choc}}/(1-\gamma^2)\). (b) In cell 4 the agent compares \(Q(4,\rightarrow)=10\) with \(Q(4,\leftarrow)=\gamma V(3)\). If looping is optimal, \(V(3)=r_{\mathrm{choc}}/(1-\gamma^2)\) and \(Q(4,\leftarrow)=\gamma r_{\mathrm{choc}}/(1-\gamma^2)\); if going to the bin is optimal, \(V(3)=r_{\mathrm{choc}}+10\gamma\) and \(Q(4,\leftarrow)=\gamma r_{\mathrm{choc}}+10\gamma^2\), which is below 10 exactly when the looping value is below 10 as well. Either way the switch happens where \(\gamma r_{\mathrm{choc}}/(1-\gamma^2)=10\), that is at \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}=10(1-\gamma^2)/\gamma\). At \(\gamma=0.9\) this is \(10\times0.19/0.9=2.111\); the code confirms that value iteration gives \(\rightarrow\) in cell 4 at 2.101 and \(\leftarrow\) at 2.121. In cell 3 the comparison of looping with going to the bin, \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}/(1-\gamma^2)\) against \(r_{\mathrm{choc}}+10\gamma\), gives the same threshold. (c) As \(\gamma\to1\) the threshold \(10(1-\gamma^2)/\gamma\to0\): any positive reward for the wrapper eventually makes looping optimal, which is the unbounded return of the undiscounted problem reappearing. A reward intended as a small bonus for tidying up changes the optimal behaviour once it is large enough relative to the discount, and the agent then pursues the bonus instead of the goal. Rewards must be checked for the behaviour they make optimal, not for the behaviour they were meant to encourage.

4. Forcing exploration without randomness

Return to the three-action example of slide 33 and the update of Proposition 4 with \(\eta=1\). (a) With ε-greedy exploration at \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{expl}}=0.05\), how many episodes are needed for probability at least 0.95 that \(a_3\) has been tried? (b) Suppose instead the agent is purely greedy, breaks ties toward the lowest index, and all three values start at a common value \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\). For which \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\) does it try \(a_3\)? (c) Why does the answer to (b) not carry over to a Q-network with shared parameters?

Solution

(a) The probability that \(a_3\) has not been tried after \(n_{\mathrm{ep}}\) episodes is \((59/60)^{n_{\mathrm{ep}}}\). Requiring this to be at most 0.05 gives \(n_{\mathrm{ep}}\ge\ln0.05/\ln(59/60)=178.2\), so 179 episodes. (b) The first episode takes \(a_1\) (tie, lowest index) and sets \(Q(a_1)=0\). If \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\le0\), then \(a_1\) is greedy again by the tie rule and is repeated forever, so \(a_3\) is never tried. Otherwise the second episode compares \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\) for \(a_2\) and \(a_3\), takes \(a_2\) by the tie rule and sets \(Q(a_2)=1\). The third compares \(Q(a_2)=1\) with \(Q(a_3)=Q_{\mathrm{init}}\): the agent takes \(a_3\) if and only if \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}>1\), and then sets \(Q(a_3)=1000\) and keeps it. So every \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}>1\) works and every \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\le1\) fails; the requirement is that the initial value exceed the reward the agent has already found, which is the optimistic-initial-values idea of Sutton and Barto (p. 34). (c) The argument uses the fact that updating \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) leaves \(Q(a_3)\) at \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\). In a network, the gradient steps for \(a_1\) and \(a_2\) change the shared parameters and therefore the output for \(a_3\); after the first two updates it can be far below \(Q_{\mathrm{init}}\), and optimism set at initialisation is not preserved. Directed methods such as the exploration bonuses of Bellemare et al. add the optimism to the reward instead, where it is recomputed from visit counts.

5. What a reward model can and cannot represent

(a) Show that the Bradley–Terry loss is unchanged when \(r_\psi(x,y)\) is replaced by \(r_\psi(x,y)+\mathrm{shift}(x)\) for any real function \(\mathrm{shift}\) of the prompt, and that the KL-regularised optimum of Proposition 5 is unchanged as well. (b) Three responses \(y_1,y_2,y_3\) to one prompt are compared once in each pair, with outcomes \(y_1\succ y_2\), \(y_2\succ y_3\) and \(y_3\succ y_1\). Find the minimum of the total Bradley–Terry loss over all scores and the preference probabilities the fitted model predicts.

Solution

(a) The loss depends on the scores only through \(r_\psi(x,y_w)-r_\psi(x,y_l)\), in which \(\mathrm{shift}(x)\) cancels. In Proposition 5, replacing \(r\) by \(r+\mathrm{shift}(x)\) multiplies every term \(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\exp(r/\lambda)\) by \(\exp(\mathrm{shift}(x)/\lambda)\), and \(Z(x)\) by the same factor, so \(\pi^*\) is unchanged; only the optimal value \(\lambda\log Z(x)\) shifts by \(\mathrm{shift}(x)\). This is why Ouyang et al. may set the reward's level by a bias without changing the fine-tuned policy's target. (b) Write the three score differences, each the logit of one comparison, as \(u_1=r_\psi(x,y_1)-r_\psi(x,y_2)\), \(u_2=r_\psi(x,y_2)-r_\psi(x,y_3)\) and \(u_3=r_\psi(x,y_3)-r_\psi(x,y_1)\); they sum to 0 for every choice of scores. The total loss is \(\sum_{j=1}^3\log(1+e^{-u_j})\), since \(-\log\sigma(u)=\log(1+e^{-u})\). The function \(u\mapsto\log(1+e^{-u})\) has second derivative \(\sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))>0\), so it is convex. By Jensen's inequality \(\tfrac13\sum_j\log(1+e^{-u_j})\ge\log\bigl(1+e^{-(u_1+u_2+u_3)/3}\bigr)=\log2\), with equality when all \(u_j=0\). The minimum total loss is \(3\log2=2.079\) nats, attained by equal scores, and the fitted model predicts probability \(\tfrac12\) for every comparison, including the three it was shown. A single scalar score orders the responses, and a cycle of preferences has no order; the Bradley–Terry model cannot represent it and reports indifference instead. Real preference data can contain such cycles, for example when labelers disagree or judge by different criteria.

6. Choosing the KL coefficient

Use the worked case of the RLHF section: reference probabilities \((0.5,0.3,0.2)\) and reward-model scores \((0,1,2)\). Suppose the reward model is wrong about the third response, and labelers asked afresh would score the three responses \((0,1,-1)\). (a) For \(\lambda=0.25\), 1 and 4, compute the expected true score of the optimal policy of Proposition 5 and compare it with the reference policy. (b) What does the calculation say about choosing \(\lambda\), and what evidence would you collect before choosing it for a real model?

Solution

(a) The optimal policies are those of the table in the RLHF section. Their expected true scores are: \(\lambda=0.25\): \(0.0008\times0+0.0267\times1+0.9725\times(-1)=-0.946\); \(\lambda=1\): \(0.2919-0.5291=-0.237\); \(\lambda=4\): \(0.3171-0.2714=0.046\). The reference policy scores \(0.3-0.2=0.1\). Every fine-tuned policy is worse than the reference by the true score, and the damage grows as \(\lambda\) falls, from 0.054 below the reference at \(\lambda=4\) to 1.046 below it at \(\lambda=0.25\). (b) A small \(\lambda\) trusts the reward model and moves the policy furthest from the data the reward model was trained on, which is where its errors are least constrained. A large \(\lambda\) limits the damage of an error and also limits the gain when the reward model is right (with the original scores, the expected score is 1.972 at \(\lambda=0.25\) and 0.860 at \(\lambda=4\)). The choice cannot be made from the reward model's own scores, because they rise monotonically as \(\lambda\) falls whether or not they are correct. The evidence needed is fresh human comparisons of samples from the fine-tuned policy against the reference, at several values of \(\lambda\), together with the measured divergence from the reference; the value to choose is the one at which the human preference rate stops improving. This is the book's design reasoning, not a procedure reported by Ouyang et al.
